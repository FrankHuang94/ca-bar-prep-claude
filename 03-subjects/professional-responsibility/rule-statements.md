# Professional Responsibility — rule statements

**How to use this file.** Each block gives a rule you can write verbatim, then elements, exceptions, authority, the **California vs. ABA** comparison and a short example. Professional Responsibility is not on the MBE; the "MBE vs. CA essay" line therefore tells you what the **MPRE** (ABA rules) expects and, more importantly, how California differs. Every recent PR call says "Answer according to California and ABA authorities", so for each duty write the ABA rule, then the California rule, then say whether the result differs.

**Abbreviations.**
- **CRPC / rule x.x** = California Rules of Professional Conduct (effective Nov. 1, 2018, as amended).
- **MR x.x** = ABA Model Rules of Professional Conduct.
- **B&P** = California Business and Professions Code (the State Bar Act is B&P § 6000 et seq.).
- **Evid. Code / CCP** = California Evidence Code / Code of Civil Procedure.
- **Informed written consent** (CA) = the lawyer's disclosure of the relevant circumstances and material risks *and* the client's consent are both in writing (rule 1.0.1(e), (e-1)) [S-556]. **Informed consent, confirmed in writing** (ABA) = oral consent is enough if the lawyer promptly confirms it in a writing (MR 1.0(b), (e)).

## Sources of law and the attorney-client relationship

### R-PRF-001 — Sources of law; how to answer a "California and ABA" call
**Rule (write this):** A California lawyer is governed by the California Rules of Professional Conduct, the State Bar Act (Bus. & Prof. Code § 6000 et seq.) and California case law; the ABA Model Rules are not binding in California but are persuasive guidance where California has no direct rule. On a call that asks for California and ABA authorities, state each rule, apply it, and conclude separately where the two diverge.

**Elements:** 1. Identify each lawyer (actor by actor). 2. For each duty: ABA rule → California rule → application → conclusion. 3. Note civil consequences (disqualification, malpractice, fee forfeiture) only if the call asks.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A violation of a rule is a ground for discipline; it does not by itself create a civil cause of action, though the rules may be evidence of the standard of care (CRPC rule 1.0 and comments; MR Preamble & Scope [20]).

**Authority:** CRPC rule 1.0; B&P §§ 6068, 6076–6077 (rules binding once approved by the Supreme Court) [S-082]; MR Preamble.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE; the MPRE tests the ABA Model Rules only. **CA vs. ABA:** frame — the California rules control discipline of a California lawyer; ABA is the comparison column.

**Example:** "What ethical violations has Lou committed? Answer according to California and ABA authorities." Head the answer *Lou*, then *Duty of loyalty*, *Duty of confidentiality*, etc.; under each, "Under the ABA Model Rules … California's rule is the same/differs because …".

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: like reading 律师法 together with 律师执业行为规范 (statute plus bar-association norms), but here the court-approved rules, not the bar association, carry disciplinary force (approx.).

### R-PRF-002 — Formation of the attorney-client relationship
**Rule (write this):** An attorney-client relationship is formed when a person seeks legal advice or services from a lawyer and the lawyer agrees, expressly or impliedly, to provide them; it may be implied from conduct when the person reasonably believes the lawyer is acting for him. No written agreement or fee is required for the relationship (as opposed to the fee agreement) to exist.

**Elements:** 1. Person seeks advice/assistance within the lawyer's professional competence. 2. Lawyer expressly agrees, or fails to disclaim when he knows or should know the person reasonably relies on him. 3. Identify **who** the client is (the organization, an insured, a fiduciary, a family member who pays).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Duties to a **prospective client** arise even if no relationship follows (R-PRF-003). Once formed, duties of loyalty and confidentiality attach immediately; written-fee-agreement rules are separate (R-PRF-042).

**Authority:** Restatement (Third) of the Law Governing Lawyers § 14; Evid. Code § 951 (client includes a person who consults a lawyer to retain him or obtain advice); CRPC rule 1.18; MR 1.18.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** same.

**Example:** A nonprofit's sole employee asks the nonprofit's lawyer to "look over" her own employment contract. The lawyer must clarify that the nonprofit is her client; otherwise the employee may reasonably believe she is a client too (cf. Feb. 2020 Q2).

### R-PRF-003 — Duties to prospective clients
**Rule (write this):** A person who consults a lawyer about retaining the lawyer is a prospective client. Even if no representation follows, the lawyer may not use or reveal information learned in the consultation, and may not represent a client with materially adverse interests in the same or a substantially related matter if the lawyer received information from the prospective client that is material to the matter (California) or could be significantly harmful (ABA), unless both clients give informed written consent or the lawyer is timely screened.

**Elements:** 1. Prospective client status. 2. Confidentiality of what was learned (as for a former client). 3. Disqualification only if the information is *material* (CA) / *significantly harmful* (ABA). 4. Firm avoids imputation by (a) informed written consent of both or (b) the consulting lawyer limited intake, is timely screened, gets no part of the fee, and written notice goes to the prospective client.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A person who communicates unilaterally to disqualify a lawyer, without a reasonable expectation that the lawyer will consider representing him, is not a prospective client (MR 1.18 cmt. [2]).

**Authority:** CRPC rule 1.18(a)–(d) [S-556]; MR 1.18(a)–(d); State Bar Formal Op. 2021-205 [VERIFY V-952].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE (MPRE: "significantly harmful"). **CA vs. ABA:** differs — CA disqualifies on information *material to the matter* (a lower threshold); ABA requires information that *could be significantly harmful*. CA consent must be informed written consent; ABA "confirmed in writing".

**Example:** W consults Firm about suing H and discloses her hidden assets; Firm declines. H later asks Firm to defend. The asset information is material, so the consulting lawyer is barred; Firm may proceed only with both consents or a timely screen plus written notice to W.

### R-PRF-004 — State Bar Act duties of attorneys (B&P § 6068)
**Rule (write this):** Under Business and Professions Code § 6068 every California attorney must, among other duties, support the federal and state constitutions and laws, maintain respect for the courts, counsel or maintain only actions that appear legal or just (except the defense of a person charged with a public offense), employ only means consistent with truth and never mislead a judge, maintain inviolate the client's confidences, respond to reasonable status inquiries, cooperate with State Bar investigations, and self-report specified events within 30 days.

**Elements:** (a) support constitutions and laws; (b) respect courts; (c) only legal or just actions; (d) truth, no artifice; (e) confidentiality; (f) advance no fact prejudicial to the honor or reputation of a party or witness unless the justice of the cause requires it [S-1252] [VERIFY V-950]; (g) no corrupt motive in encouraging litigation; (h) never reject the cause of the defenseless or oppressed for personal reasons; (i) cooperate in discipline; (m) communication; (o) self-reporting.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Willful violation of § 6068 is itself a ground for discipline (B&P § 6103 covers willful disobedience of court orders and violation of the attorney's oath or duties).

**Authority:** B&P §§ 6068(a)–(i), (m), (o), 6103 [S-552] [S-1250].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE; no ABA equivalent statute. **CA vs. ABA:** California-only source; cite it alongside the matching CRPC rule (e.g., § 6068(e) with rule 1.6; § 6068(m) with rule 1.4; § 6068(d) with rule 3.3).

**Example:** A lawyer who tells a judge a hearing was "properly noticed" knowing it was not violates rule 3.3(a)(1) **and** § 6068(d).

## Scope of representation and allocation of authority

### R-PRF-005 — Allocation of authority between client and lawyer
**Rule (write this):** The client decides the objectives of the representation and, after consultation, whether to settle a civil matter and, in a criminal case, what plea to enter, whether to waive a jury trial and whether to testify; the lawyer, after reasonable consultation, controls the means, including tactical and procedural decisions, and may take action impliedly authorized to carry out the representation.

**Elements:** 1. Client-reserved decisions: objectives; settlement; plea; jury waiver; testifying (and, by case law, whether to appeal). 2. Lawyer's sphere: means, strategy, tactics, witnesses, motions — after consultation (R-PRF-010). 3. Settling without client authority is a violation and may not bind the client.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A client may expressly authorize the lawyer in advance to settle within stated limits. A lawyer who appears or settles without authority is subject to discipline (B&P § 6104).

**Authority:** CRPC rule 1.2(a); MR 1.2(a); B&P § 6104; *Blanton v. Womancare, Inc.* (1985) 38 Cal.3d 396 (attorney lacks implied authority to stipulate to arbitration impairing the client's substantial rights).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** same in substance.

**Example:** Lawyer August's co-counsel accepts a $200,000 settlement before the client is told of the offer. The decision to settle belongs to the client; both rule 1.2(a) and the duty to communicate offers (R-PRF-011) are violated (cf. July 2024 Q3).

### R-PRF-006 — Limited-scope representation
**Rule (write this):** A lawyer may limit the scope of the representation if the limitation is reasonable under the circumstances, is not otherwise prohibited by law, and the client gives informed consent.

**Elements:** 1. Reasonable limitation. 2. Not prohibited by law. 3. Informed consent (ABA: informed consent; CA rule says informed consent; California's limited-scope forms and fee-agreement rules make a writing advisable).

**Exceptions / defenses:** A limitation cannot reduce the representation below competence for what is undertaken (MR 1.2 cmt. [7]).

**Authority:** CRPC rule 1.2(b); MR 1.2(c); Cal. Rules of Court, rules 3.35–3.37, 5.425 (limited-scope appearances).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** same standard (CA paragraph (b) vs. ABA (c)).

**Example:** A lawyer agrees to draft only a response to a demand letter for a fixed fee. That is a reasonable limitation if explained; it does not authorize ignoring an obvious statute-of-limitations problem without mentioning it.

### R-PRF-007 — Counseling or assisting a crime, fraud or violation of law
**Rule (write this):** A lawyer may not counsel a client to engage, or assist a client in conduct, that the lawyer knows is criminal or fraudulent (ABA), or criminal, fraudulent or a violation of any law, rule or ruling of a tribunal (California); the lawyer may discuss the legal consequences of any proposed course of conduct and help the client make a good-faith effort to determine the validity, scope, meaning or application of the law.

**Elements:** 1. Knowledge (actual, inferable from circumstances). 2. Counseling or assisting (drafting, structuring, concealing). 3. Prohibited conduct. 4. Safe harbor: explaining consequences; good-faith test of a law.

**Exceptions / defenses:** If the client persists, the lawyer must advise of the limits on the lawyer's conduct (rule 1.4(a)(4); MR 1.4(a)(5)) and may or must withdraw (R-PRF-050, R-PRF-051).

**Authority:** CRPC rule 1.2.1 [S-556]; MR 1.2(d).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** differs — CA adds "a violation of any law, rule, or ruling of a tribunal", so it is broader than the ABA's "criminal or fraudulent".

**Example:** An employee asks the organization's lawyer to describe salary-survey data as "independent" when the employee supplied it. Helping draft that description to mislead the board is assisting fraud on the client (cf. Feb. 2020 Q2).

## Competence, diligence and communication

### R-PRF-008 — Competence
**Rule (write this):** ABA: a lawyer must provide competent representation — the legal knowledge, skill, thoroughness and preparation reasonably necessary. California: a lawyer shall not intentionally, recklessly, with gross negligence, or repeatedly fail to perform legal services with competence, meaning the learning and skill and the mental, emotional and physical ability reasonably necessary. A lawyer who lacks the skill may still act by associating or consulting with a competent lawyer, acquiring the skill in time, or referring the matter.

**Elements:** 1. Learning and skill (+ CA: mental, emotional, physical ability). 2. Thoroughness and preparation. 3. Cure: associate/consult, learn, or refer. 4. Emergency: limited assistance when referral is impractical. 5. Technology: duty to keep abreast of the benefits and risks of relevant technology (comment).

**Exceptions / defenses:** In California a single negligent mistake is malpractice, not necessarily a disciplinary violation, because the rule requires intentional, reckless, grossly negligent or repeated failure [S-566]. Proposed AI-specific comments to rule 1.1 were **pending, not adopted**, as of Nov. 2026 [S-122] [VERIFY V-031]; the existing duty already reaches AI use (State Bar Practical Guidance on Generative AI, 2023; ABA Formal Op. 512 (2024)).

**Authority:** CRPC rule 1.1(a)–(d) and cmt. [1] (technology, eff. Mar. 22, 2021) [S-566]; MR 1.1 and cmt. [8].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE (MPRE: simple competence). **CA vs. ABA:** differs — CA disciplinary standard is intentional/reckless/grossly negligent/repeated; CA expressly adds mental, emotional and physical ability.

**Example:** A family lawyer takes a securities arbitration with no experience and files nothing for months. ABA: incompetent unless he associated an expert or studied in time; CA: a pattern of failures likely meets the gross-negligence/repeated standard.

### R-PRF-009 — Diligence
**Rule (write this):** A lawyer must act with reasonable diligence and promptness (ABA); in California, a lawyer shall not intentionally, repeatedly, recklessly or with gross negligence fail to act with reasonable diligence, meaning commitment and dedication to the client's interests without neglecting, disregarding or unduly delaying the matter.

**Elements:** 1. Commitment and dedication. 2. No neglect, disregard or undue delay (missed deadlines, unfiled papers). 3. CA culpability threshold as for competence.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Reasonable scheduling choices and client-requested delays are not violations.

**Authority:** CRPC rule 1.3; MR 1.3.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** differs — CA requires intentional, repeated, reckless or grossly negligent failure.

**Example:** A lawyer lets the statute of limitations run on a personal-injury claim while on vacation. That is a lack of diligence (and malpractice); repeated neglect satisfies the CA standard.

### R-PRF-010 — Communication with clients
**Rule (write this):** A lawyer must promptly inform the client of any decision or circumstance requiring the client's informed consent, reasonably consult about the means, keep the client reasonably informed of significant developments, promptly comply with reasonable requests for information, advise the client of limits on what the lawyer may do, and explain matters enough for the client to make informed decisions; California adds the statutory duty to respond promptly to reasonable status inquiries.

**Elements:** 1. Prompt notice of matters needing consent (conflicts, settlement offers). 2. Consultation about means. 3. Keep informed; respond to requests (and, CA, provide copies of significant documents when necessary). 4. Explain enough for informed decisions. 5. Advise of limits when the client expects unethical help.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A lawyer may delay transmitting information if the client would likely react in a way causing imminent harm to the client or others (CRPC rule 1.4(c)); obligations yield to protective orders and nondisclosure agreements (rule 1.4(d)).

**Authority:** CRPC rule 1.4; B&P § 6068(m); MR 1.4.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** largely the same; CA adds § 6068(m) and the express imminent-harm delay rule.

**Example:** A client calls five times over two months asking about her case and gets no reply. Violation of rule 1.4(a)(3), MR 1.4(a)(3)–(4) and B&P § 6068(m).

### R-PRF-011 — Communicating settlement offers and plea offers
**Rule (write this):** A lawyer must promptly communicate to the client all terms and conditions of a proposed plea bargain or other dispositive offer in a criminal matter, and all amounts, terms and conditions of any written settlement offer in any other matter (California); the ABA reaches the same result through the general communication duty.

**Elements:** 1. Criminal: every plea or dispositive offer, oral or written. 2. Civil: every **written** offer (CA rule and statute); oral offers that are significant must also be communicated under rule 1.4 and MR 1.4. 3. Promptly. 4. "Client" includes the person with authority to accept.

**Exceptions / defenses:** None for "unreasonable" offers — the lawyer may advise rejection but must still transmit the offer, unless the client has previously said that such an offer is acceptable or unacceptable (MR 1.4 cmt. [2]).

**Authority:** CRPC rule 1.4.1; B&P § 6103.5; MR 1.4(a)(1) and cmt. [2]; *Missouri v. Frye*, 566 U.S. 134 (2012) (failing to convey a formal plea offer is deficient performance).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** differs in form — CA has a specific rule and statute; ABA relies on MR 1.4.

**Example:** Defense counsel rejects a written $50,000 offer as "insulting" without telling the client. Violates rule 1.4.1, § 6103.5 and MR 1.4.

### R-PRF-012 — Disclosure of lack of professional liability insurance (California)
**Rule (write this):** A California lawyer who knows or reasonably should know that he does not have professional liability (malpractice) insurance must inform a client in writing at the time of engagement, and must notify a client in writing within 30 days if coverage ends during the representation.

**Elements:** 1. No insurance (or coverage lapses). 2. Written notice at engagement or within 30 days of lapse.

**Exceptions / defenses:** The rule does not apply (rule 1.4.2(c)): (1) where the lawyer knows or reasonably should know at engagement that the representation will not exceed four hours (if it later does, the lawyer must then comply); (2) to a government lawyer or in-house counsel representing or advising the employer in that capacity; (3) to legal services rendered in an emergency to avoid foreseeable prejudice to the client; or (4) where the lawyer has already given the client the written notice [S-1262].

**Authority:** CRPC rule 1.4.2(a)–(c) [S-1262].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** California-only rule; the ABA has no disclosure rule (only a Model Court Rule on insurance disclosure to the court).

**Example:** A solo practitioner without insurance signs up a client by email and says nothing about coverage. Violation of rule 1.4.2; no ABA violation.

## Duty of confidentiality, privilege and work product

### R-PRF-013 — Duty of confidentiality
**Rule (write this):** A lawyer must not reveal information relating to the representation of a client unless the client gives informed consent, the disclosure is impliedly authorized to carry out the representation, or an exception applies (ABA). In California, a lawyer must "maintain inviolate the confidence, and at every peril to himself or herself … preserve the secrets, of his or her client", and may reveal protected information only with informed consent or under the single death-or-substantial-bodily-harm exception.

**Elements:** 1. Scope: all information relating to the representation, from any source, whether or not privileged, including publicly available but embarrassing or detrimental information (CA). 2. Duty survives the end of the representation and the client's death. 3. Duty to take reasonable precautions against inadvertent or unauthorized disclosure (shared offices, printers, email, cloud, AI tools).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Informed consent; implied authorization; CA exception (R-PRF-014); ABA exceptions (R-PRF-015). Disclosure compelled by court order after the privilege is properly claimed is not a violation.

**Authority:** B&P § 6068(e)(1); CRPC rule 1.6(a); MR 1.6(a), (c); State Bar Formal Op. 2020-203 (data breaches).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** same core duty; CA's duty is statutory and stricter (one exception only); ABA expressly codifies the reasonable-precautions duty in MR 1.6(c).

**Example:** Two lawyers share a suite and printer; a family-law client's financial declaration sits on the shared printer where the other lawyer's clients can see it. Failing to take reasonable precautions breaches both rules (cf. July 2021 Q2).

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: 律师法 also imposes a 保密义务 with a narrow exception for crimes endangering national/public security or serious personal safety (approx.); the US duty is broader than privilege and covers all information relating to the representation.

### R-PRF-014 — California's only exception: preventing a criminal act likely to cause death or substantial bodily harm
**Rule (write this):** A California lawyer may, but is not required to, reveal confidential information to the extent the lawyer reasonably believes disclosure is necessary to prevent a criminal act that the lawyer reasonably believes is likely to result in death of, or substantial bodily harm to, an individual. Before revealing, the lawyer must, if reasonable, try to persuade the client not to commit the act and inform the client of the lawyer's ability or decision to reveal; the disclosure must be no more than necessary.

**Elements:** 1. Future **criminal act** (not past; not a non-criminal accident). 2. Reasonably believed likely to cause death or substantial bodily harm. 3. Disclosure reasonably necessary to prevent it. 4. Pre-disclosure steps if reasonable: dissuade; tell the client. 5. Limited disclosure.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Permissive only — a lawyer who does not reveal does not violate the rule (rule 1.6(e)). No exception for financial crimes, fraud, or to rectify past harm.

**Authority:** B&P § 6068(e)(2); CRPC rule 1.6(b)–(e); Evid. Code § 956.5 (parallel privilege exception).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** differs — ABA covers reasonably certain death or substantial bodily harm from **any** source (not only crimes) and has six more exceptions (R-PRF-015).

**Example:** Client tells his lawyer he will "take care of" a witness tonight and shows a gun. The lawyer may disclose to police after (if reasonable) trying to dissuade the client; the lawyer may not disclose the client's past embezzlement.

### R-PRF-015 — ABA confidentiality exceptions (MR 1.6(b))
**Rule (write this):** Under the ABA rule, a lawyer may reveal information to the extent reasonably necessary (1) to prevent reasonably certain death or substantial bodily harm; (2) to prevent the client's crime or fraud reasonably certain to cause substantial financial injury, in furtherance of which the client used the lawyer's services; (3) to prevent, mitigate or rectify such financial injury; (4) to secure legal advice about the lawyer's own compliance; (5) to establish a claim or defense in a controversy between lawyer and client or respond to allegations about the lawyer's conduct; (6) to comply with other law or a court order; or (7) to detect and resolve conflicts arising from a change of employment.

**Elements:** For (2)–(3): client crime or fraud + lawyer's services used + substantial financial injury. For (5): actual or threatened claim; disclose only what is necessary.

**Exceptions / defenses:** All are permissive. MR 1.13(c) (reporting out) and MR 3.3(c) (candor overrides confidentiality) are separate.

**Authority:** MR 1.6(b)(1)–(7).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MPRE tests these heavily. **CA vs. ABA:** differs sharply — California has **no** financial-fraud exception and no rule-based self-defense exception. California recognizes lawyer self-defense only through privilege exceptions (e.g., Evid. Code § 958, breach of duty in a lawyer-client dispute) and narrow case law; write "California likely permits disclosure only to the extent necessary to respond to the client's claim" [VERIFY V-472].

**Example:** General counsel learns the CEO is using the lawyer's loan documents to defraud lenders. ABA: may disclose to prevent/rectify the fraud (MR 1.6(b)(2)–(3)). California: may not disclose outside the company; must report up (R-PRF-055, R-PRF-056) and may withdraw (cf. July 2023 Q3).

### R-PRF-016 — Confidentiality vs. attorney-client privilege vs. work product
**Rule (write this):** The ethical duty of confidentiality is broader than the attorney-client privilege: it covers all information relating to the representation from any source, applies everywhere, and bars voluntary disclosure; the privilege is an evidentiary rule that protects only confidential lawyer-client communications made for legal advice and applies only when testimony or documents are compelled. Work product protects the lawyer's own preparation materials from discovery.

**Elements:** Confidentiality — ethics, all information, all settings, exceptions narrow (CA) or broader (ABA). Privilege — evidence, communications only, compelled-disclosure settings, exceptions (crime-fraud, breach of duty, joint clients). Work product — litigation preparation; CA absolute for impressions and opinions, qualified for the rest.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A lawyer compelled by a court after asserting the privilege does not violate the duty of confidentiality by testifying.

**Authority:** B&P § 6068(e); CRPC rule 1.6 and cmt.; Evid. Code §§ 952, 954, 955; CCP § 2018.030; MR 1.6 cmt. [3]; FRE 501; FRCP 26(b)(3); *Hickman v. Taylor*, 329 U.S. 495 (1947).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Evidence crossovers test the privilege; PR answers test confidentiality — keep the headings separate. **CA vs. ABA:** same distinction.

**Example:** The prosecutor subpoenas defense counsel to testify where he found a client's bloody clothing. The privilege question (Evidence) and the confidentiality question (PR) are analyzed separately (cf. Feb. 2022 Q4).

### R-PRF-017 — Attorney-client privilege and its exceptions (California Evidence Code)
**Rule (write this):** The client holds a privilege to refuse to disclose, and to prevent others from disclosing, a confidential communication between client and lawyer made in the course of the lawyer-client relationship; the lawyer must claim the privilege whenever it is sought to be disclosed. It does not apply to communications made to enable anyone to commit a crime or fraud, communications relevant to a breach of duty by lawyer or client, communications between joint clients in a later dispute between them, or disclosures the lawyer reasonably believes necessary to prevent a criminal act likely to cause death or substantial bodily harm.

**Elements:** 1. Communication (not underlying facts; but in California the entire communication, facts included, is protected — *Costco*). 2. Confidential. 3. Between client (or representative) and lawyer. 4. For legal services. 5. Holder has not waived (Evid. Code § 912).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Crime-fraud (§ 956); prevent death/SBH (§ 956.5); breach of duty (§ 958); joint clients (§ 962); deceased-client exceptions (§§ 957, 960–961).

**Authority:** Evid. Code §§ 952, 954, 955, 956, 956.5, 958, 962; *Costco Wholesale Corp. v. Superior Court* (2009) 47 Cal.4th 725; *Upjohn Co. v. United States*, 449 U.S. 383 (1981) (federal: corporate employee communications); *Swidler & Berlin v. United States*, 524 U.S. 399 (1998) (survives death).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE Evidence uses federal common law (FRE 501). **CA vs. ABA:** not an ABA topic; state California Evidence Code in a PR answer when privilege is in issue.

**Example:** A client tells his lawyer, "Help me backdate this deed so creditors can't reach the house." No privilege — the crime-fraud exception applies — but the lawyer still owes confidentiality and may not volunteer it in California.

### R-PRF-018 — Work-product protection
**Rule (write this):** In California, a writing that reflects an attorney's impressions, conclusions, opinions, or legal research or theories is not discoverable under any circumstances; other work product is discoverable only if denial would unfairly prejudice the party seeking discovery or result in an injustice. Under the federal rule, documents prepared in anticipation of litigation are discoverable only on substantial need and undue hardship, and mental impressions receive near-absolute protection.

**Elements:** 1. Prepared by or for a party/attorney. 2. CA: attorney work product (belongs to the attorney); federal: in anticipation of litigation. 3. Level: absolute (opinion) vs. qualified (other).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Waiver by voluntary disclosure to an adversary; witness statements obtained by counsel get at least qualified protection in California (*Coito*).

**Authority:** CCP § 2018.030; *Coito v. Superior Court* (2012) 54 Cal.4th 480; FRCP 26(b)(3); *Hickman v. Taylor*, 329 U.S. 495 (1947).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** FRCP version on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** not an ABA rule; mention in Evidence/Civ Pro crossovers.

**Example:** Opposing counsel demands an associate's memo analyzing a witness's credibility. The memo reflects impressions and opinions — absolutely protected under CCP § 2018.030(a).

### R-PRF-019 — Using client information to the client's disadvantage
**Rule (write this):** A lawyer may not use information relating to the representation of a current client to the client's disadvantage without the client's informed consent, and may not use a former client's information to that client's disadvantage unless the information has become generally known or the rules otherwise permit.

**Elements:** 1. Information from the representation. 2. Use (not only disclosure). 3. Disadvantage to the client. 4. No informed consent.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Use that benefits or does not harm the client is permitted; former-client information that is "generally known" may be used.

**Authority:** CRPC rules 1.8.2, 1.9(c); MR 1.8(b), 1.9(c).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** same.

**Example:** A lawyer learns while drafting a client's business plan that the client will buy a vacant lot, and buys the neighboring lot first to resell at a premium. Violation of rule 1.8.2 / MR 1.8(b) (and possibly rule 1.8.1).

### R-PRF-020 — Physical evidence of a crime
**Rule (write this):** A lawyer may not unlawfully obstruct access to evidence or unlawfully alter, destroy or conceal material having potential evidentiary value, and may not counsel or assist another to do so. A defense lawyer who takes possession of physical evidence of a crime may keep it only as long as reasonably necessary to examine it and then must turn it over; if the lawyer removes or alters it, the privilege does not protect testimony about its original location or condition.

**Elements:** 1. Physical evidence (weapon, clothing, documents) vs. communications. 2. Lawyer may decline to take possession; client's communications about it remain privileged/confidential. 3. If taken: reasonable time to examine, then turn over (to court or prosecution under California practice) without revealing the client as the source if possible. 4. No destruction, concealment or return to the client for hiding.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Merely observing evidence without moving it does not waive the privilege as to its location.

**Authority:** CRPC rule 3.4(a) [S-550]; MR 3.4(a) and cmt. [2]; Penal Code § 135 (destroying or concealing evidence); *People v. Meredith* (1981) 29 Cal.3d 682; *People v. Superior Court (Fairbank)* (1987) 192 Cal.App.3d 32 [VERIFY V-462].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** same principle.

**Example:** The client hands his lawyer the bloody pants he wore. The lawyer may examine them briefly but then must turn them over; giving them to the prosecutor was proper, but the lawyer should not volunteer the client's statements about them (cf. Feb. 2022 Q4).

## Duty of loyalty: conflicts of interest

### R-PRF-021 — Current-client conflicts: the general rule
**Rule (write this):** A lawyer may not represent a client if the representation is directly adverse to another current client, or if there is a significant risk that the representation will be materially limited by the lawyer's responsibilities to another client, a former client or a third person, or by the lawyer's own interests, unless (1) the lawyer reasonably believes he can provide competent and diligent representation to each affected client, (2) the representation is not prohibited by law, (3) it does not involve a claim by one client against another in the same litigation, and (4) each affected client gives informed written consent (California) or informed consent, confirmed in writing (ABA).

**Elements:** 1. Identify the conflict type: direct adversity (same or unrelated matter) or material limitation. 2. Is it consentable? (reasonable belief; not prohibited; not same-litigation adversity). 3. Proper consent from **each** affected client. 4. CA: also written disclosure of relationships under rule 1.7(c) (R-PRF-022).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Generally adverse economic interests (competitors) are not direct adversity (MR 1.7 cmt. [6]). An unconsentable conflict requires declining or withdrawing.

**Authority:** CRPC rule 1.7(a), (b), (d) [S-556]; rule 1.0.1(e), (e-1); MR 1.7(a)–(b); *Flatt v. Superior Court* (1994) 9 Cal.4th 275 (simultaneous adverse representation: per se disqualification).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** same structure; differs in consent formality — CA requires **informed written consent** (client signs); ABA accepts informed consent confirmed in writing by the lawyer.

**Example:** Firm represents Bank in loan work and is asked to sue Bank for a new client on an unrelated tort. Direct adversity: both Bank and the new client must give informed written consent (CA) — and Firm must reasonably believe it can represent both.

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: 律师法 bars representing both sides in the same case (双方代理) outright; the US rule reaches far more (unrelated matters, material limitations) but allows consent for most (approx.).

### R-PRF-022 — California written disclosure of relationships (rule 1.7(c))
**Rule (write this):** Even when there is no significant risk of material limitation, a California lawyer must give the client **written disclosure** before representing the client if the lawyer (or another lawyer in the firm, to the lawyer's knowledge) has a legal, business, financial, professional or personal relationship with or responsibility to a party or witness in the same matter, or if the lawyer knows or reasonably should know that another party's lawyer is the lawyer's spouse, parent, child or sibling, lives with the lawyer, is the lawyer's client or a client of the firm, or has an intimate personal relationship with the lawyer.

**Elements:** 1. Relationship with a party or witness, or a family/cohabiting/intimate/client relationship with opposing counsel. 2. Written disclosure (consent not required unless rule 1.7(b) is also triggered).

**Exceptions / defenses:** If the relationship also creates a significant risk of material limitation, rule 1.7(b) consent is required instead.

**Authority:** CRPC rule 1.7(c) [S-556]; compare MR 1.7 cmt. [11] (related lawyers: personal-interest conflict requiring informed consent).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** differs — California has a stand-alone written-disclosure duty with no ABA text equivalent; ABA treats close relationships with opposing counsel as a possible material-limitation conflict.

**Example:** Plaintiff's lawyer once dated defense counsel. CA: if the relationship was an "intimate personal relationship" (present tense), written disclosure is required; a past relationship may still need disclosure if it creates a significant risk of limitation. ABA: consent needed only if there is a significant risk (cf. Oct. 2020 Q1).

### R-PRF-023 — Personal-interest conflicts (the lawyer's own interests)
**Rule (write this):** A material-limitation conflict exists when the lawyer's own financial, business, ideological, employment or personal interests create a significant risk of limiting the representation; the lawyer must obtain the client's informed written consent (CA) / informed consent confirmed in writing (ABA), or decline.

**Elements:** Typical triggers: negotiating for employment with the opposing party's firm; membership in or funding of an advocacy group opposed to the client's position; a romantic relationship with the opposing lawyer; investments in the opposing party; the lawyer's own exposure to malpractice.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Membership in an organization alone is not a conflict unless it creates a significant risk (CA rule 6.3–6.4 address legal-services and law-reform organizations).

**Authority:** CRPC rule 1.7(b); MR 1.7(a)(2) and cmt. [10] (job negotiations).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** same test; CA consent formality stricter.

**Example:** An associate defending a manufacturer accepts an interview with the plaintiff's firm. He has a significant-risk conflict and must disclose and obtain consent or stop working on the case (cf. July 2019 Q4).

### R-PRF-024 — Joint representation (co-parties; entity and constituent)
**Rule (write this):** A lawyer may represent multiple clients in the same matter only if the conflict is consentable and each client gives informed written consent after disclosure of the risks, including that information will be shared among them, that the lawyer cannot take sides if their interests diverge, and that the lawyer may have to withdraw from all.

**Elements:** 1. Present and foreseeable divergence (co-defendants with different defenses; buyer and seller; spouses in a divorce). 2. Consentability. 3. Informed written consent from each. 4. Privilege: joint-client communications are not privileged in later disputes between them (Evid. Code § 962).

**Exceptions / defenses:** No joint representation where one client asserts a claim against another in the same litigation (rule 1.7(d)(3)). In criminal cases the Sixth Amendment requires conflict-free counsel; courts may refuse a waiver.

**Authority:** CRPC rule 1.7; MR 1.7 cmts. [23], [29]–[33]; Evid. Code § 962; *Holloway v. Arkansas*, 435 U.S. 475 (1978); *Cuyler v. Sullivan*, 446 U.S. 335 (1980); *Wheat v. United States*, 486 U.S. 153 (1988).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE; Sixth Amendment overlaps with Criminal Procedure. **CA vs. ABA:** same; CA consent must be signed.

**Example:** One lawyer represents a driver and passenger suing the other driver. If the defendant claims the plaintiff-driver was partly at fault, the passenger has a claim against the driver — the joint representation becomes unconsentable for that litigation.

### R-PRF-025 — Advance waivers and the "hot potato" rule
**Rule (write this):** A lawyer may not drop a current client "like a hot potato" to cure a conflict and take on a more attractive adverse client; the dropped client is still treated as a current client for conflict purposes. An advance (prospective) waiver is effective only if the client reasonably understood the material risks, and a waiver is ineffective as to a conflict the lawyer already knew of but did not disclose.

**Elements:** Hot potato: withdrawal motivated by the desire to take an adverse matter → current-client standard applies. Advance waiver: specificity, client sophistication, independent counsel, disclosure of known conflicts.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Thrust-upon conflicts (e.g., a corporate merger creates adversity) may permit withdrawal from one client.

**Authority:** *Flatt v. Superior Court* (1994) 9 Cal.4th 275; *Truck Ins. Exchange v. Fireman's Fund Ins. Co.* (1992) 6 Cal.App.4th 1050 [VERIFY V-465]; *Sheppard, Mullin, Richter & Hampton, LLP v. J-M Manufacturing Co.* (2018) 6 Cal.5th 59; CRPC rule 1.7 cmt.; MR 1.7 cmt. [22].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** same principles.

**Example:** Firm's engagement letter contains a general advance waiver, but Firm knew it was then representing the client's adversary in another matter and said nothing. Under *Sheppard Mullin* the consent is invalid and the engagement agreement unenforceable.

### R-PRF-026 — Business transactions with a client and adverse pecuniary interests
**Rule (write this):** A lawyer may not enter into a business transaction with a client, or knowingly acquire an ownership, possessory, security or other pecuniary interest adverse to a client, unless (1) the transaction and its terms are fair and reasonable to the client and are fully disclosed and transmitted in writing in a manner the client can understand; (2) the client is advised in writing to seek independent counsel and given a reasonable opportunity to do so (or is independently represented); and (3) the client consents — in California, informed written consent to the terms of the transaction and to the lawyer's role in it (rule 1.8.1(c)); under the ABA, informed consent, in a writing signed by the client, to the essential terms and the lawyer's role, including whether the lawyer represents the client in the transaction (MR 1.8(a)(3)).

**Elements:** 1. Business transaction or adverse interest (loans, partnerships, buying client property, taking stock for fees, a charging lien in California). 2. Fair and reasonable terms. 3. Written disclosure. 4. Written advice to consult independent counsel + opportunity. 5. Written consent.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Ordinary standard-commercial transactions for products the client sells to the public (MR 1.8 cmt. [1]). ABA: a lien authorized by law to secure fees is allowed (MR 1.8(i)(1)); California treats a charging lien as an adverse interest subject to rule 1.8.1 (*Fletcher v. Davis*).

**Authority:** CRPC rule 1.8.1; MR 1.8(a); *Fletcher v. Davis* (2004) 33 Cal.4th 61.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** same three requirements; differs on liens (CA rule 1.8.1 applies to a charging lien); both require client consent in writing to the terms and to the lawyer's role (CA: informed written consent, rule 1.8.1(c); ABA: informed consent in a writing signed by the client, MR 1.8(a)(3)).

**Example:** After a divorce, the lawyer drafts a partnership agreement for a florist business with his former client, contributing capital in exchange for 50%. Unless the terms are fair, disclosed in writing, the client is advised in writing to get independent counsel, and she consents in writing, rule 1.8.1 / MR 1.8(a) is violated (cf. Feb. 2025 Q5).

### R-PRF-027 — Gifts from clients and instruments benefiting the lawyer
**Rule (write this):** A lawyer may not solicit a substantial gift from a client (including a testamentary gift) for the lawyer or a person related to the lawyer, or prepare an instrument giving the lawyer or a related person a substantial gift, unless the recipient is related to the client; California also allows preparation if the client has been advised by an independent lawyer who provides a certificate of independent review under the Probate Code.

**Elements:** 1. Substantial gift. 2. Solicitation or drafting. 3. Recipient = lawyer or related person. 4. Exceptions: recipient related to client; (CA) certificate of independent review.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A client may make an unsolicited, simple gift (a holiday present) that the lawyer did not draft (MR 1.8 cmt. [6]). California's Probate Code presumes fraud or undue influence for donative transfers to the drafter [VERIFY V-471].

**Authority:** CRPC rule 1.8.3; MR 1.8(c); Prob. Code §§ 21380, 21384 [VERIFY V-471].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE (Wills crossover). **CA vs. ABA:** differs — CA adds the independent-review certificate route.

**Example:** A lawyer drafts a will for an elderly client leaving the lawyer her house. Violation unless the lawyer is related to her; in California, it is permissible only with a certificate of independent review.

### R-PRF-028 — Financial assistance to clients
**Rule (write this):** ABA: in connection with pending or contemplated litigation, a lawyer may not provide financial assistance to a client except advancing court costs and litigation expenses (repayment may be contingent on outcome), paying those costs for an indigent client, and modest humanitarian gifts to an indigent pro bono client. California: a lawyer may not pay or agree to pay a client's personal or business expenses, but may advance litigation costs, pay costs for an indigent client, and — after being retained — lend money to the client on the client's written promise to repay, after complying with rules 1.7(b), 1.7(c) and 1.8.1.

**Elements:** 1. What is paid (costs vs. living/business expenses). 2. Timing (CA loan only after retention; no promise of loans to obtain the case). 3. Written promise to repay + conflict compliance (CA).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Paying expenses to third persons from funds collected for the client, with consent (CA rule 1.8.5(b)(1)).

**Authority:** CRPC rule 1.8.5 [S-556]; MR 1.8(e)(1)–(3).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE (MPRE: no living-expense loans). **CA vs. ABA:** differs — CA permits post-retention loans for any purpose with a written promise to repay; ABA prohibits them (except modest humanitarian gifts in pro bono cases).

**Example:** An injured client cannot pay rent while the case is pending. CA: lawyer may lend him $3,000 after retention on a written repayment promise, complying with rules 1.7 and 1.8.1. ABA: prohibited.

### R-PRF-029 — Compensation from a third party
**Rule (write this):** A lawyer may accept payment for representing a client from someone other than the client only if there is no interference with the lawyer's independent professional judgment or with the lawyer-client relationship, the client's confidential information is protected, and the client gives informed written consent (CA) / informed consent (ABA).

**Elements:** 1. Third-party payor (parent, employer, insurer). 2. No interference — the payor cannot direct the work (rule 5.4(c); MR 5.4(c)). 3. Confidentiality toward the payor. 4. Consent.

**Exceptions / defenses:** CA: no consent needed where nondisclosure is authorized by law or court order, or for public-agency/nonprofit legal services.

**Authority:** CRPC rule 1.8.6; MR 1.8(f); CRPC rule 5.4(c); MR 5.4(c).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** differs — CA requires informed **written** consent; ABA informed consent (MR 1.8(f) has no writing requirement).

**Example:** A mother pays her daughter's divorce fees and asks to be told everything her daughter says. The lawyer may take the payment with the daughter's informed written consent but may not share her confidences with the mother (cf. Feb. 2025 Q5).

### R-PRF-030 — Aggregate settlements
**Rule (write this):** A lawyer who represents two or more clients may not make an aggregate settlement of their claims (or, in a criminal case, an aggregated agreement on pleas) unless each client gives informed written consent (CA) / informed consent in a writing signed by the client (ABA), after disclosure of the existence and nature of all the claims and each person's participation.

**Elements:** 1. Two or more clients. 2. Settlement that depends on all or allocates a lump sum. 3. Disclosure of all claims/participants and shares. 4. Signed written consent of each.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Class actions are governed by court approval procedures.

**Authority:** CRPC rule 1.8.7; MR 1.8(g).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** same.

**Example:** Defendant offers $1 million to settle the claims of five plaintiffs represented by one lawyer. Each must know the others' shares and consent in writing.

### R-PRF-031 — Limiting liability to clients and settling malpractice claims
**Rule (write this):** California: a lawyer may not contract with a client prospectively limiting the lawyer's liability for malpractice, and may not settle a claim for malpractice with a client or former client unless the client is independently represented or is advised in writing to seek independent counsel and given a reasonable opportunity to do so. ABA: a prospective limitation is allowed only if the client is independently represented in making it; settlement rules are the same. California also forbids any agreement that the client will not report the lawyer to, or will withdraw a complaint from, the State Bar.

**Elements:** 1. Prospective limitation (absolute bar in CA). 2. Settlement of an existing claim: independent counsel or written advice + opportunity. 3. No gag on State Bar complaints (B&P § 6090.5; see also rule 5.6(b), R-PRF-081).

**Exceptions / defenses:** An agreement to arbitrate malpractice claims is not a limitation of liability if the client is fully informed (MR 1.8 cmt. [17]).

**Authority:** CRPC rule 1.8.8; MR 1.8(h); B&P § 6090.5.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** differs — CA bans prospective limitations outright; ABA permits them if the client is independently represented. B&P § 6090.5 is California-only.

**Example:** A lawyer who collected a $30,000 fee before doing any work offers a $10,000 refund if the client signs a release of all claims. Without written advice to consult independent counsel and a reasonable opportunity to do so, the release violates rule 1.8.8(b) / MR 1.8(h)(2); a clause barring a State Bar complaint violates § 6090.5 (cf. July 2022 Q3).

### R-PRF-032 — Sexual relations with a client
**Rule (write this):** A lawyer may not engage in sexual relations with a current client unless a consensual sexual relationship existed between them when the lawyer-client relationship began (California also excepts a spouse or registered domestic partner). California's statute further forbids requiring sex as a condition of representation, using coercion, intimidation or undue influence, and continuing a representation when the relationship causes incompetent performance.

**Elements:** 1. Current client (not a former client). 2. Sexual relations (intercourse or touching of intimate parts for sexual purposes). 3. No pre-existing consensual relationship. 4. Statutory prohibitions (§ 6106.9).

**Exceptions / defenses:** A relationship with a **former** client is not barred by these rules, but a romance that begins during the representation may still create a rule 1.7(b) personal-interest conflict. The rule is not imputed to other firm lawyers (rule 1.8.11 imputes only rules 1.8.1–1.8.9; MR 1.8(k) excludes 1.8(j)).

**Authority:** CRPC rule 1.8.10; B&P § 6106.9 [S-564]; MR 1.8(j).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** same core rule since 2018; California adds § 6106.9 and a procedural limit on State Bar charges brought on a non-client's complaint.

**Example:** A lawyer begins dating a referred client mid-case. Sexual relations violate rule 1.8.10 / MR 1.8(j); even a non-sexual romance is a material-limitation conflict needing informed written consent (cf. Feb. 2021 Q4).

### R-PRF-033 — Other specific conflicts (literary rights, proprietary interest, judicial sales) and imputation of rule 1.8 prohibitions
**Rule (write this):** ABA rules forbid a lawyer, before the representation ends, from acquiring literary or media rights to a portrayal based on the representation, and from acquiring a proprietary interest in the cause of action except a lien to secure fees or a reasonable contingent fee; California has no equivalent rules and analyzes such arrangements as business transactions under rule 1.8.1. California bars a lawyer from purchasing property at a probate, foreclosure or judicial sale in a matter in which the lawyer or an affiliated lawyer acts. While lawyers are associated in a firm, a prohibition in rules 1.8.1–1.8.9 that applies to one applies to all.

**Elements:** 1. Identify the specific arrangement. 2. ABA flat bans (1.8(d), (i)); CA rule 1.8.1 analysis. 3. Imputation: all of 1.8 except sexual relations.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Contingent fees are permitted in civil matters (R-PRF-043).

**Authority:** MR 1.8(d), (i), (k); CRPC rules 1.8.9, 1.8.11; State Bar Commission, concepts considered but rejected [S-569].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE (MPRE tests 1.8(d)). **CA vs. ABA:** differs — no literary-rights or proprietary-interest rule in California.

**Example:** A criminal defense lawyer takes the movie rights to the client's story as his fee. ABA: prohibited before the case ends. California: a rule 1.8.1 transaction plus a rule 1.7(b) conflict (the lawyer may prefer a dramatic trial to a plea).

### R-PRF-034 — Former-client conflicts (substantial relationship)
**Rule (write this):** A lawyer who formerly represented a client may not represent another person in the same or a substantially related matter in which that person's interests are materially adverse to the former client, unless the former client gives informed written consent (CA) / informed consent confirmed in writing (ABA). Matters are substantially related if they involve the same transaction or legal dispute or there is a substantial risk that confidential information normally obtained in the prior representation would materially advance the new client's position.

**Elements:** 1. Former client. 2. Same or substantially related matter (compare facts, legal issues, and the lawyer's involvement). 3. Material adversity. 4. No consent. 5. California: if the lawyer had a direct and personal relationship with the former client, access to confidential information is presumed.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Generally known information and general knowledge of a client's policies do not alone make matters related (MR 1.9 cmt. [3]).

**Authority:** CRPC rule 1.9(a); MR 1.9(a) and cmt. [3]; *H.F. Ahmanson & Co. v. Salomon Brothers, Inc.* (1991) 229 Cal.App.3d 1445; *Jessen v. Hartford Casualty Ins. Co.* (2003) 111 Cal.App.4th 698; *Flatt v. Superior Court* (1994) 9 Cal.4th 275.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** same test; CA consent formality stricter.

**Example:** Lawyer drafted Seller's warranty disclosures for a building, then is asked by Buyer to sue Seller for nondisclosure about the same building. Same matter, materially adverse: Seller's informed written consent is required.

### R-PRF-035 — Lawyers who change firms; former-client information
**Rule (write this):** A lawyer who moves firms may not knowingly represent a person in the same or a substantially related matter adverse to a client of the lawyer's former firm about whom the lawyer actually acquired material confidential information, unless that client gives informed written consent; and no lawyer may use or reveal a former client's information except as permitted for a current client or when it has become generally known.

**Elements:** 1. Former firm's client (not the lawyer's personal client). 2. Actual acquisition of material confidential information. 3. Substantially related + materially adverse.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A lawyer who had no access to the information is not personally disqualified.

**Authority:** CRPC rule 1.9(b)–(c); MR 1.9(b)–(c).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** same.

**Example:** An associate who never worked on or heard about Firm A's representation of Bank moves to Firm B, which sues Bank on a related loan. She is not personally disqualified under rule 1.9(b).

### R-PRF-036 — Imputation of conflicts within a firm
**Rule (write this):** While lawyers are associated in a firm, none may knowingly represent a client when any one of them practicing alone would be prohibited by rule 1.7 or 1.9, unless the prohibition rests on a personal interest of the disqualified lawyer that creates no significant risk of limiting the other lawyers' representation, or the lateral-screening exception applies. After a lawyer leaves, the firm may act adversely to the departed lawyer's former client unless the matter is substantially related and a remaining lawyer has material confidential information.

**Elements:** 1. "Firm" (including office-sharers who hold themselves out as a firm, and of-counsel relationships). 2. Underlying 1.7/1.9 conflict. 3. Exceptions: personal-interest; screening (R-PRF-037); consent of the affected client under rule 1.7 conditions. 4. Rule 1.8 prohibitions are imputed under rule 1.8.11 (except sexual relations).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Government lawyers are governed by rule 1.11 (R-PRF-038).

**Authority:** CRPC rule 1.10(a)–(d); MR 1.10(a)–(d); *People ex rel. Dept. of Corporations v. SpeeDee Oil Change Systems, Inc.* (1999) 20 Cal.4th 1135 (of counsel attorney's conflict imputed; disqualification).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** same general rule; screening differs (R-PRF-037).

**Example:** Two lawyers share a suite, a receptionist and a conference room and answer the phone with a joint name. If they hold themselves out as a firm, one lawyer's conflict is imputed to the other (cf. July 2021 Q2).

### R-PRF-037 — Screening lateral lawyers
**Rule (write this):** ABA: a lateral lawyer's former-client conflict is not imputed if the lawyer is timely screened, apportioned no part of the fee, and written notice (with later certifications of compliance on request) is given to the former client. California: screening works only if the prohibited lawyer **did not substantially participate** in the same or a substantially related matter, is timely screened and apportioned no part of the fee, and written notice is promptly given to the affected former client.

**Elements:** 1. Conflict arises from the lawyer's prior firm (rule 1.9(a) or (b)). 2. CA: no substantial participation. 3. Timely screen (before the lawyer touches the matter). 4. No fee share. 5. Prompt written notice describing the screen.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Courts may still disqualify if the screen is late or porous; *Kirk* held an effective screen can rebut the presumption of shared knowledge in some circumstances.

**Authority:** CRPC rule 1.10(a)(2) [S-556]; MR 1.10(a)(2); rule 1.0.1(k) ("screened"); *Kirk v. First American Title Ins. Co.* (2010) 183 Cal.App.4th 776.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE (MPRE: screening cures any lateral conflict). **CA vs. ABA:** differs — California bars screening when the lateral substantially participated; ABA allows screening regardless of the level of participation.

**Example:** Associate X was second chair defending Acme at Firm A and moves to Firm B, which is suing Acme in the same case. ABA: screen + notice lets Firm B continue. California: X substantially participated, so screening does not cure; Firm B needs Acme's informed written consent.

### R-PRF-038 — Government lawyers (revolving door) and former judges and neutrals
**Rule (write this):** A former government lawyer may not represent a private client in a matter in which the lawyer participated personally and substantially as a public officer or employee unless the agency gives informed written consent; the lawyer's new firm avoids imputation by timely screening, no fee share, and prompt written notice to the agency. A current government lawyer may not participate in a matter in which he participated personally and substantially in private practice without the agency's consent, nor negotiate private employment with a party or lawyer in a matter in which he is participating personally and substantially. Former judges, arbitrators, mediators and law clerks are similarly barred, with screening available to their firms.

**Elements:** 1. "Matter" (specific parties, not rulemaking). 2. Personal and substantial participation. 3. Agency consent or firm screen. 4. Confidential government information about a person cannot be used against that person.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A law clerk may negotiate employment with a party's lawyer after notifying the judge (rule 1.12 / MR 1.12(b)).

**Authority:** CRPC rules 1.11, 1.12; MR 1.11, 1.12.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** same structure; CA consent is informed written consent.

**Example:** A deputy district attorney who supervised a fraud investigation joins a defense firm and is asked to defend the target. She is personally barred; the firm may proceed only if she is screened and the DA's office receives written notice.

### R-PRF-039 — Insurance defense (tripartite relationship) and Cumis counsel
**Rule (write this):** Defense counsel retained by an insurer represents the insured (and, in California, usually the insurer too) and must not let the insurer's interests compromise the defense; when the insurer reserves its rights on a coverage issue whose outcome defense counsel could control, California requires the insurer to provide independent (Cumis) counsel at its expense.

**Elements:** 1. Third-party payor rules (R-PRF-029). 2. Conflict check between insurer and insured (coverage, policy limits). 3. Reservation of rights + counsel's control of the coverage fact → independent counsel.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A reservation of rights on punitive damages or on amounts over policy limits does not alone require independent counsel under the statute.

**Authority:** Civ. Code § 2860; *San Diego Navy Federal Credit Union v. Cumis Ins. Society, Inc.* (1984) 162 Cal.App.3d 358; CRPC rules 1.7, 1.8.6, 5.4(c); MR 1.7 cmt. [13], 1.8(f).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** CA-specific statute; ABA analyzes under MR 1.7 and 1.8(f).

**Example:** The insurer defends under a reservation that intentional acts are not covered, and the defense lawyer's handling of the "intent" issue will determine coverage. The insured is entitled to independent counsel under § 2860.

### R-PRF-040 — Disqualification and other consequences of conflicts
**Rule (write this):** A court may disqualify a lawyer under its inherent power to protect the integrity of the judicial process; simultaneous representation of adverse clients results in near-automatic disqualification, while former-client conflicts require a substantial relationship, and the court weighs the client's right to chosen counsel, the attorney's interest, the financial burden of replacing counsel, and possible tactical abuse.

**Elements:** 1. Standing (usually the current or former client). 2. Conflict proven. 3. Balancing factors. 4. Other consequences: discipline, fee forfeiture or unenforceable fee agreement (*Sheppard Mullin*), malpractice/breach-of-fiduciary-duty liability.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Delay in moving to disqualify may amount to waiver.

**Authority:** CCP § 128(a)(5); *People ex rel. Dept. of Corporations v. SpeeDee Oil Change Systems, Inc.* (1999) 20 Cal.4th 1135; *Flatt v. Superior Court* (1994) 9 Cal.4th 275.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** disqualification is court law, not a Model Rule.

**Example:** Firm represents both a company and the employee suing it. The company's motion to disqualify will be granted automatically under *Flatt*.

## Fees

### R-PRF-041 — Fee standard: unconscionable (CA) vs. unreasonable (ABA)
**Rule (write this):** ABA: a lawyer may not make an agreement for, charge or collect an unreasonable fee or unreasonable expenses, judged by factors such as time and labor, novelty and difficulty, customary fees, amount involved and results obtained, time limits, the relationship, the lawyer's experience, and whether the fee is fixed or contingent. California: a lawyer may not make an agreement for, charge or collect an unconscionable or illegal fee, judged on the facts when the agreement was made, considering factors including fraud or overreaching, failure to disclose material facts, the amount of the fee in proportion to the value of the services, the parties' relative sophistication, and whether the client gave informed consent to the fee.

**Elements:** ABA 8 factors (MR 1.5(a)). CA 13 non-exclusive factors (rule 1.5(b)), assessed at formation unless the parties contemplated later events.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A fee that is high but agreed by a sophisticated client after full disclosure is rarely unconscionable.

**Authority:** CRPC rule 1.5(a)–(b); MR 1.5(a); *Herrscher v. State Bar* (1935) 4 Cal.2d 399 (fee so exorbitant as to shock the conscience) [VERIFY V-473].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** differs — California's "unconscionable" is a higher threshold than the ABA's "unreasonable"; California adds fraud/overreaching, nondisclosure and sophistication factors.

**Example:** A lawyer charges 40% of a $2 million insurance payout that the insurer had already offered before she was retained, for two hours of work. Likely unreasonable (ABA) and unconscionable (CA: disproportion and nondisclosure of the existing offer).

### R-PRF-042 — Written fee agreements (B&P §§ 6147–6148) and communicating the basis of the fee
**Rule (write this):** ABA: the scope of the representation and the basis or rate of the fee and expenses must be communicated, preferably in writing, before or within a reasonable time after starting. California: when it is reasonably foreseeable that total expense to the client, including fees, will exceed $1,000, the agreement must be in writing, signed by lawyer and client, with a copy to the client, stating the basis of compensation, the general nature of the services, and the responsibilities of lawyer and client; noncompliance makes the agreement voidable at the client's option, leaving the lawyer a reasonable fee.

**Elements:** § 6148: (1) > $1,000 foreseeable; (2) writing signed by both; (3) basis of compensation, nature of services, responsibilities; (4) bills must state the basis. Exceptions: emergency; services of the same kind previously rendered to and paid by the client; client's written waiver after full disclosure; corporate client.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Contingency agreements fall under § 6147 (R-PRF-043).

**Authority:** B&P § 6148; MR 1.5(b); CRPC rule 1.5.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** differs — California requires a signed writing above $1,000; ABA only "preferably" in writing (except contingency fees).

**Example:** A lawyer agrees by phone to defend a client in a $20,000 lawsuit at $400 an hour and sends nothing in writing. ABA: no violation if the rate was communicated. California: § 6148 violation; the client may void the agreement and pay only a reasonable fee (cf. Feb. 2020 Q2).

### R-PRF-043 — Contingent fees
**Rule (write this):** A contingent fee is permitted except where prohibited, but the agreement must be in writing signed by the client and state the method of calculation, including the percentages for settlement, trial and appeal, the expenses deducted and whether before or after the fee is calculated, and the expenses the client must pay regardless of outcome; at the end the lawyer must give a written statement of the outcome and the computation. California's statute also requires a statement that the fee is not set by law and is negotiable, and how the lawyer will be paid for related matters not covered; noncompliance makes the agreement voidable by the client, leaving a reasonable fee.

**Elements:** ABA: writing signed by client; method; expenses; closing statement. CA (§ 6147): signed by both, copy to client; rate; effect of costs on fee and recovery; related-matter compensation; "not set by law and negotiable" statement (unless § 6146 applies); voidable if noncompliant.

**Exceptions / defenses:** § 6147 does not cover workers' compensation matters, and claims between merchants are governed by § 6147.5 [S-560]. Medical-malpractice fees are capped by § 6146.

**Authority:** MR 1.5(c); B&P §§ 6146, 6147, 6147.5 [S-560]; CRPC rule 1.5.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** differs — California adds the negotiability statement and makes the contract voidable.

**Example:** A personal-injury contingency agreement says "33% of recovery" and nothing else. ABA: incomplete (no expense treatment). California: voidable for lack of the § 6147 statements; the lawyer recovers only a reasonable fee, not exceeding the contract amount.

### R-PRF-044 — Prohibited contingent fees (family law and criminal defense)
**Rule (write this):** A lawyer may not make an agreement for, charge or collect a fee in a family-law (ABA: domestic-relations) matter whose payment or amount is contingent on securing a dissolution or declaration of nullity of a marriage (ABA: a divorce) or on the amount of spousal or child support (ABA: alimony or support), "or property settlement in lieu thereof"; nor may a lawyer charge a contingent fee for representing a defendant in a criminal case.

**Elements:** 1. Family: contingency on obtaining the dissolution or nullity, or on the amount of support or of a property settlement made in lieu of support. A fee contingent on an ordinary division of community property is not expressly covered by the text; argue both ways (text vs. the rule's policy against giving the lawyer a stake in the divorce outcome), and test it under the fee and conflict rules (R-PRF-041, R-PRF-023). 2. Criminal defense: any fee contingent on the result (acquittal, dismissal, reduced charge), including non-cash rewards.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Contingent fees to collect past-due support or property already awarded are generally allowed (MR 1.5 cmt. [6]).

**Authority:** CRPC rule 1.5(c)(1)–(2); MR 1.5(d)(1)–(2) [VERIFY V-1010].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** same.

**Example:** A divorce lawyer's fee is 20% of a lump sum the client accepts in place of spousal support: prohibited under both rules. A 10% "bonus" on the client's share of the community estate is not expressly covered; argue the rule's policy and the lawyer's personal stake. A criminal defense lawyer who asks for the client's season tickets if she wins an acquittal charges a prohibited contingent fee under both rules (cf. Feb. 2024 Q3).

### R-PRF-045 — True retainers, nonrefundable fees, advance fees and flat fees
**Rule (write this):** A fee may be called "earned on receipt" or "nonrefundable" only if it is a true retainer — paid solely to secure the lawyer's availability for a period or matter and not as compensation for services — and the client agrees in writing after disclosure that it is not refundable. Advance fees for services are the client's property until earned and must be refunded if unearned at termination. A flat fee for specified services is permitted; in California it may be deposited in the operating account only after written disclosure of the client's right to have it held in trust and to a refund of any unearned portion (signed by the client if over $1,000).

**Elements:** 1. Classify the payment: true retainer / advance fee / flat fee. 2. Nonrefundable label only for a true retainer with written agreement. 3. Unearned portion refunded on termination (R-PRF-053). 4. Deposit rules (R-PRF-047).

**Exceptions / defenses:** A true retainer is still subject to the unconscionability standard. The State Bar's 2026 ethics opinion concludes that labeling a flat fee "nonrefundable" or "earned on receipt" violates rule 1.5(d) and may be dishonest [S-559] [VERIFY V-467].

**Authority:** CRPC rules 1.5(d)–(e), 1.15(b), 1.16(e)(2); MR 1.5 cmt. [4], 1.15(c), 1.16(d); *Baranowski v. State Bar* (1979) 24 Cal.3d 153 [VERIFY V-466].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** largely the same; California codifies the true-retainer and flat-fee rules and the operating-account option.

**Example:** A lawyer takes a "$15,000 nonrefundable retainer" to handle a DUI and is fired after a week. It was an advance fee for services, not a true retainer; the unearned portion must be refunded.

### R-PRF-046 — Fee division between lawyers not in the same firm; referral fees
**Rule (write this):** ABA: lawyers not in the same firm may divide a fee only if the division is in proportion to the services each performs or each assumes joint responsibility, the client agrees in writing to the arrangement including each lawyer's share, and the total fee is reasonable. California: lawyers not in the same firm may divide a fee only if (1) they enter a written agreement to divide it; (2) the client consents in writing, at the time or as soon as reasonably practicable, after full written disclosure that a division will be made, of the identity of the lawyers, and of the terms of the division; and (3) the total fee is not increased by reason of the division — there is no proportionality or joint-responsibility requirement, so pure referral fees are allowed.

**Elements:** CA: written lawyer-lawyer agreement; written client consent after written disclosure; no increase. ABA: proportionality or joint responsibility; client written agreement to shares; reasonable total.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A division under court order is outside rule 1.5.1. Paying a **nonlawyer** for referrals is governed by rules 5.4 and 7.2 (R-PRF-080, R-PRF-086). A lawyer who did not obtain written client consent cannot enforce the split (*Chambers v. Kay*) but may recover in quantum meruit (*Huskinson & Brown v. Wolf*).

**Authority:** CRPC rule 1.5.1; MR 1.5(e); *Chambers v. Kay* (2002) 29 Cal.4th 142; *Huskinson & Brown v. Wolf* (2004) 32 Cal.4th 453.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE (MPRE: proportional or joint responsibility). **CA vs. ABA:** differs — CA allows pure referral fees but requires written disclosure and written client consent and a written lawyer-to-lawyer agreement.

**Example:** August refers a case to Rita for one-third of Rita's contingency fee and does no work. California: proper only with a written August-Rita agreement and the client's written consent after written disclosure of the split. ABA: improper unless August assumes joint responsibility (cf. July 2024 Q3).

## Safekeeping funds and property

### R-PRF-047 — Client trust account; no commingling
**Rule (write this):** All funds received or held for the benefit of a client or other person, including advances for fees, costs and expenses, must be deposited in an identifiable trust account maintained in California (ABA: in the state where the office is), and the lawyer's own funds may not be commingled with them except enough to pay bank charges; funds belonging partly to the lawyer must be deposited in trust and the lawyer's share withdrawn at the earliest reasonable time after it becomes fixed.

**Elements:** 1. Trust account labeled as such. 2. No commingling (personal money in trust; or client money in operating account). 3. Lawyer's portion withdrawn promptly once fixed. 4. Disputed portion stays in trust (R-PRF-049). 5. California flat-fee exception (R-PRF-045). 6. IOLTA for nominal/short-term funds (B&P § 6211).

**Exceptions / defenses:** None for "temporary" use; intent to repay is not a defense. Misappropriation is usually treated as moral turpitude (B&P § 6106). California licensees must also register trust accounts and certify annually under CTAPP [S-123].

**Authority:** CRPC rule 1.15(a)–(c) [S-553]; MR 1.15(a)–(c), (e); B&P §§ 6106, 6211; State Bar rule 2.5 (CTAPP) [S-123].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** same core rules; California account must be in California (unless the client consents in writing to a jurisdiction with a substantial relationship) and adds CTAPP.

**Example:** A lawyer deposits a $100,000 settlement check in her operating account "for a day" to cover payroll. Commingling and misappropriation — violations under both rules regardless of repayment.

### R-PRF-048 — Notice, records, accounting and prompt distribution
**Rule (write this):** A lawyer must notify the client or other person of the receipt of funds or property in which they have an interest (in California, absent good cause, within 14 days), keep complete records, promptly account in writing, preserve records for five years, and promptly distribute undisputed funds; in California there is a rebuttable presumption of a violation if undisputed funds are not distributed within 45 days after they become undisputed.

**Elements:** 1. Notice (CA 14-day standard since Jan. 1, 2023). 2. Records and accounting. 3. Prompt delivery; the CA duty no longer depends on a client request. 4. CA 45-day presumption (rebuttable by good cause; inapplicable if lawyer and client agree in writing to continued holding).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Good cause (e.g., liens being resolved) rebuts the presumption [S-553] [VERIFY V-469].

**Authority:** CRPC rule 1.15(d)–(g) [S-553]; MR 1.15(d).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** differs in detail — California has fixed 14-day notice and 45-day distribution standards; ABA says "promptly".

**Example:** A settlement clears on March 1 and the lawyer tells the client on April 10 and pays her on May 30. California: late notice (over 14 days) and presumptively late distribution (over 45 days); ABA: not "prompt".

### R-PRF-049 — Disputed funds and third-party claims
**Rule (write this):** When the client or a third person disputes the lawyer's entitlement to part of funds held in trust, the disputed portion must stay in the trust account until the dispute is resolved, and the undisputed portion must be distributed promptly; a lawyer must also protect funds subject to a third person's lawful claim (such as a medical lien) of which the lawyer has notice.

**Elements:** 1. Identify disputed vs. undisputed. 2. Hold disputed amount in trust. 3. Pay undisputed amount promptly. 4. Honor valid liens; interplead if necessary.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A lawyer may not unilaterally pay himself a disputed fee from trust.

**Authority:** CRPC rule 1.15(c)(2), (d)(7) [S-553]; MR 1.15(d)–(e) and cmt. [4].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** same.

**Example:** Client disputes $10,000 of a $40,000 fee on a $120,000 recovery. The lawyer pays the client $80,000 at once, keeps $10,000 in trust, and may take the undisputed $30,000.

## Declining and terminating representation

### R-PRF-050 — Mandatory withdrawal (and ABA's duty to inquire)
**Rule (write this):** A lawyer must decline or withdraw if the representation will result in a violation of the rules or the State Bar Act (ABA: or other law), if the lawyer's physical or mental condition renders effective representation unreasonably difficult (ABA: materially impairs), or if the client discharges the lawyer; California also requires withdrawal when the client is pursuing litigation without probable cause to harass or maliciously injure. Since 2023 the ABA also requires a lawyer to inquire into and assess the facts and circumstances of each representation and to decline or withdraw if the client or prospective client seeks to use or persists in using the lawyer's services to commit or further a crime or fraud, despite the lawyer's discussion of the limits on the lawyer's assistance.

**Elements:** 1. Rule violation will result. 2. Impairment. 3. Discharge (client may fire the lawyer at any time). 4. CA: harassing/malicious litigation. 5. ABA: risk-based inquiry duty and crime-fraud withdrawal (MR 1.16(a)(4) and cmts. [1]–[2]) [S-125] [S-126] [S-1263] [S-1264].

**Exceptions / defenses:** If the tribunal's permission is required, the lawyer must continue when ordered (R-PRF-052).

**Authority:** CRPC rule 1.16(a); MR 1.16(a) (as amended Aug. 2023) [S-125] [S-1263]; ABA Formal Op. 513 (2024) [S-126].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE (MPRE: the 2023 inquiry duty). **CA vs. ABA:** differs — CA adds harassing litigation as mandatory; ABA adds the duty to inquire and crime-fraud withdrawal. California has not adopted the ABA inquiry language.

**Example:** A new client wants a series of shell companies formed to move cash with no stated business purpose. ABA: the lawyer must inquire and decline if the client seeks to further a crime. California: decline if the representation would result in assisting a violation of law (rules 1.2.1, 1.16(a)(2)).

### R-PRF-051 — Permissive withdrawal
**Rule (write this):** California: a lawyer may withdraw if, among other listed grounds, the client insists on a claim, defense or position not warranted by existing law or a good-faith argument for changing it; the client seeks to pursue a criminal or fraudulent course or has used the lawyer's services to advance one; the client insists that the lawyer pursue a criminal or fraudulent course; the client's other conduct makes effective representation unreasonably difficult; the client breaches a material term of the agreement or an obligation (including fees) after a reasonable warning; the client knowingly and freely assents; or, in a pending proceeding, the lawyer believes in good faith that the tribunal will find other good cause. The ABA list overlaps (client crime or fraud; failure to fulfil an obligation after warning; unreasonable financial burden or unreasonable difficulty; other good cause) and adds withdrawal that can be accomplished without material adverse effect on the client and the client's insistence on action the lawyer considers repugnant or with which the lawyer fundamentally disagrees.

**Elements:** CA rule 1.16(b)(1)–(10) [VERIFY V-457]; ABA MR 1.16(b)(1)–(7).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Withdrawal still requires court permission where applicable and steps to avoid prejudice.

**Authority:** CRPC rule 1.16(b); MR 1.16(b).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** differs — California has no "no material adverse effect" ground and no "repugnant/fundamental disagreement" ground; it lists more specific grounds (co-counsel conflicts, client assent).

**Example:** A client insists that the lawyer use the opposing party's extramarital affair to pressure settlement. ABA: the lawyer may withdraw if she finds the tactic repugnant. California: no "repugnant" ground — she must fit (b)(4) (unreasonably difficult) or (b)(3) (criminal course, e.g., extortion) (cf. Oct. 2020 Q1).

### R-PRF-052 — Tribunal permission and protecting the client on withdrawal
**Rule (write this):** If a tribunal's rules require permission to withdraw, a lawyer may not withdraw without it and must continue if ordered; on any termination the lawyer must take reasonable steps to avoid reasonably foreseeable prejudice to the client, such as giving reasonable notice, allowing time to retain other counsel, surrendering papers and property, and refunding unearned fees.

**Elements:** 1. Court permission where required (motion to withdraw; do not reveal confidences beyond "professional considerations"). 2. Notice and time. 3. Return file and property. 4. Refund unearned fees/costs.

**Exceptions / defenses:** None; the duties apply whether withdrawal is mandatory, permissive or a discharge.

**Authority:** CRPC rule 1.16(c)–(d); MR 1.16(c)–(d); CCP § 284 (substitution or change of attorney).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** same.

**Example:** A lawyer stops appearing in a pending case after the client misses a payment, without moving to withdraw. Violation — permission was required, and the client was prejudiced.

### R-PRF-053 — Client file and unearned fees on termination
**Rule (write this):** On termination, a California lawyer must promptly release to the client, at the client's request, all client materials and property — correspondence, pleadings, transcripts, expert reports, exhibits and physical evidence, in any form — whether or not the client has paid, and must promptly refund any unearned advance fee or unincurred expense (other than a true retainer). The ABA allows the lawyer to retain papers only to the extent other law permits.

**Elements:** 1. Request. 2. All client materials (CA defines broadly). 3. Regardless of unpaid fees. 4. Refund unearned fees.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Subject to protective orders, nondisclosure agreements and statutes. The lawyer may keep copies at his own expense.

**Authority:** CRPC rule 1.16(e); MR 1.16(d); *Fracasse v. Brent* (1972) 6 Cal.3d 784 (client may discharge at any time; discharged contingency lawyer recovers in quantum meruit only when the contingency occurs).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** differs — no retaining lien in California; ABA permits a retaining lien where state law allows.

**Example:** A discharged lawyer refuses to give the file to new counsel until his $8,000 bill is paid. Violation of rule 1.16(e)(1) in California; under the ABA, possibly allowed if local law recognizes a retaining lien, but never to the client's material prejudice (cf. Feb. 2025 retest Q4).

### R-PRF-054 — Clients with diminished capacity / decision-making limitations
**Rule (write this):** ABA: when a client's capacity to make adequately considered decisions is limited, the lawyer should maintain a normal client-lawyer relationship as far as reasonably possible and may take reasonably necessary protective action, revealing information only to the extent necessary; in February 2026 the ABA replaced Model Rule 1.14 with a rule titled "Clients with Decision-Making Limitations". California has no rule 1.14 (it is "[Reserved]"), so a California lawyer is limited by § 6068(e) and may not reveal confidences to protect the client without consent.

**Elements:** ABA: normal relationship; protective action (consult family, professionals; seek guardian) when the client is at risk of substantial harm and cannot act in own interest; impliedly authorized disclosure. CA: rules 1.4, 1.6, 1.7; State Bar ethics opinions.

**Exceptions / defenses:** The new ABA text (2026) reframes the rule around supported decision-making; its exact wording is not yet in this repo [S-127] [S-554] [VERIFY V-032].

**Authority:** MR 1.14 (2026) [S-127] [S-554]; CRPC rule 1.14 [Reserved] [S-551]; B&P § 6068(e).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MPRE may test the new ABA rule after it is phased in. **CA vs. ABA:** differs — no California counterpart.

**Example:** An elderly client with early dementia wants to give her house to a new "friend". ABA: lawyer may consult her physician or family to the extent necessary. California: without her consent the lawyer may not reveal her information; he may advise candidly and decline to act.

## Organization as client

### R-PRF-055 — Organization as client; reporting up
**Rule (write this):** A lawyer employed or retained by an organization represents the organization acting through its authorized constituents. If the lawyer knows that a constituent is acting, intends to act or refuses to act in a matter related to the representation in a way that violates a legal obligation to the organization or a law reasonably imputable to it, and that is likely to result in substantial injury to the organization, the lawyer must proceed as reasonably necessary in the organization's best lawful interest, which ordinarily means referring the matter to higher authority, up to the highest authority that can act for the organization.

**Elements:** 1. Client = entity (not officers, employees, shareholders). 2. Knowledge of the constituent's misconduct. 3. Violation of duty to the organization or of law imputable to it. 4. Likely substantial injury to the organization. 5. Report up unless reasonably unnecessary. 6. When the organization's interests are adverse to a constituent's, explain whom the lawyer represents (Upjohn-type warning). 7. Dual representation of entity and constituent is allowed subject to rule 1.7, with consent given by an official other than the individual.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A lawyer who reasonably believes he was discharged or withdrew because of reporting must inform the highest authority (CA rule 1.13; MR 1.13(e)) [VERIFY V-453].

**Authority:** CRPC rule 1.13(a)–(b), and its later paragraphs [S-551] [VERIFY V-453]; MR 1.13(a)–(b), (f)–(g); *General Dynamics Corp. v. Superior Court* (1994) 7 Cal.4th 1164 (in-house counsel's retaliatory-discharge claim may proceed if it can be established without breaching privilege).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** same duty to report up.

**Example:** The nonprofit's lawyer learns that its only employee is drafting her own above-market contract and hiding the data source from the board. The board is the client's authorized constituent; the lawyer must tell the board and may not represent the employee (cf. Feb. 2020 Q2).

### R-PRF-056 — Organization as client: reporting out (California vs. ABA)
**Rule (write this):** ABA: if the highest authority insists on or fails to address a clear violation of law that is reasonably certain to cause substantial injury to the organization, the lawyer may reveal information outside the organization to the extent reasonably necessary to prevent that injury, whether or not rule 1.6 permits it (but not if the lawyer was retained to investigate or defend the violation). California: in acting under rule 1.13 the lawyer shall not reveal information protected by § 6068(e); the lawyer's options are to continue to urge the right course and to resign or withdraw.

**Elements:** ABA: report up exhausted → clear violation → reasonably certain substantial injury → limited disclosure outside. CA: no reporting out; only the § 6068(e)(2) death/substantial-bodily-harm exception remains.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Federal law (SEC attorney-conduct rules) may separately permit securities lawyers to disclose; outside the scope of most essays.

**Authority:** MR 1.13(c)–(d); CRPC rule 1.13(c) [S-551]; B&P § 6068(e).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE (MPRE tests reporting out). **CA vs. ABA:** differs sharply — California forbids reporting out.

**Example:** General counsel of a mortgage company reports a loan-fraud scheme to the CEO and board, who do nothing, and then sends documents to the Attorney General. ABA: permitted if the fraud is reasonably certain to injure the company substantially (and possibly under MR 1.6(b)(2)–(3)). California: a breach of § 6068(e) and rule 1.13(c); she should have urged action and withdrawn (cf. July 2023 Q3).

## Counselor

### R-PRF-057 — Advisor: independent judgment and candid advice
**Rule (write this):** In representing a client, a lawyer must exercise independent professional judgment and render candid advice, even if unwelcome; in giving advice the lawyer may refer not only to law but to other considerations such as moral, economic, social and political factors relevant to the client's situation.

**Elements:** 1. Independence (no deference to a payor, employer or the lawyer's own interests). 2. Candor — tell the client what he does not want to hear. 3. Non-legal considerations permitted, not required.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Ordinarily no duty to initiate an investigation of the client's affairs or to give unrequested advice, though it may be given when in the client's interest.

**Authority:** CRPC rule 2.1 and cmts. [1]–[2] [S-558]; MR 2.1.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** same in substance; the "moral, economic, social and political" language is in the ABA rule text and in California's comment.

**Example:** A lawn-care company's lawyer, who personally supports a group campaigning against pesticides, advises the company to say nothing about the lawsuit to regulators even where disclosure is required. Advice skewed by the lawyer's own views fails rule 2.1 and raises a rule 1.7(b) conflict (cf. Feb. 2023 Q4).

## Advocate

### R-PRF-058 — Meritorious claims and contentions
**Rule (write this):** A lawyer may not bring or defend a proceeding, or assert or controvert an issue, without a basis in law and fact that is not frivolous, which includes a good-faith argument for extending, modifying or reversing existing law (ABA); California forbids bringing or continuing an action, defense, position or appeal without probable cause and for the purpose of harassing or maliciously injuring any person, and presenting a claim or defense not warranted by existing law or a good-faith argument for changing it. A criminal defense lawyer may require the prosecution to prove every element.

**Elements:** 1. Legal and factual basis. 2. Good-faith argument for change counts. 3. CA: probable cause + no harassing/malicious purpose. 4. Criminal defense exception.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Procedural sanctions run in parallel (FRCP 11; CCP § 128.7).

**Authority:** CRPC rule 3.1 [S-550]; B&P § 6068(c); MR 3.1; FRCP 11; CCP § 128.7.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** same in substance; CA adds § 6068(c).

**Example:** A partner tells an associate to file a motion to compel trade secrets that both know has no legal basis, to run up the opponent's costs. Frivolous and harassing: rule 3.1 / MR 3.1 and rule 3.2 (cf. July 2019 Q4).

### R-PRF-059 — Delay and expediting litigation
**Rule (write this):** ABA: a lawyer must make reasonable efforts to expedite litigation consistent with the client's interests. California: a lawyer shall not use means that have no substantial purpose other than to delay or prolong the proceeding or to cause needless expense.

**Elements:** 1. Delay or expense. 2. No substantial legitimate purpose (financial benefit to the client from delay is not legitimate — MR 3.2 cmt. [1]).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Legitimate scheduling or strategic continuances.

**Authority:** CRPC rule 3.2; MR 3.2; B&P § 6128(b) (willful delay of a client's suit for the lawyer's own gain is a misdemeanor).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** differs in framing — ABA states an affirmative duty to expedite; CA prohibits dilatory means.

**Example:** Defense counsel seeks a third continuance solely so the defendant can sell assets before judgment. Violates both rules.

### R-PRF-060 — Candor toward the tribunal: false statements, adverse authority, ex parte proceedings
**Rule (write this):** A lawyer may not knowingly make a false statement of fact or law to a tribunal or fail to correct a false statement of material fact or law previously made, must disclose legal authority in the controlling jurisdiction known to be directly adverse to the client's position and not disclosed by opposing counsel, and may not knowingly misquote authority; in an ex parte proceeding the lawyer must disclose all material facts known, adverse or not, that will enable the tribunal to make an informed decision.

**Elements:** 1. Knowing false statement (including fabricated or AI-"hallucinated" citations the lawyer failed to check). 2. Duty to correct. 3. Directly adverse controlling authority. 4. Ex parte: all material facts.

**Exceptions / defenses:** No duty to volunteer adverse **facts** in an adversary proceeding (only adverse controlling **law**).

**Authority:** CRPC rule 3.3(a)(1)–(2), (d); B&P § 6068(d); MR 3.3(a)(1)–(2), (d).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** same; CA adds § 6068(d) (never mislead a judge by artifice or false statement).

**Example:** Plaintiff's lawyer asks a court ex parte for a temporary restraining order without mentioning that the defendant has already cured the breach. Violates rule 3.3(d) / MR 3.3(d).

### R-PRF-061 — False evidence and client perjury
**Rule (write this):** A lawyer may not offer evidence he knows is false. If the lawyer, the client or a witness the lawyer called has offered material evidence and the lawyer later learns it is false, the lawyer must take reasonable remedial measures — first remonstrating with the client, then seeking to withdraw. Under the ABA rule, if necessary the lawyer must disclose to the tribunal even if the information is otherwise confidential; in California, remedial measures stop short of disclosure prohibited by § 6068(e) and rule 1.6. A lawyer may refuse to offer evidence he reasonably believes is false, except a criminal defendant's own testimony.

**Elements:** 1. Knowledge of falsity (actual; may be inferred). 2. Material evidence. 3. Remedial sequence: remonstrate → withdraw → (ABA) disclose. 4. Criminal defendant: right to testify; a California rule 3.3 comment permits the narrative approach (*People v. Johnson*) once the lawyer has made reasonable efforts to dissuade the client and has sought permission to withdraw [S-1265]. 5. Duties last until the proceeding concludes.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A lawyer who merely suspects falsity may present the testimony (resolve doubts in the client's favor).

**Authority:** CRPC rule 3.3(a)(3), (b), (c) and cmt. (narrative approach) [S-550] [S-1265]; MR 3.3(a)(3), (b), (c); *Nix v. Whiteside*, 475 U.S. 157 (1986); *People v. Johnson* (1998) 62 Cal.App.4th 608 [S-557].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE (MPRE: disclose if necessary). **CA vs. ABA:** differs — ABA candor overrides confidentiality; California confidentiality (§ 6068(e)) prevails, so no disclosure to the court.

**Example:** A defense lawyer presents a paid expert whose opinion he knows rests on fabricated test results. He violated rule 3.3(a)(3) / MR 3.3(a)(3) by offering it; having learned of the falsity he must remonstrate and withdraw (ABA: disclose if necessary) (cf. Feb. 2018 Q1; Feb. 2024 Q3).

### R-PRF-062 — Fairness: access to evidence, suppression and falsification
**Rule (write this):** A lawyer may not unlawfully obstruct another party's access to evidence, including a witness, or unlawfully alter, destroy or conceal a document or other material having potential evidentiary value, or counsel or assist anyone to do so; may not suppress evidence the lawyer or client has a legal obligation to reveal or produce; and may not falsify evidence, counsel or assist a witness to testify falsely, or offer an inducement to a witness that the law prohibits.

**Elements:** 1. Evidence with potential evidentiary value (a proceeding need not be pending if reasonably foreseeable). 2. Unlawful obstruction, destruction, concealment. 3. Legal duty to produce (discovery request, subpoena). 4. No false evidence or coaching of false testimony.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Preparing a witness to testify truthfully is proper; good-faith privilege objections are not suppression.

**Authority:** CRPC rule 3.4(a)–(c) [S-550]; MR 3.4(a)–(b); Penal Code §§ 132, 135.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** same in substance; CA adds an express suppression clause (rule 3.4(b)).

**Example:** An associate is told to withhold a damaging engineering memo that is responsive to a document request. Withholding it without a valid objection violates rule 3.4(b) (suppression) and MR 3.4(a), (d) (cf. July 2019 Q4).

### R-PRF-063 — Payments and inducements to witnesses
**Rule (write this):** A lawyer may not pay, offer to pay or acquiesce in paying a witness compensation contingent on the content of the testimony or the outcome of the case; a lawyer may advance or pay a witness's reasonable expenses of attending or testifying, reasonable compensation for lost time, and a reasonable fee for an expert's professional services.

**Elements:** 1. Lay witness: expenses + lost-time compensation only. 2. Expert: reasonable professional fee, never contingent. 3. No payment tied to what the witness says or whether the client wins.

**Exceptions / defenses:** None for contingent payments.

**Authority:** CRPC rule 3.4(d) [S-550] [S-551]; MR 3.4(b) and cmt. [3].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** same; the ABA reaches it through "offer an inducement to a witness that is prohibited by law" and its comment, California through an express rule.

**Example:** Defense counsel offers a fact witness $100 an hour "for his time" — far beyond his wages — and only if he refuses to meet the prosecutor. Excessive compensation operating as an inducement, and conditioned on non-cooperation: violation (cf. Feb. 2024 Q3).

### R-PRF-064 — Making witnesses unavailable; asking others not to cooperate
**Rule (write this):** A lawyer may not advise or cause a person to hide or leave the jurisdiction to make that person unavailable as a witness. ABA also bars asking a person other than a client to refrain from voluntarily giving relevant information to another party, unless the person is the client's relative, employee or agent and the lawyer reasonably believes the person's interests will not be adversely affected; California has no equivalent express rule, so the request is analyzed as unlawful obstruction of access to a witness or conduct prejudicial to the administration of justice.

**Elements:** 1. Secreting or sending away a witness (both). 2. ABA 3.4(f): request to non-client not to cooperate; relative/employee/agent exception. 3. CA: rule 3.4(a) ("unlawfully obstruct … access to … a witness"), rule 8.4(d).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Witnesses have the right to decline an interview; a lawyer may tell a witness that.

**Authority:** CRPC rule 3.4(a), (e) [S-550]; MR 3.4(a), (f); CRPC rule 8.4(d); MR 8.4(d).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** differs — ABA 3.4(f) has no California counterpart.

**Example:** Defense counsel pays a bystander-witness on condition that he refuse to talk to the prosecutor. ABA: violates MR 3.4(f) (and 3.4(b)). California: argue rule 3.4(a) (obstructing access) and 3.4(d); the payment term is the strongest hook (cf. Feb. 2024 Q3).

### R-PRF-065 — Disobeying tribunal rules; discovery abuse
**Rule (write this):** A lawyer may not knowingly disobey an obligation under the rules of a tribunal except by an open refusal based on an assertion that no valid obligation exists; under the ABA rule a lawyer also may not make a frivolous discovery request or fail to make reasonably diligent efforts to comply with a proper discovery request.

**Elements:** 1. Known obligation. 2. Disobedience. 3. Open-refusal safe harbor. 4. ABA discovery clause (California handles discovery abuse through the Civil Discovery Act and rule 3.1/3.2).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Good-faith challenge to an order made openly.

**Authority:** CRPC rule 3.4(f) [S-550]; MR 3.4(c)–(d); B&P § 6103 (willful disobedience of a court order); CCP § 2023.010 (misuses of discovery).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** differs — no express discovery clause in California's rule 3.4.

**Example:** Counsel ignores a court order to produce a witness for deposition without saying why. Violation of rule 3.4(f) / MR 3.4(c) and B&P § 6103.

### R-PRF-066 — Trial conduct: personal knowledge and personal opinion
**Rule (write this):** ABA: in trial a lawyer may not allude to matters not supported by admissible evidence, assert personal knowledge of facts in issue except as a witness, or state a personal opinion as to the justness of a cause, the credibility of a witness, the culpability of a civil litigant, or the guilt or innocence of an accused. California: in trial a lawyer may not assert personal knowledge of facts in issue except when testifying as a witness, or state a personal opinion as to the guilt or innocence of an accused; the rule does not list opinions on the justness of a cause, a witness's credibility, or a civil litigant's culpability.

**Elements:** 1. In trial. 2. Personal knowledge assertions (both). 3. Personal opinion: ABA four categories; CA guilt/innocence only. 4. California case law on prosecutorial argument separately limits vouching.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Argument framed as an inference from the evidence ("the evidence shows Dan is guilty") is not a personal opinion.

**Authority:** CRPC rule 3.4(g) [S-550] [S-1255]; MR 3.4(e).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** differs — California's list is narrower. **Resolves V-058:** California does bar personal opinion on the guilt or innocence of an accused, so a claim that California has no such bar is wrong; it is correct only for credibility, justness of the cause and civil culpability [S-550] [S-1255].

**Example:** In closing, defense counsel says, "I personally believe my client is innocent, and I believe the officer is lying." ABA: both statements violate MR 3.4(e). California: the innocence statement violates rule 3.4(g); the credibility statement is not covered by the rule's text (argue rule 8.4(d) only if prejudicial) (cf. Feb. 2024 Q3).

### R-PRF-067 — Impartiality and decorum: judges, officials and jurors
**Rule (write this):** A lawyer may not seek to influence a judge, juror or official by means prohibited by law, may not communicate ex parte with a judge about the merits of a pending matter except as permitted by law or court order, may not communicate with a juror or prospective juror during the proceeding, and after discharge may not communicate with a juror who has declined contact or by misrepresentation, coercion or harassment; California also bars giving or lending anything of value to a judge or court employee except as the Code of Judicial Ethics allows.

**Elements:** 1. Gifts/loans to judges or staff. 2. Ex parte merits communications (permitted: in open court; with all counsel's consent or presence; in writing copied to all counsel; in ex parte matters). 3. Juror contact rules. 4. ABA 3.5(d): conduct intended to disrupt a tribunal.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Scheduling and administrative communications are not merits communications.

**Authority:** CRPC rule 3.5 [VERIFY V-455]; MR 3.5.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** same core; California's rule is more detailed (gifts, investigations of venire members, reporting juror misconduct).

**Example:** During trial a lawyer emails the judge, without copying opposing counsel, to explain why the defense expert is unreliable. Prohibited ex parte communication under both rules.

### R-PRF-068 — Trial publicity
**Rule (write this):** A lawyer participating in a matter may not make an extrajudicial statement that the lawyer knows or reasonably should know will be publicly disseminated and will have a substantial likelihood of materially prejudicing an adjudicative proceeding in the matter; the lawyer may state the claim, offense or defense involved, information in the public record, that an investigation is in progress, scheduling information, requests for help, warnings of danger, and (in a criminal case) basic facts about the accused and arrest, and may reply to protect a client from recent substantial undue prejudicial publicity not initiated by the lawyer or client.

**Elements:** 1. Extrajudicial statement. 2. Public dissemination. 3. Substantial likelihood of material prejudice (character, credibility, confessions, test results, opinion on guilt, inadmissible evidence — high risk). 4. Safe harbors; right of reply.

**Exceptions / defenses:** First Amendment limits: *Gentile v. State Bar of Nevada*, 501 U.S. 1030 (1991) (substantial-likelihood standard valid; vague safe harbor unconstitutional as applied).

**Authority:** CRPC rule 3.6; MR 3.6; MR 3.8(f) and CRPC rule 3.8 (prosecutors' extrajudicial statements).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE (constitutional overlap with Con Law). **CA vs. ABA:** same.

**Example:** Before jury selection, a defense lawyer tells reporters the victim "has a long history of lying to police". Substantial likelihood of material prejudice — violation of both rules.

### R-PRF-069 — Lawyer as witness
**Rule (write this):** A lawyer may not act as advocate in a trial in which the lawyer is likely to be a witness unless the testimony relates to an uncontested issue, relates to the nature and value of legal services rendered, or (ABA) disqualification would work substantial hardship on the client or (California) the lawyer has obtained the client's informed written consent — for a lawyer representing the People or a governmental entity, consent of the head of the office or a designee; other lawyers in the firm may act as advocate unless barred by the conflict rules.

**Elements:** 1. Advocate at trial (pretrial work usually allowed). 2. Likely necessary witness. 3. Exceptions. 4. Imputation: none, unless rule 1.7/1.9 applies.

**Exceptions / defenses:** California's consent exception is available to a prosecutor or government lawyer, but the consent must come from the head of the office or a designee (rule 3.7(a)(3)). The rule applies to trials (before a jury, judge, administrative law judge or arbitrator), and the court retains discretion to disqualify despite consent (rule 3.7 cmts. [1], [3], citing *Lyle v. Superior Court* (1981)) [S-1253].

**Authority:** CRPC rule 3.7 [S-1253]; MR 3.7.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** differs — California allows client informed written consent; the ABA instead allows a substantial-hardship exception.

**Example:** The lawyer who negotiated a disputed contract is the only witness to an oral side agreement and plans to try the case. ABA: disqualified unless hardship. California: may proceed with the client's informed written consent, subject to the court's discretion.

### R-PRF-070 — Special responsibilities of a prosecutor
**Rule (write this):** A prosecutor must refrain from prosecuting a charge he knows is not supported by probable cause; make reasonable efforts to assure the accused is advised of the right to counsel; not seek waivers of important pretrial rights from an unrepresented accused; timely disclose to the defense all evidence or information known to the prosecutor (California: that he knows or reasonably should know) that tends to negate guilt, mitigate the offense or mitigate the sentence; exercise reasonable care to prevent others from making improper extrajudicial statements; and act on new, credible and material evidence that a convicted defendant is innocent.

**Elements:** 1. Probable cause. 2. Counsel. 3. No waivers from unrepresented accused. 4. Disclosure (broader than *Brady*: no materiality limit; includes sentencing mitigation). 5. Publicity control. 6. Post-conviction duties (CA rule 3.8(f)–(g); MR 3.8(g)–(h)); a good-faith judgment that new evidence does not trigger them, even if later found wrong, is not a violation (CA cmt. [9]) [S-1261].

**Exceptions / defenses:** A protective order may relieve the disclosure duty.

**Authority:** CRPC rule 3.8(d), (f)–(g) and cmt. [9] [S-1261] [VERIFY V-454]; MR 3.8; *Brady v. Maryland*, 373 U.S. 83 (1963); Penal Code § 1054.1.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE (constitutional overlap with Criminal Procedure). **CA vs. ABA:** largely the same; California's disclosure duty adds "reasonably should know".

**Example:** A prosecutor learns that the eyewitness first identified someone else and does not disclose it before a plea. Violates rule 3.8(d) / MR 3.8(d) regardless of whether the evidence is "material" under *Brady*.

### R-PRF-071 — Threatening criminal, administrative or disciplinary charges (California)
**Rule (write this):** A California lawyer may not threaten to present criminal, administrative or disciplinary charges to obtain an advantage in a civil dispute. The ABA has no such rule: a threat is permitted if the criminal matter is related to the civil claim, the lawyer believes both are warranted, and the threat does not amount to extortion or an attempt to improperly influence the criminal process.

**Elements:** 1. Threat (express or implied). 2. Criminal, administrative (licensing or quasi-criminal) or disciplinary (including State Bar) charges. 3. Purpose: advantage in a civil dispute. 4. Actual filing without a threat is not covered.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Administrative charges exclude filings required by law before suing (e.g., an agency complaint as a precondition) [VERIFY V-458]. A threat may also be criminal extortion (Penal Code §§ 518–519; *Flatley v. Mauro* (2006) 39 Cal.4th 299).

**Authority:** CRPC rule 3.10 [VERIFY V-458]; ABA Formal Op. 92-363 (1992); MR 4.4(a), 8.4.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** differs — California-only prohibition.

**Example:** In a harassment suit, plaintiff's lawyer writes, "Settle for $500,000 or I will tell the DA about your client's tax fraud and the State Bar about your conduct." California: violates rule 3.10 (and likely extortion). ABA: permitted only if related, warranted and not extortionate — the unrelated tax-fraud threat fails (cf. Oct. 2020 Q1; Feb. 2021 Q4).

## Transactions with persons other than clients

### R-PRF-072 — Truthfulness in statements to others
**Rule (write this):** In representing a client a lawyer may not knowingly make a false statement of material fact or law to a third person, or fail to disclose a material fact when disclosure is necessary to avoid assisting a client's crime or fraud, unless disclosure is prohibited by the confidentiality rules (in California, § 6068(e)(1) and rule 1.6).

**Elements:** 1. Knowing. 2. Material fact or law. 3. Affirmative misstatement, or half-truth. 4. Negotiation conventions (estimates of price, a party's settlement intentions) are not "material facts".

**Exceptions / defenses:** Puffing is permitted; withdrawal and "noisy withdrawal" (disaffirming documents) may be required to avoid assisting fraud (MR 4.1 cmt. [3]).

**Authority:** CRPC rule 4.1; MR 4.1; B&P §§ 6106, 6128(a) (deceit or collusion with intent to deceive a party).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** same text; result differs because California's confidentiality exceptions are narrower, so California more often forbids disclosure.

**Example:** In settlement talks, defense counsel says, "My client has no insurance," knowing there is a $1 million policy. Material misrepresentation — violation of both rules and B&P § 6106.

### R-PRF-073 — Communicating with a represented person
**Rule (write this):** In representing a client a lawyer may not communicate directly or indirectly about the subject of the representation with a person the lawyer knows is represented by another lawyer in the matter, unless that lawyer consents or the communication is authorized by law or court order. For a represented organization, California covers current officers, directors, partners and managing agents, and any current employee or other constituent when the communication concerns that person's own act or omission in the matter that may be binding on or imputed to the organization; the ABA covers a constituent who supervises, directs or regularly consults with the organization's lawyer about the matter, has authority to obligate the organization in the matter, or whose act or omission in the matter may be imputed to the organization.

**Elements:** 1. Knowledge of representation in the matter. 2. Subject of the representation. 3. Organizational constituents. 4. No consent of counsel (the represented person's own consent is not enough).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Parties may talk to each other directly; a lawyer may advise a client about such talks. Communications with public officials and investigations authorized by law are permitted. Former employees are generally not covered.

**Authority:** CRPC rule 4.2; MR 4.2 and cmt. [7].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** same core; California defines covered constituents by position and by the imputed act or omission, while the ABA comment adds those who supervise, direct or regularly consult with the organization's lawyer or can obligate the organization. The ABA comment's former category of constituents "whose statement may constitute an admission" was deleted in 2002.

**Example:** Plaintiff's lawyer calls the defendant company's plant supervisor whose negligence caused the accident. Prohibited under both rules without company counsel's consent.

### R-PRF-074 — Dealing with unrepresented persons
**Rule (write this):** A lawyer dealing with an unrepresented person on behalf of a client may not state or imply that the lawyer is disinterested, must correct a misunderstanding about the lawyer's role, and may not give legal advice (other than to get counsel) if the person's interests conflict or may conflict with the client's; California also bars seeking privileged or confidential information that the person may not reveal without violating a duty to another.

**Elements:** 1. Unrepresented. 2. No implied neutrality; correct confusion. 3. No advice to a person with adverse interests. 4. CA: no fishing for privileged information.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A lawyer may negotiate terms and explain the client's position.

**Authority:** CRPC rule 4.3(a)–(b); MR 4.3.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** differs — California adds rule 4.3(b).

**Example:** The organization's lawyer interviews an employee, who asks, "Should I get my own lawyer?" The lawyer must explain that he represents the company and may only advise her to get counsel (the corporate "Upjohn warning").

### R-PRF-075 — Inadvertently sent documents and respect for third persons
**Rule (write this):** California: a lawyer who receives a writing relating to the representation that he knows or reasonably should know was inadvertently sent and is privileged or work product must stop examining it beyond what is needed to determine that it is privileged and must promptly notify the sender. ABA: the lawyer must promptly notify the sender; whether to stop reading or return it is left to other law. ABA also bars means that have no substantial purpose other than to embarrass, delay or burden a third person, or methods of obtaining evidence that violate a person's legal rights.

**Elements:** 1. Inadvertent transmission. 2. Obviously privileged/work product. 3. CA: stop reading + notify (rule codifies *Rico*). 4. ABA: notify (MR 4.4(b)); MR 4.4(a) respect for rights of others.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Using the document after notice can lead to disqualification (*Rico*).

**Authority:** CRPC rule 4.4; *Rico v. Mitsubishi Motors Corp.* (2007) 42 Cal.4th 807; *State Comp. Ins. Fund v. WPS, Inc.* (1999) 70 Cal.App.4th 644; MR 4.4(a)–(b); B&P § 6068(f).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** differs — California requires the lawyer to stop reading; ABA requires only notice.

**Example:** Defense counsel's email to her client about trial strategy is accidentally sent to plaintiff's lawyer. California: stop reading once it is apparent it is privileged and notify her. ABA: notify; further handling depends on other law (cf. Feb. 2022 Q4 email exchange).

## Law firms and associations

### R-PRF-076 — Responsibilities of partners, managers and supervisors
**Rule (write this):** Lawyers with managerial authority must make reasonable efforts to ensure the firm has measures giving reasonable assurance that all lawyers comply with the rules; a lawyer with direct supervisory authority must make reasonable efforts to ensure the supervised lawyer complies; and a lawyer is responsible for another lawyer's violation if he orders or knowingly ratifies it, or, as manager or supervisor, knows of it when its consequences can be avoided or mitigated and fails to take reasonable remedial action.

**Elements:** 1. Managerial or direct supervisory authority. 2. Reasonable measures (conflict checks, trust-account controls, training). 3. Vicarious responsibility: order, ratify, or fail to remediate.

**Exceptions / defenses:** No strict liability for subordinates' violations.

**Authority:** CRPC rule 5.1; MR 5.1.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** same.

**Example:** A partner tells an associate to withhold a responsive document. The partner is responsible for ordering the violation (rule 5.1(c)(1)); the associate is not excused (R-PRF-077) (cf. July 2019 Q4).

### R-PRF-077 — Responsibilities of a subordinate lawyer
**Rule (write this):** A lawyer is bound by the rules even when acting at another's direction, but a subordinate lawyer does not violate the rules by acting in accordance with a supervisor's reasonable resolution of an arguable question of professional duty.

**Elements:** 1. Subordinate. 2. Arguable question (genuinely debatable). 3. Supervisor's resolution reasonable. 4. Clear violations are never excused.

**Exceptions / defenses:** "Following orders" is not a defense to a clear violation.

**Authority:** CRPC rule 5.2; MR 5.2.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** same.

**Example:** Whether a document is within the scope of a vague request is arguable; the associate may follow the partner's reasonable call. Hiding a document plainly requested is not arguable — the associate violates the rules if he complies (cf. July 2019 Q4).

### R-PRF-078 — Responsibilities regarding nonlawyer assistants
**Rule (write this):** Lawyers with managerial or supervisory authority over nonlawyers (paralegals, secretaries, investigators, vendors) must make reasonable efforts to ensure the nonlawyers' conduct is compatible with the lawyer's professional obligations, and a lawyer is responsible for a nonlawyer's conduct that would violate the rules if the lawyer orders or ratifies it, or knows of it in time to avoid or mitigate its consequences and fails to act.

**Elements:** 1. Nonlawyer employed, retained or associated. 2. Reasonable efforts (training on confidentiality, supervision of outside vendors and technology). 3. Responsibility for ordered/ratified/unremedied conduct.

**Exceptions / defenses:** As for rule 5.1.

**Authority:** CRPC rule 5.3; MR 5.3.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** same.

**Example:** A shared receptionist (one lawyer's son) discusses callers' legal problems with his friends. The employing lawyer failed to train and supervise him on confidentiality (cf. July 2021 Q2).

### R-PRF-079 — Employing disbarred, suspended, resigned or inactive lawyers (California)
**Rule (write this):** A California lawyer may not employ, associate with or assist a person he knows or reasonably should know is a disbarred, suspended, resigned or involuntarily inactive lawyer to give legal advice to clients, appear for a client in any proceeding or at a deposition, negotiate for a client, handle client funds, or otherwise practice law; such a person may do research, drafting and clerical work, communicate with clients about scheduling and similar matters, and accompany an active lawyer at a deposition only to provide clerical assistance. The lawyer must give written notice to the State Bar and to each affected client before or when the person begins work.

**Elements:** 1. Status of the person. 2. Prohibited tasks. 3. Permitted preparatory/clerical tasks. 4. Written notice to State Bar and clients (and on termination, to the State Bar).

**Exceptions / defenses:** No notice needed if the person only does physical-plant, courier, delivery or similar work [S-563].

**Authority:** CRPC rule 5.3.1 [S-563]; MR 5.5(a) (assisting UPL) and 5.3.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** California-specific detailed rule; ABA covers it only through 5.3 and 5.5(a).

**Example:** A disbarred lawyer drafts deposition questions and passes notes to the examining lawyer during the deposition. Drafting is permitted; acting as more than clerical help at the deposition is not, and the lawyer must have given written notice to the State Bar and the client (cf. Feb. 2019 Q5).

### R-PRF-080 — Fee sharing with nonlawyers; nonlawyer ownership; independence
**Rule (write this):** A lawyer may not share legal fees with a nonlawyer, form a partnership with a nonlawyer if any activity is the practice of law, or practice in a for-profit entity in which a nonlawyer owns an interest, is a director or officer, or controls the lawyer's professional judgment; and may not let a person who recommends, employs or pays the lawyer direct his professional judgment. Exceptions include payments to a deceased lawyer's estate, purchase of a practice, nonlawyer employees' profit-sharing retirement or compensation plans, and sharing court-awarded fees with a nonprofit that employed, retained or recommended the lawyer; California also allows paying a certified lawyer referral service's fees and, under rule 5.4(a)(6), sharing a settlement (non-court-awarded) fee with a qualifying 501(c)(3) nonprofit.

**Elements:** 1. Fee sharing (percentage of fees to a nonlawyer, e.g., a "marketing" firm or chiropractor). 2. Ownership/control. 3. Independence. 4. Exceptions.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Under AB 931, until Jan. 1, 2030 a California lawyer may not share fees with an out-of-state alternative business structure (nonlawyer-owned firm), with exceptions [S-124] [VERIFY V-031]. California rule 5.4(a)(5) covers court-awarded fees shared with a nonprofit that employed, retained, recommended or facilitated the lawyer's employment in the matter; since Mar. 22, 2021, rule 5.4(a)(6) also permits sharing a fee that is not court-awarded but arises from a settlement or other resolution of the matter with a nonprofit that qualifies under IRC § 501(c)(3), under a written fee-division agreement with the nonprofit and the rule's other conditions [S-1258] [S-1259]; whether those conditions include the client's written consent is unconfirmed [VERIFY V-951]. A lawyer sharing fees under (a)(5) or (a)(6) must still exercise independent professional judgment.

**Authority:** CRPC rule 5.4(a)–(d) [S-1258]; MR 5.4(a)–(d); AB 931 (2025) [S-124].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** largely the same; California adds the lawyer-referral-service exception, the settlement-fee nonprofit exception (rule 5.4(a)(6)) and the AB 931 bar; the ABA allows sharing only court-awarded fees with a nonprofit that employed, retained or recommended the lawyer (MR 5.4(a)(4)).

**Example:** A new lawyer pays a chiropractor 10% of each fee from patients he refers. Fee sharing with a nonlawyer (rule 5.4(a)) and paying for recommendations (rule 7.2(b)); possibly also capping (B&P § 6152) (cf. Feb. 2021 Q4).

### R-PRF-081 — Restrictions on a lawyer's right to practice
**Rule (write this):** A lawyer may not participate in offering or making an employment, partnership or similar agreement that restricts a lawyer's right to practice after the relationship ends (except retirement-benefit conditions), or an agreement that restricts a lawyer's right to practice in connection with the settlement of a client controversy. California also forbids participating in an agreement that precludes the reporting of a violation of the rules.

**Elements:** 1. Agreement type. 2. Restriction on future practice. 3. Retirement exception. 4. CA: no agreement precluding reporting of a rule violation (rule 5.6(b)).

**Exceptions / defenses:** California permits a reasonable cost imposed on departing partners who compete (*Howard v. Babcock*) and agreements in the sale of a practice.

**Authority:** CRPC rule 5.6(a)–(b) [S-1254]; MR 5.6(a)–(b); *Howard v. Babcock* (1993) 6 Cal.4th 409.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** largely the same, but California numbers the settlement restriction rule 5.6(a)(2) (ABA: MR 5.6(b)) and adds rule 5.6(b), which bars agreements precluding the reporting of a rule violation (carried over from former rule 1-500(B); no ABA counterpart) [S-1254]; *Howard* is a California gloss.

**Example:** Defendant offers a big settlement if plaintiff's lawyer agrees never to sue Defendant again. Proposing or accepting the term violates rule 5.6(a)(2) / MR 5.6(b).

### R-PRF-082 — Unauthorized practice of law; assisting UPL
**Rule (write this):** No person may practice law in California unless an active licensee of the State Bar, and a lawyer may not practice law in a jurisdiction in violation of its regulations or knowingly assist another person in the unauthorized practice of law. "Practice of law" includes giving legal advice, preparing legal instruments and appearing for others; nonlawyers may not do these for others.

**Elements:** 1. Who is practicing (nonlawyer, disbarred or inactive lawyer, out-of-state lawyer). 2. What they do (advice, document drafting for others, appearances, negotiation). 3. Lawyer's knowing assistance (delegating without supervision, letting a paralegal advise).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Delegation of tasks to supervised nonlawyers is allowed (rule 5.3); self-representation is not UPL.

**Authority:** B&P §§ 6125, 6126; CRPC rule 5.5(a); MR 5.5(a).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** same principle; California adds criminal statutes (B&P § 6126).

**Example:** A real-estate broker who later became a lawyer lets his unlicensed former assistant advise clients on lease terms. Assisting UPL (rule 5.5(a)(2) / MR 5.5(a)) (cf. Feb. 2026 Q3).

### R-PRF-083 — Multijurisdictional practice (out-of-state lawyers in California)
**Rule (write this):** A lawyer not admitted in California may not establish or maintain a resident office or other systematic or continuous presence in California for the practice of law, or hold out as admitted here, except as authorized by the rules or other law. California's authorizations are in the Rules of Court: pro hac vice appearances in a pending case (rule 9.40), registered in-house counsel (9.46), temporary practice as part of litigation (9.47), temporary non-litigation services (9.48), arbitration counsel (9.43), and registered foreign legal consultants (9.44). Practicing without authorization bars recovery of fees for the California work (*Birbrower*). The ABA instead allows temporary practice that is in association with a local lawyer, related to a pending or potential proceeding or ADR, or reasonably related to the lawyer's home-state practice, plus in-house and federally authorized practice.

**Elements:** 1. Admitted elsewhere, not in California. 2. Physical or virtual presence; systematic/continuous? 3. Fits a Rule of Court category? 4. No holding out. 5. Consequences: discipline at home, UPL crime, fee forfeiture.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Rule 9.47 was amended effective Oct. 1, 2025 [S-562] [VERIFY V-463]. ABA Formal Op. 495 (2020) permits remote work for home-state clients while physically in another state if the lawyer does not hold out or establish a local office; California has not adopted that opinion as a rule.

**Authority:** CRPC rule 5.5(b); B&P §§ 6125–6126; Cal. Rules of Court, rules 9.40, 9.43, 9.44, 9.46–9.48 [S-562]; *Birbrower, Montalbano, Condon & Frank, P.C. v. Superior Court* (1998) 17 Cal.4th 119; MR 5.5(c)–(d); ABA Formal Op. 495.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE (MPRE: MR 5.5(c) four safe harbors). **CA vs. ABA:** differs — California uses specific Rules of Court with registration and conditions; the ABA uses broad temporary-practice safe harbors.

**Example:** A Nevada lawyer flies to Los Angeles weekly to negotiate a client's California real-estate deals and keeps a desk at the client's office. Systematic and continuous presence — UPL in California; no fees for that work under *Birbrower* (cf. Feb. 2026 Q3).

## Public service

### R-PRF-084 — Public service: pro bono, appointments, legal services and law-reform organizations
**Rule (write this):** ABA: every lawyer should aspire to render at least 50 hours of pro bono service a year, and a lawyer should not avoid a court appointment except for good cause (violation of law or rules, unreasonable financial burden, or a client or cause so repugnant it would impair the representation). California has no rules 6.1 or 6.2 but imposes a statutory duty never to reject, for personal considerations, the cause of the defenseless or oppressed, and states a policy encouraging pro bono work. Both systems let lawyers serve in legal services and law-reform organizations without creating a conflict, so long as they do not participate in decisions adverse to their own clients, and both allow short-term limited legal services programs with relaxed conflict checking.

**Elements:** 1. Aspirational pro bono (no discipline). 2. Appointments. 3. Membership in legal services organizations (rule 6.3). 4. Law reform (rule 6.4). 5. Limited services programs (rule 6.5).

**Exceptions / defenses:** None; no discipline for failing to do pro bono.

**Authority:** MR 6.1–6.5; CRPC rules 6.1–6.2 [Reserved], 6.3–6.5 [VERIFY V-460]; B&P §§ 6068(h), 6073 [VERIFY V-460].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** differs — no California pro bono or appointment rule; California uses § 6068(h).

**Example:** A court appoints a lawyer to defend an indigent accused of a notorious crime; she asks to be excused because she dislikes the defendant. ABA: personal dislike is not good cause unless it impairs her representation. California: § 6068(h) points the same way.

## Information about legal services

### R-PRF-085 — False or misleading communications
**Rule (write this):** A lawyer may not make a false or misleading communication about the lawyer or the lawyer's services; a communication is misleading if it contains a material misrepresentation of fact or law or omits a fact necessary to make the statement as a whole not materially misleading, and statements that create unjustified expectations (guarantees, predictions of results, unverifiable comparisons, testimonials without disclaimers) are presumptively misleading in California under State Bar standards.

**Elements:** 1. Any communication (websites, social media, letterhead, firm names). 2. Material falsity or misleading omission. 3. CA presumptions (standards adopted by the Board of Trustees).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Truthful statements of past results with appropriate context. Commercial speech protection: *Bates v. State Bar of Arizona*, 433 U.S. 350 (1977).

**Authority:** CRPC rule 7.1 [S-555]; B&P §§ 6157–6159.2; MR 7.1.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** same core rule; California adds Board standards (presumptions) and statutory advertising rules.

**Example:** A website says "We win 98% of our cases — guaranteed results." A guarantee is false or misleading under both rules; California presumes violation.

### R-PRF-086 — Advertising and paying for recommendations
**Rule (write this):** A lawyer may advertise through any written, recorded or electronic medium, but may not compensate, promise or give anything of value to a person for recommending or securing the lawyer's services, except to pay the reasonable cost of permitted advertising, the usual charges of a legal services plan or qualified lawyer referral service, the price of a law practice, and reciprocal referral arrangements that are not exclusive and are disclosed to clients; California also allows a gift to a person who has made a recommendation if it was not given under any promise, agreement or understanding about future referrals, and the ABA allows nominal gifts of appreciation that are neither intended nor expected as compensation. An advertisement must include the name and address of at least one responsible lawyer or firm.

**Elements:** 1. Thing of value. 2. For recommending or securing employment. 3. Exceptions. 4. Runners and cappers are unlawful (B&P § 6152).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Paying a marketing vendor a fixed fee for advertising is allowed; paying per referral is not.

**Authority:** CRPC rule 7.2 [S-555]; MR 7.2(b); B&P §§ 6152, 6155.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** largely the same; the gift exceptions differ in wording (CA: any gift not tied to an understanding about future referrals; ABA: nominal gifts only).

**Example:** A lawyer sends a chiropractor a $500 watch after each referral and tells him "keep them coming". Prohibited under both rules — the gift is tied to future referrals (cf. Feb. 2021 Q4).

### R-PRF-087 — Solicitation
**Rule (write this):** A lawyer may not solicit professional employment by live person-to-person contact — in person, live telephone or real-time electronic contact — when a significant motive is the lawyer's pecuniary gain, unless the person is a lawyer or has a family, close personal or prior professional relationship with the lawyer (the ABA also excepts persons who routinely use the type of legal services offered for business purposes). No solicitation of any kind is allowed if the person has said he does not want to be solicited or it involves coercion, duress, harassment (or, in California, intrusion). California requires the word "Advertisement" on targeted written or electronic solicitations, and since June 1, 2026 bars soliciting a respondent in an ex parte domestic-violence restraining-order proceeding before service, unless a current or former client.

**Elements:** 1. Live contact vs. written/recorded. 2. Pecuniary motive. 3. Exempt relationships. 4. Coercion/known refusal. 5. CA "Advertisement" label. 6. CA DVRO-respondent rule (rule 7.3(f)) [S-120].

**Exceptions / defenses:** Non-pecuniary solicitation by public-interest lawyers is protected (*In re Primus*, 436 U.S. 412 (1978)). Targeted mail is protected speech (*Shapero v. Kentucky Bar Ass'n*, 486 U.S. 466 (1988)), but a 30-day waiting period after accidents was upheld (*Florida Bar v. Went For It, Inc.*, 515 U.S. 618 (1995)). In-person solicitation may be banned (*Ohralik v. Ohio State Bar Ass'n*, 436 U.S. 447 (1978)).

**Authority:** CRPC rule 7.3(a)–(c), (f) [S-120] [S-555] [S-1257]; MR 7.3; B&P § 6152.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** differs — California keeps the "Advertisement" label, has no business-user exception [S-1257], lists "intrusion", and adds the 2026 DVRO rule.

**Example:** A lawyer visits a hospital room to offer representation to an accident victim he has never met. Prohibited live solicitation under both rules; possibly capping if a hospital worker tipped him off for pay.

### R-PRF-088 — Specialization and firm names
**Rule (write this):** A lawyer may state that he practices in particular fields, but may not claim to be a "certified specialist" unless certified by the State Bar's Board of Legal Specialization or an accredited entity (ABA: an organization approved by a state or the ABA) and the certifying body is identified. A lawyer may not use a firm name, trade name or designation that is misleading, implies a connection with a government agency or legal services organization, or implies a partnership that does not exist.

**Elements:** 1. "Certified specialist" claims. 2. Firm names (trade names allowed if not misleading). 3. Holding out as a firm (office-sharers using a joint name).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Patent and admiralty designations are traditional exceptions.

**Authority:** CRPC rules 7.4, 7.5 [S-555]; MR 7.1 cmts., 7.2(c).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** differs in form — California keeps separate rules 7.4 and 7.5; the ABA folded them into 7.1 and 7.2 in 2018.

**Example:** Two solo practitioners sharing a suite put "Smith & Jones, Attorneys" on the door. Misleading implication of a partnership — and their conflicts may be imputed (R-PRF-036) (cf. July 2021 Q2).

## Maintaining the integrity of the profession

### R-PRF-089 — Bar admission and disciplinary matters
**Rule (write this):** An applicant for admission, or a lawyer in connection with an admission application or a disciplinary matter, may not knowingly make a false statement of material fact, fail to disclose a fact necessary to correct a known misapprehension, or knowingly fail to respond to a lawful demand for information from an admissions or disciplinary authority, except that confidential client information need not be disclosed; California licensees must also cooperate and participate in disciplinary investigations.

**Elements:** 1. Admission or disciplinary matter (including statements about another applicant). 2. False statement or misleading omission. 3. Failure to respond. 4. Confidentiality carve-out.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Privilege against self-incrimination may be asserted openly.

**Authority:** CRPC rule 8.1; B&P § 6068(i); MR 8.1.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** same; California adds § 6068(i).

**Example:** A lawyer asked to vouch for a friend's moral character says he has "no knowledge of any dishonesty", knowing the friend was fired for falsifying time records. Violation of rule 8.1 / MR 8.1.

### R-PRF-090 — Statements about judges and legal officials
**Rule (write this):** A lawyer may not make a statement that he knows is false, or with reckless disregard as to its truth or falsity, about the qualifications or integrity of a judge, judicial officer, or candidate for judicial (ABA: or legal) office; a lawyer who is a judicial candidate must comply with the applicable code of judicial conduct.

**Elements:** 1. Statement about a judge/candidate. 2. Knowingly false or reckless (actual-malice standard). 3. Opinion based on disclosed facts is protected.

**Exceptions / defenses:** The Supreme Court in April 2026 denied the State Bar's proposed amendments to rules 8.2 and 8.4 and proposed its own comment language for public comment [S-121].

**Authority:** CRPC rule 8.2 [S-121]; MR 8.2.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** same core.

**Example:** After losing, a lawyer posts that the judge "takes bribes from insurers", with no basis. Reckless disregard — violation of both rules.

### R-PRF-091 — Duty to report another lawyer's misconduct
**Rule (write this):** California (eff. Aug. 1, 2023): a lawyer must, without undue delay, inform the State Bar or a tribunal with jurisdiction when the lawyer knows of **credible evidence** that another lawyer has committed a criminal act or engaged in conduct involving dishonesty, fraud, deceit, or reckless or intentional misrepresentation or misappropriation of funds or property that raises a substantial question as to that lawyer's honesty, trustworthiness or fitness as a lawyer. ABA: a lawyer who **knows** that another lawyer has committed any rule violation raising a substantial question about honesty, trustworthiness or fitness must inform the appropriate professional authority.

**Elements:** CA: 1. Knowledge of credible evidence. 2. Another lawyer. 3. Listed categories (criminal act — as defined by California law; dishonesty; fraud; deceit; reckless/intentional misrepresentation; misappropriation). 4. Substantial question about honesty/fitness. 5. Report to State Bar or tribunal. ABA: knowledge of any violation + substantial question.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Neither requires disclosure of confidential client information or information from a lawyer-assistance program (MR 8.3(c)). California's rule 8.3(d) neither requires nor authorizes disclosure of information protected by § 6068(e), rule 1.6 or rule 1.8.2, information gained in a substance-use or mental-health program (including information confidential under B&P § 6234), or information protected by mediation confidentiality, privilege or other law; and its "criminal act" excludes acts criminal only in another jurisdiction (rule 8.3(c)) [S-118] [S-119] [S-570] [S-1256]. Outside the mandatory duty, a California lawyer may, but need not, report other violations of the rules or the State Bar Act (rule 8.3(b)). Self-reporting is not required by 8.3 (but see R-PRF-094). Threatening to report to gain a civil advantage violates rule 3.10 (R-PRF-071).

**Authority:** CRPC rule 8.3(a)–(d) [S-118] [S-119] [S-570] [S-1256]; MR 8.3(a), (c).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE (MPRE: ABA "knows"). **CA vs. ABA:** differs — California triggers on credible evidence (lower) but only for listed categories; ABA triggers on knowledge but covers any serious violation.

**Example:** Len sees co-counsel Rita take $20,000 of a client's settlement for a "consulting fee" never agreed to. Credible evidence of misappropriation — Len must report to the State Bar (California) and, knowing of the violation, under the ABA rule too, unless the information is confidential to Len's own client (cf. July 2024 Q3).

### R-PRF-092 — Misconduct (catch-all)
**Rule (write this):** It is professional misconduct to violate the rules or knowingly assist or induce another to do so, or do so through another; to commit a criminal act that reflects adversely on honesty, trustworthiness or fitness as a lawyer; to engage in conduct involving dishonesty, fraud, deceit or (California) reckless or intentional misrepresentation; to engage in conduct prejudicial to the administration of justice; to state or imply an ability to influence a government agency or official improperly; or to knowingly assist a judge in violating the judicial code.

**Elements:** (a) violate/assist; (b) criminal act + adverse reflection on fitness; (c) dishonesty; (d) prejudice to justice; (e) improper influence; (f) assisting judicial misconduct.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Not every crime reflects on fitness (a single minor traffic offense does not); private conduct counts if dishonest. Proposed amendments to rule 8.4 were denied in April 2026 [S-121].

**Authority:** CRPC rule 8.4 [S-121]; MR 8.4(a)–(f).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** same structure; California adds "reckless or intentional misrepresentation" to 8.4(c); ABA adds 8.4(g) (R-PRF-095).

**Example:** A lawyer falsely tells a client that a motion was filed when it was not. Dishonesty under rule 8.4(c) / MR 8.4(c), plus rule 1.4 and B&P § 6106.

### R-PRF-093 — Moral turpitude and criminal convictions (State Bar Act)
**Rule (write this):** Any act involving moral turpitude, dishonesty or corruption — whether committed as a lawyer or otherwise, and whether a felony, a misdemeanor or no crime at all — is cause for disbarment or suspension; conviction of a felony or of a misdemeanor involving moral turpitude also triggers summary or conviction-referral discipline, and other convictions may support discipline as "other misconduct warranting discipline".

**Elements:** 1. Act or conviction. 2. Moral turpitude (dishonesty, misappropriation, fraud) — per se or on the facts. 3. If no moral turpitude: other misconduct warranting discipline (e.g., repeated DUIs in violation of probation).

**Exceptions / defenses:** A single DUI conviction does not per se involve moral turpitude (*In re Kelley*).

**Authority:** B&P §§ 6101, 6102, 6106; *In re Kelley* (1990) 52 Cal.3d 487 [S-565]; MR 8.4(b).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** California-specific statutes; ABA uses MR 8.4(b)–(c).

**Example:** In-house counsel pleads guilty to a first misdemeanor DUI. No moral turpitude per se; discipline is unlikely unless there is a pattern or a probation violation, but self-reporting duties must be checked (R-PRF-094) (cf. July 2026 Q1).

### R-PRF-094 — Self-reporting duties (B&P § 6068(o))
**Rule (write this):** A California attorney must report to the State Bar in writing within 30 days of knowing of: (1) three or more lawsuits filed against the attorney in a 12-month period for malpractice or other wrongful conduct in a professional capacity; (2) a civil judgment against the attorney for fraud, misrepresentation, breach of fiduciary duty or gross negligence in a professional capacity; (3) judicial sanctions (except for failure to make discovery or monetary sanctions under $1,000); (4) an indictment or information charging a felony; (5) conviction — including a guilty verdict or a guilty or no-contest plea — of a felony, or of a misdemeanor committed in the course of practice, in which a client was the victim, or a necessary element of which involves improper conduct of an attorney, including dishonesty or other moral turpitude (attempts, conspiracies and solicitations included); (6) discipline by another professional or occupational disciplinary agency or licensing board; or (7) reversal of a judgment based in whole or in part on the attorney's misconduct, grossly incompetent representation or willful misrepresentation.

**Elements:** 1. Listed event. 2. Knowledge. 3. Written report within 30 days. 4. Failure to report is itself a ground for discipline.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A misdemeanor unrelated to practice, with no client victim and no moral-turpitude element (e.g., a simple DUI), is not self-reportable under (o)(5), although courts transmit conviction records to the State Bar [S-568] [S-1250].

**Authority:** B&P § 6068(o)(1)–(7) [S-552] [S-1250] [S-1251].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE; no ABA counterpart. **CA vs. ABA:** California-only duty (ABA rules have no self-reporting rule; some states do by court rule).

**Example:** A former broker turned lawyer is sued for malpractice four times in eighteen months. If three of the suits were filed within any 12-month window, he had to report within 30 days of knowing of the third (cf. Feb. 2026 Q3).

### R-PRF-095 — Discrimination, harassment and retaliation
**Rule (write this):** California: in representing a client, or in terminating or refusing to accept a representation, and in relation to law firm operations, a lawyer may not unlawfully harass or unlawfully discriminate on the basis of protected characteristics, or unlawfully retaliate against a person who opposes or reports such conduct. ABA: it is misconduct to engage in conduct the lawyer knows or reasonably should know is harassment or discrimination on the basis of race, sex, religion, national origin, ethnicity, disability, age, sexual orientation, gender identity, marital status or socioeconomic status in conduct related to the practice of law.

**Elements:** CA: "unlawfully" (tracks anti-discrimination law); representation-related or firm operations; retaliation covered. ABA: knows or reasonably should know; conduct related to the practice of law (including bar activities and social events).

**Exceptions / defenses:** ABA 8.4(g) does not limit accepting, declining or withdrawing from representation under rule 1.16, and legitimate advice or advocacy is protected.

**Authority:** CRPC rule 8.4.1 [S-1256]; rule 1.0.1(c) [S-1260]; MR 8.4(g) [S-082].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** differs — California never adopted MR 8.4(g); its own rule requires "unlawful" conduct and expressly reaches firm operations and retaliation.

**Example:** An in-house lawyer pursues a subordinate analyst romantically after she says no and then downgrades her review. Unlawful harassment and retaliation in the operations of a legal department — rule 8.4.1, because a corporate legal department is a "law firm" under rule 1.0.1(c) [S-1260]; ABA 8.4(g) conduct related to practice (cf. July 2026 Q1).

### R-PRF-096 — Disciplinary authority and choice of law
**Rule (write this):** A lawyer admitted in California is subject to California discipline wherever the conduct occurs, and a lawyer not admitted here is subject to California discipline for providing or offering legal services here. For conduct before a tribunal, the rules of the tribunal's jurisdiction apply; for other conduct, the rules of the jurisdiction where the conduct occurred, or where its predominant effect is, apply.

**Elements:** 1. Who may discipline. 2. Which rules: tribunal's; otherwise conduct/predominant effect. 3. Safe harbor for reasonable belief about predominant effect.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Reciprocal discipline in other states.

**Authority:** CRPC rule 8.5; MR 8.5.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** same.

**Example:** A California lawyer appears pro hac vice in an Arizona court. Arizona's rules govern his conduct there, but California may discipline him for it.

## Civil and other consequences

### R-PRF-097 — Legal malpractice
**Rule (write this):** A client may recover for legal malpractice by proving (1) the lawyer's duty to use the skill, prudence and diligence that members of the profession commonly possess, (2) breach, (3) proximate causation — usually that the client would have obtained a better result but for the negligence (the "case within a case"), and (4) actual damages. A criminal defendant must also prove actual innocence. In California the action must be filed within one year after the client discovers (or should have discovered) the facts, or four years from the wrongful act, whichever is first, subject to tolling.

**Elements:** Duty; breach (expert testimony usually needed; the rules are evidence of the standard of care); causation; damages; (criminal) actual innocence; limitations (CCP § 340.6).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Contributory fault of the client; judgmental immunity for reasonable tactical choices.

**Authority:** *Wiley v. County of San Diego* (1998) 19 Cal.4th 532; CCP § 340.6; CRPC rule 1.0 cmt.; MR Scope [20].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE; occasionally in a PR + Torts crossover or when a call asks about "liability". **CA vs. ABA:** tort law, not a Model Rule.

**Example:** A lawyer misses the deadline to file a client's car-accident suit. The client must show she would have recovered (and collected) a judgment but for the lapse.

### R-PRF-098 — Breach of fiduciary duty and fee forfeiture
**Rule (write this):** A lawyer is a fiduciary of the client and owes duties of loyalty, confidentiality, candor and care; breach (for example, an undisclosed conflict or self-dealing) supports damages and may also lead to forfeiture or disgorgement of fees, and a fee agreement made in violation of the conflict rules may be unenforceable, leaving at most a quantum meruit recovery.

**Elements:** 1. Fiduciary relationship. 2. Breach (disloyalty, nondisclosure, self-dealing). 3. Causation and damages, or equitable forfeiture without proof of damage in egregious cases.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Quantum meruit may be denied where the violation is egregious.

**Authority:** *Sheppard, Mullin, Richter & Hampton, LLP v. J-M Manufacturing Co.* (2018) 6 Cal.5th 59; *Huskinson & Brown v. Wolf* (2004) 32 Cal.4th 453; Restatement (Third) of the Law Governing Lawyers § 37.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** court law.

**Example:** A firm hides a conflict and bills $1 million. Under *Sheppard Mullin*, its engagement agreement is unenforceable and it may recover, if at all, only the reasonable value of its services.

### R-PRF-099 — Fee disputes, fee arbitration and the discharged lawyer
**Rule (write this):** In California, before or when suing a client for fees, a lawyer must give notice of the client's right to arbitrate the fee dispute under the Mandatory Fee Arbitration Act, and the client may elect nonbinding arbitration; a client may discharge a lawyer at any time, and a discharged contingency-fee lawyer may recover only the reasonable value of services (quantum meruit), payable if and when the contingency occurs.

**Elements:** 1. MFAA notice (B&P § 6201(a)). 2. Client election; nonbinding unless agreed after the dispute arises. 3. Discharge: quantum meruit capped by the contract fee. 4. Lawyer may not hold the file hostage (R-PRF-053).

**Exceptions / defenses:** MFAA does not apply to claims for affirmative malpractice relief.

**Authority:** B&P §§ 6200–6206; *Fracasse v. Brent* (1972) 6 Cal.3d 784; CRPC rule 1.16(e).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** California statute; ABA recommends fee arbitration in MR 1.5 cmt. [9].

**Example:** A client fires her contingency lawyer midway; new counsel later wins $300,000. The first lawyer recovers the reasonable value of his services out of the recovery, not his full percentage.

### R-PRF-100 — Civility and the attorney's oath
**Rule (write this):** California attorneys take an oath that includes a pledge to conduct themselves with dignity, courtesy and integrity, and since Oct. 1, 2025 must annually reaffirm the full oath; the State Bar Act separately requires maintaining the respect due to the courts and advancing no fact prejudicial to the honor or reputation of a party or witness unless the justice of the cause requires it (do not rely on the old "offensive personality" wording) [S-1252] [VERIFY V-950], while the Supreme Court has declined to make incivility, as such, a separate ground for discipline.

**Elements:** 1. Oath (B&P § 6067; Cal. Rules of Court, rule 9.4). 2. Annual reaffirmation (rule 9.7, as amended eff. Oct. 1, 2025) [S-561]. 3. § 6068(b), (f).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Uncivil conduct becomes disciplinable when it violates a specific rule (rule 3.4, 4.4, 8.4(d)) or statute.

**Authority:** B&P §§ 6067, 6068(b), (f); Cal. Rules of Court, rules 9.4, 9.7 [S-561].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. **CA vs. ABA:** California-specific; the ABA has no oath rule (MR Preamble refers to professionalism).

**Example:** Opposing counsel sends insulting, profane emails to a self-represented litigant. Mention the civility oath, then anchor the violation in a rule: MR 4.4(a) / rule 8.4(d), B&P § 6068(f).
