# Professional Responsibility — worked examples

All fact patterns are original. Each answer gives the rule (with its ID in [`rule-statements.md`](rule-statements.md)), the application, and a conclusion under **both** the ABA and California rules.

---

**1. The paying parent.** Mira represents Dana in a custody dispute. Dana's father pays the $25,000 retainer and emails Mira: "Send me copies of everything Dana tells you." Dana signs nothing about the payment.
- **Rule:** third-party payment requires no interference, protection of confidences, and client consent — informed consent (ABA) / informed written consent (CA) (R-PRF-029); confidentiality (R-PRF-013).
- **Application:** Mira took the father's money without any consent from Dana, let alone written consent. Sending Dana's statements to the father would breach § 6068(e)(1) and both versions of rule 1.6 unless Dana gives informed consent.
- **Conclusion:** violation of rule 1.8.6 (no written consent) and of MR 1.8(f) if Dana was not informed. Mira must refuse the father's request absent Dana's consent.

**2. The lawyer's new romance.** Theo, divorce counsel for Lena, starts a sexual relationship with her two months into the case.
- **Rule:** no sexual relations with a current client unless a consensual relationship pre-dated the representation (R-PRF-032); personal-interest conflict (R-PRF-023).
- **Application:** the relationship began during the representation, and the divorce is emotionally charged, so there is a significant risk Theo's judgment is affected.
- **Conclusion:** violates MR 1.8(j) and rule 1.8.10. Under California law, B&P § 6106.9 is also implicated if the relationship makes his work incompetent. It is a 1.7(b) conflict under both systems.

**3. Rent loan.** After being retained in a slip-and-fall case, Pia lends her client $2,500 for rent; the client signs a promissory note. Pia gave no written disclosures.
- **Rule:** ABA allows only advances of litigation costs (and humanitarian gifts in pro bono cases); California allows post-retention loans on a written promise to repay **after** complying with rules 1.7(b)–(c) and 1.8.1 (R-PRF-028).
- **Application:** the note satisfies the written promise, but Pia skipped the 1.8.1 written disclosure, the advice to consult independent counsel, and the client's informed written consent.
- **Conclusion:** violates MR 1.8(e) outright. In California, the loan type is allowed but violates rule 1.8.5(b)(2) because the conflict steps were skipped.

**4. Second chair moves firms.** Associate Ray was second chair defending Acme in *Baker v. Acme*. He joins Firm B, which represents Baker in the same case. Firm B screens Ray on day one and notifies Acme in writing.
- **Rule:** former-client conflicts are imputed; screening cures the conflict under the ABA, but in California only if the lateral did not substantially participate (R-PRF-034, R-PRF-037).
- **Application:** Ray substantially participated as second chair.
- **Conclusion:** ABA: the screen, the written notice and certifications let Firm B continue. California: screening is unavailable; Firm B is disqualified unless Acme gives informed written consent.

**5. The ex on the other side.** Ana represents a plaintiff; defense counsel is her former boyfriend of three years. They broke up last year. Ana tells her client nothing.
- **Rule:** CA rule 1.7(c)(2) requires written disclosure if opposing counsel has an intimate personal relationship with the lawyer; under either system a significant risk of material limitation requires consent (R-PRF-022, R-PRF-023).
- **Application:** the relationship is over, so (c)(2)'s present-tense category may not apply. But lingering feelings or animosity could create a significant risk, and (c)(1)'s "personal relationship with a party or witness" does not fit.
- **Conclusion:** the safer answer is that California likely required written disclosure, and both systems require consent if Ana's judgment is at risk. Argue both sides and conclude. Silence was likely a violation of rule 1.4 in any event.

**6. Client's past fraud.** A client tells his California lawyer that last year he used the lawyer's escrow documents to defraud a lender of $400,000.
- **Rule:** ABA permits disclosure to rectify substantial financial injury caused by a client fraud that used the lawyer's services; California permits disclosure only to prevent a future criminal act likely to cause death or substantial bodily harm (R-PRF-014, R-PRF-015).
- **Application:** the fraud is past and the harm is financial.
- **Conclusion:** ABA: may disclose under MR 1.6(b)(3). California: may not; the lawyer may withdraw and must not assist further.

**7. The threat in the demand letter.** In a wrongful-termination suit, Ed writes: "Pay $300,000 or I will report your company's OSHA violations to Cal/OSHA and the DA."
- **Rule:** California rule 3.10 forbids threatening criminal or administrative charges to gain advantage in a civil dispute; the ABA has no rule but bars extortionate threats (R-PRF-071).
- **Application:** an agency complaint and a criminal referral are both threatened, as leverage for money.
- **Conclusion:** violates California rule 3.10, and possibly the extortion statutes (*Flatley*). ABA: arguably permitted under Formal Op. 92-363 if the violations relate to the termination claim and are well-founded. Still improper if the threat amounts to extortion.

**8. Season tickets.** A criminal defense lawyer agrees to defend an athlete for $20,000 plus season tickets "if I get you acquitted".
- **Rule:** no contingent fee in a criminal case (R-PRF-044).
- **Application:** the tickets depend on the outcome; a non-cash contingent payment is still contingent.
- **Conclusion:** violates MR 1.5(d)(2) and rule 1.5(c)(2). If the agreement exceeds $1,000 and is not in writing, it also violates § 6148.

**9. Paying the eyewitness.** Defense counsel offers a bystander $150 an hour (he earns $25) to "be available", provided he does not speak with the prosecutor.
- **Rule:** a lay witness may receive expenses and reasonable compensation for lost time only. The ABA bars asking non-clients to refrain from giving information (R-PRF-063, R-PRF-064).
- **Application:** the payment is six times his wage — an inducement — and is conditioned on non-cooperation.
- **Conclusion:** ABA: violates MR 3.4(b) and (f). California: violates rule 3.4(d), because the payment is unreasonable and effectively contingent on content. Rule 3.4(a) (obstructing access to a witness) also applies; there is no direct 3.4(f) analogue.

**10. Closing argument.** In closing, a defense lawyer says: "I believe the officer lied, and I know my client is innocent."
- **Rule:** ABA bars personal opinion on credibility and guilt and assertions of personal knowledge; California rule 3.4(g) bars personal knowledge and opinion on guilt or innocence only (R-PRF-066).
- **Application:** "I believe the officer lied" is an opinion on credibility. "I know my client is innocent" is an opinion on innocence and implies personal knowledge.
- **Conclusion:** ABA: both statements violate MR 3.4(e). California: the innocence statement violates rule 3.4(g); the credibility statement is outside the rule's text.

**11. Referral fee.** Rosa refers a personal-injury client to Lee for 25% of Lee's fee and does no work. Lee and Rosa shake hands; the client is told orally.
- **Rule:** ABA: proportion or joint responsibility + client agreement confirmed in writing. California: written lawyer agreement + client written consent after full written disclosure + no increase in total (R-PRF-046).
- **Application:** Rosa did no work and took no joint responsibility. There is no writing between the lawyers and no written client consent.
- **Conclusion:** violates MR 1.5(e). It also violates rule 1.5.1, which would have permitted the pure referral fee if the writings existed. Rosa cannot enforce the split (*Chambers v. Kay*).

**12. Settlement check.** A $90,000 settlement clears on May 1. The lawyer tells the client on June 2 and pays her share on July 20; a $10,000 medical lien is disputed.
- **Rule:** California: notice within 14 days; presumption of violation if undisputed funds are not distributed within 45 days of becoming undisputed; disputed amounts stay in trust (R-PRF-048, R-PRF-049).
- **Application:** notice took 32 days. Payment of the undisputed share took about 80 days, with no good cause shown.
- **Conclusion:** California: violates rule 1.15(d)(1) and is presumptively late under the 45-day rule. The $10,000 should stay in trust. ABA: not "prompt" under MR 1.15(d).

**13. Unpaid bill, held file.** A client fires her lawyer, who refuses to send the file to new counsel until his $6,000 invoice is paid.
- **Rule:** California requires release of all client materials on request regardless of payment; ABA allows retention only as other law permits (R-PRF-053).
- **Conclusion:** California: clear violation of rule 1.16(e)(1). ABA: depends on whether local law recognizes a retaining lien, and the lawyer still must avoid prejudice to the client (MR 1.16(d)).

**14. General counsel and the Attorney General.** GC learns the CFO is falsifying loan files, which will expose the company to massive fines. The board refuses to act. GC sends the files to the Attorney General.
- **Rule:** report up under both systems; report out allowed under the ABA, forbidden in California (R-PRF-055, R-PRF-056).
- **Conclusion:** ABA: permissible if the violation is clear and substantial injury to the company is reasonably certain (MR 1.13(c)). California: violates rule 1.13(c) and § 6068(e). GC's proper course was to keep urging the board and to resign.

**15. Partner's orders.** A partner tells associate Kim to withhold a responsive test report: "We'll call it non-responsive."
- **Rule:** no suppression of evidence; a subordinate is excused only for a supervisor's reasonable resolution of an arguable question (R-PRF-062, R-PRF-076, R-PRF-077).
- **Application:** the report is plainly responsive, so the question is not arguable.
- **Conclusion:** if Kim complies, both violate rule 3.4 / MR 3.4. The partner is also responsible under rule 5.1(c) for ordering it.

**16. The disbarred "consultant".** Lawyer Jo employs a disbarred lawyer, Cal, who drafts deposition outlines and sits beside Jo passing notes during a deposition. Jo told neither the State Bar nor the client.
- **Rule:** California rule 5.3.1 permits drafting and clerical help at depositions, prohibits acting as the client's representative, and requires written notice to the State Bar and the client (R-PRF-079).
- **Application:** drafting outlines is permitted. Passing substantive notes is arguably more than clerical help. No notice was given.
- **Conclusion:** California: violation (no notice; likely beyond clerical help). ABA: possibly assisting UPL under MR 5.5(a), and a failure to supervise under MR 5.3.

**17. Nevada lawyer in Los Angeles.** A Nevada-only lawyer rents a Los Angeles office, advises California clients on California leases, and lists "Los Angeles" on her letterhead.
- **Rule:** no systematic and continuous presence or holding out in California without authorization (R-PRF-083).
- **Conclusion:** UPL under B&P § 6125 and rule 5.5(b). No Rule of Court applies. Under *Birbrower* she cannot collect fees for that work. Under ABA MR 5.5(b) she also violates the rule because she established an office.

**18. Malpractice suits.** Lawyer Rex was served with malpractice complaints in January, March, August and the following April, and reported none.
- **Rule:** B&P § 6068(o)(1): report within 30 days of knowledge of three or more such suits filed in a 12-month period (R-PRF-094).
- **Application:** January, March and August fall within 12 months.
- **Conclusion:** Rex had to report within 30 days of learning of the August suit; his failure is a separate disciplinary violation. There is no ABA counterpart.

**19. Seeing a colleague's theft.** Co-counsel Len sees co-counsel Rita move $15,000 of a client's settlement into her personal account.
- **Rule:** California rule 8.3 requires a report on credible evidence of misappropriation raising a substantial question about honesty; ABA MR 8.3 requires a report when the lawyer knows of such a violation (R-PRF-091).
- **Conclusion:** Len must report to the State Bar under both rules, unless the information is confidential to Len's own client. If Len and Rita share the client, Len must also tell the client (rule 1.4).

**20. Pre-filing press conference.** A prosecutor announces at a press conference that the defendant "failed a polygraph and is obviously guilty".
- **Rule:** trial publicity rules — polygraph results and opinions on guilt are high-risk statements; prosecutors have special duties (R-PRF-068, R-PRF-070).
- **Conclusion:** violates rule 3.6 / MR 3.6 and the prosecutor's extrajudicial-statement duties under both systems.

**21. Inadvertent email.** Plaintiff's lawyer receives defense counsel's privileged strategy memo by mistake and reads all of it before telling anyone.
- **Rule:** California: stop reading once it is apparent the document is privileged, and notify the sender (*Rico*). ABA: notify the sender (R-PRF-075).
- **Conclusion:** California: violation of rule 4.4, and the lawyer risks disqualification. ABA: no violation if she promptly notified the sender.

**22. The specialist billboard.** A billboard reads "Certified Injury Specialist — We Never Lose." The lawyer holds no certification.
- **Rule:** no false or misleading communications; "certified specialist" requires recognized certification (R-PRF-085, R-PRF-088).
- **Conclusion:** violates rules 7.1 and 7.4 and MR 7.1–7.2(c). In California, the guarantee language is presumptively misleading.

**23. Shared office, shared printer.** Two solo lawyers share a suite, a receptionist (one lawyer's son) and a printer, and answer the phone "Law Offices". A family-law client's tax return is left on the printer.
- **Rule:** confidentiality safeguards; supervision of nonlawyers; holding out as a firm creates imputation (R-PRF-013, R-PRF-036, R-PRF-078, R-PRF-088).
- **Conclusion:** both lawyers likely failed reasonable precautions (MR 1.6(c); rule 1.6). The son needed training (rule 5.3). The joint presentation risks being treated as one firm, with conflicts imputed, and is misleading under rule 7.5 / MR 7.1.

**24. Refund for release.** A lawyer who took a $40,000 flat fee and did little offers to refund $10,000 if the client signs a general release and agrees not to complain to the State Bar.
- **Rule:** settling malpractice exposure requires written advice to seek independent counsel; California forbids agreements not to report to the State Bar (R-PRF-031); unearned fees must be refunded (R-PRF-045).
- **Conclusion:** violates MR 1.8(h)(2) and rule 1.8.8(b) unless the written advice and opportunity were given. The no-complaint term violates B&P § 6090.5. Withholding the unearned remainder violates rule 1.16(e)(2) / MR 1.16(d).
