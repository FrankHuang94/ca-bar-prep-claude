# Professional Responsibility — California vs. ABA

**Why this file is the core of the subject.** Every recent PR call says "Answer according to California and ABA authorities" (`06-past-exams/index.csv`). Graders reward an answer that, for each duty, states the ABA rule, states the California rule, and says whether the result differs (`00-exam-intel/grader-expectations.md` §5). Where the two are the same, one sentence ("California's rule is the same") earns the point; where they differ, the difference is usually the point being tested.

**Decision rule for the essay.**
1. Answer **lawyer by lawyer**, then **duty by duty** (loyalty, confidentiality, competence, communication, fees, candor/fairness, withdrawal, supervision, advertising).
2. Under each duty: **ABA rule → California rule → application → conclusion under each** where they differ.
3. Add the State Bar Act section (B&P) when one exists — it is California law the ABA lacks.
4. Do not over-claim: if the two rules are the same, say so in one line and move on.

The column headed "ABA / majority / MPRE rule" is the rule the MPRE tests. Rule IDs point to [`rule-statements.md`](rule-statements.md).

**Essay sentence for confidentiality:** "Under Business and Professions Code § 6068(e)(1) and rule 1.6, a California lawyer must maintain inviolate the client's confidences and, at every peril to himself, preserve the client's secrets; the only exception is to prevent a criminal act likely to result in death or substantial bodily harm (§ 6068(e)(2))."

## Confidentiality in California: one exception only
- The statute's language ("at every peril to himself or herself") is why California refuses the ABA's financial-fraud, self-defense-by-rule, organizational reporting-out and candor-overrides-confidentiality exceptions.
- **Practical consequences on essays:**
  - **Client fraud using the lawyer's services:** ABA — disclose (MR 1.6(b)(2)–(3)) or "noisy withdrawal". California — withdraw (rule 1.16(b)(2)), and disaffirm only work product that would otherwise assist the fraud, without revealing confidences.
  - **Client perjury:** ABA — disclose to the court as a last resort (MR 3.3(c)). California — no disclosure; withdraw if possible. Criminal defendant: narrative testimony.
  - **Corporate wrongdoing:** ABA — report out (MR 1.13(c)). California — report up only (rule 1.13(c)).
- Confidentiality differs from privilege; see outline [I.D](outline.md#id-duty-of-confidentiality-vs-attorney-client-privilege-and-work-product-exceptions).

## Conflicts: California's stricter formalities
- **Consent:** California requires informed written consent for rules 1.7, 1.9, 1.8.1, 1.8.6, 1.8.7, 1.11 and 1.18 (rule 1.0.1(e-1)).
- **Written disclosure:** rule 1.7(c) adds disclosure of relationships with parties, witnesses and opposing counsel even when there is no significant risk.
- **Screening:** under rule 1.10(a)(2) screening is unavailable if the lateral substantially participated.
- **Liens:** under *Fletcher v. Davis* a charging lien triggers rule 1.8.1.
- **Loans:** under rule 1.8.5 post-retention loans are allowed, and rules 1.7 and 1.8.1 must be satisfied first.
- **Malpractice limits:** under rule 1.8.8(a) prospective liability limits are never allowed.

## Duty to report misconduct: the 2023 California rule
California had no reporting duty until rule 8.3 took effect on **Aug. 1, 2023** [S-118] [S-119]. Older selected answers (2021 and before) therefore say "California has no duty to report". For July 2027 that statement is **wrong**: apply rule 8.3 (R-PRF-091). The State Bar publishes an FAQ and scenarios [S-570].

## Areas where California and the ABA are the same (one-line comparison)
For these, write: "California's rule is the same."
- Allocation of authority (1.2(a)).
- Prohibited contingent fees (1.5(c) / 1.5(d)).
- Aggregate settlements (1.8.7).
- Settling malpractice claims (1.8.8(b) / 1.8(h)(2)).
- Former-client conflicts (1.9).
- Government lawyers (1.11).
- Organization reports up (1.13(b)).
- Meritorious claims (3.1, plus § 6068(c)).
- Candor about law and ex parte disclosure (3.3(a)(1)–(2), (d)).
- Trial publicity (3.6).
- Truthfulness to others (4.1).
- Represented persons (4.2) — same core; for an organization, compare California's rule 4.2(b) with MR 4.2 cmt. [7] (R-PRF-073).
- Supervisory and subordinate lawyers (5.1–5.3).
- Restrictions on practice (5.6) — same core; California numbers the settlement restriction 5.6(a)(2) and adds 5.6(b) (no agreement precluding the reporting of a rule violation) (R-PRF-081).
- False and misleading advertising (7.1).
- Bar admission (8.1).
- Statements about judges (8.2).
- Choice of law (8.5).

## Writing the comparison on the essay (templates)
- **Same:** "Under ABA Model Rule 1.9(a), … California rule 1.9(a) is the same, except that consent must be informed written consent. Here …, so Lou violated both."
- **Different:** "Under the ABA, Laura may reveal … to prevent substantial financial injury (MR 1.6(b)(2)). California, however, permits disclosure only to prevent a criminal act likely to cause death or substantial bodily harm (B&P § 6068(e)(2); rule 1.6(b)). Because the threatened harm is purely financial, Laura's disclosure is permitted under the ABA but violates California law."
- **California-only:** "Although the ABA has no comparable rule, California rule 3.10 forbids threatening criminal charges to gain an advantage in a civil dispute. Lawyer's letter … violates rule 3.10."
- **ABA-only:** "ABA Model Rule 3.4(f) forbids asking a non-client to refrain from giving information to another party; California has no counterpart, but the payment conditioned on non-cooperation still violates rule 3.4(d)."
