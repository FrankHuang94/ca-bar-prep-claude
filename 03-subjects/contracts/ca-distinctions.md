# Contracts — California distinctions

## How California essays use these distinctions
- **Default rule.** California Contracts essays are graded on **general principles**: the common law as reflected in the Restatement (Second), plus UCC Article 2. California law applies only if the call asks for it [S-077].
  - In the past essays indexed in Phase 3, no Contracts call was seen to ask for California law. Several calls were not captured, though [VERIFY V-071].
- **What to do.**
  1. Write the general rule first.
  2. Where California differs **and the point matters to the facts**, add one sentence: "In California, …".
  3. Never replace the general rule with the California rule unless the call says "under California law."
- **Commercial Code.** The California Commercial Code mirrors UCC Article 2 section by section (UCC § 2-207 = Com. Code § 2207). Citing the UCC section is enough.
- **Recent California Supreme Court cases that touch contracts** (from [`../../00-exam-intel/recent-changes.md`](../../00-exam-intel/recent-changes.md)):
  - *Rattagan v. Uber Technologies* (2024): fraud during performance [S-132];
  - *Quach v. California Commerce Club* (2024): arbitration waiver without prejudice [S-131].
  - Both appear below.

## Contract defenses (statute of frauds, parol evidence, mistake, fraud) — California points
These are the defenses most often paired on California essays (Feb 2026 Q2 tested mistake, the statute of frauds and parol evidence together) [VERIFY V-071]. The California additions are:
1. **Statute of frauds.**
   - Civil Code § 1624 is California's list; Commercial Code § 2201 for goods is the same as UCC § 2-201 [S-202] [S-211].
   - *Sterling v. Taylor* lets extrinsic evidence clarify, but not supply, essential terms in a memorandum [S-204].
   - Part performance is framed as estoppel (*Monarco*) [S-1358].
2. **Parol evidence.**
   - CCP § 1856 codifies the rule.
   - *Masterson* admits collateral agreements that would not naturally be included.
   - *PG&E* admits evidence to show the language is reasonably susceptible to another meaning.
   - *Riverisland* admits fraud evidence even if it contradicts the writing [S-214].
3. **Mistake.** *Donovan* adopts R2d § 153 for unilateral mistake [S-205]. Civil Code § 1689(b) lists mistake as a ground for rescission. Section 1691 requires prompt notice and an offer to restore.
4. **Fraud.**
   - Civil Code § 1572 (actual fraud includes suppression of known facts).
   - A real-property seller's duty to disclose (*Lingsch*) [S-1359].
   - Tort damages, including punitive damages under § 3294, are available on proof of fraud.

## Statute of frauds (Civ. Code § 1624)
- **Confirmed categories** [S-202] [S-1350]:
  - (a)(1) not to be performed within a year;
  - (a)(2) special promise to answer for another's debt;
  - (a)(3) a lease for longer than one year, or a sale of real property or an interest in it;
  - (a)(4) real-estate agent or broker employment for compensation;
  - (a)(5) not to be performed during the promisor's lifetime;
  - (a)(6) an agreement by a purchaser of real property to pay an indebtedness secured by a mortgage or deed of trust on the property purchased, unless the conveyance specifically provides for the purchaser's assumption of the debt [S-1834];
  - (a)(7) a commitment to lend money or extend credit of more than $100,000, not primarily for personal, family or household purposes, by a person in the business of lending (a loan secured solely by a one-to-four-unit residence is deemed personal).
- **Not yet confirmed:** whether any marriage category remains anywhere in California's statute of frauds; none of (a)(1)–(7) concerns marriage (premarital agreements are covered by Fam. Code § 1611) [VERIFY V-100].
- **Essay use.**
  - The general MY LEGS analysis is enough for most essays.
  - The broker category matters when a real-estate agent sues on an oral commission promise.
  - The broker category does not cover agents selling goods, such as a car dealer's agent on commission; those fall under the general rules [S-202].

## Parol evidence in California
- **Code.** CCP § 1856 is the general rule; Com. Code § 2202 applies to goods.
- **Integration.** *Masterson v. Sine* (1968): even a writing that looks complete does not bar a collateral oral agreement that would not naturally be included, judged by the circumstances.
- **Interpretation.** *PG&E* (1968): the question is not whether the writing looks clear to the judge, but whether the offered evidence is relevant to prove a meaning to which the language is reasonably susceptible. The judge provisionally receives the evidence to decide that.
- **Fraud.** *Riverisland* (2013) overruled *Pendergrass* (1935). Evidence of fraud is admissible even if it shows a promise or representation directly at odds with the writing [S-214].
- **Essay sentence:** "California applies the parol evidence rule liberally: extrinsic evidence is admissible to show that the contract is reasonably susceptible to the meaning [party] urges (*PG&E*), and evidence of fraud is admissible even though it contradicts the writing (*Riverisland*)."

## Liquidated damages (Civ. Code § 1671)
- **§ 1671(b):** in contracts not covered by (c), a liquidated-damages provision is **valid unless the party seeking to invalidate it proves it was unreasonable under the circumstances existing when the contract was made** [S-200].
- **§ 1671(c)–(d):** when liquidated damages are sought from a consumer (goods or services bought or rented primarily for personal, family or household purposes) or from a residential tenant, a clause is void unless it would be impracticable or extremely difficult to fix actual damages [S-200].
- **Residential purchase deposits (Civ. Code § 1675):** if the amount actually paid does not exceed **3% of the purchase price**, the provision is valid unless the buyer proves it unreasonable; above 3%, it is invalid unless the party seeking to uphold it proves it reasonable [S-1352].
- **Essay sentence:** "Under the general rule the clause is valid if damages were hard to estimate and the sum was a reasonable forecast; in California the clause is presumed valid and the party challenging it must show it was unreasonable when made (Civ. Code § 1671(b))."

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: a PRC court can adjust 违约金; California's § 1671 does not let a court adjust a clause, but asks whether it was reasonable when made (approx.).

## Promissory estoppel and subcontractor bids (Drennan)
- In *Drennan v. Star Paving Co.* (1958) 51 Cal.2d 409, the California Supreme Court held that a subcontractor's bid, relied on by a general contractor in its own bid, became irrevocable under Restatement § 90 reasoning. The Restatement (Second) later codified the result in § 87(2).
- Elements on an essay: an offer, foreseeable reliance, actual reasonable reliance (using the bid), and injustice.
- Defenses: the bid was so low the contractor should have known it was a mistake; the contractor engaged in bid shopping or renegotiation.

## Tort overlay: bad faith, economic loss rule and Rattagan
- **No tort for ordinary breach.** Breach of the implied covenant outside insurance gives contract damages only (*Foley*; *Freeman & Mills*).
- **Fraud is different.**
  - Fraud that induced the contract is an independent tort (*Robinson Helicopter*).
  - Under *Rattagan v. Uber Technologies* (2024) 17 Cal.5th 1, a party may sue for fraudulent concealment during performance if all tort elements are established independently of the contract and the conduct exposes the plaintiff to a risk of harm beyond what the parties contemplated [S-132].
- **Essay move.** After the contract analysis, add a short heading "Possible tort claim (fraud)" when the facts show lies or concealment. Note that punitive damages are available for the tort (Civ. Code § 3294), not for the breach.

## Arbitration agreements (Quach)
- Arbitration clauses are contracts. Federal law (the FAA) and California law treat them like other contracts, subject to defenses such as unconscionability (*Armendariz*). Fine or blurry print is a procedural-unconscionability fact only; it does not by itself make a term substantively unconscionable (*Fuentes v. Empire Nissan* (2026) [S-1727]).
- **Waiver by litigation conduct.** *Quach v. California Commerce Club* (2024) abrogated California's former rule requiring the party opposing arbitration to show **prejudice** [S-131]. That aligns California with *Morgan v. Sundance, Inc.*, 596 U.S. 411 (2022). Ordinary waiver principles apply: did the party knowingly act inconsistently with the right to arbitrate?
- This mainly matters in Civil Procedure and Performance Tests. In Contracts, it appears only if a dispute-resolution clause is in play.
