# Contracts — rule statements

**How to use this file.** Each block gives a rule you can write verbatim on an essay, then its elements, exceptions, authority, the MBE/California split and a short example. `build_anki.py` turns the **Rule (write this)** paragraphs into cards. Order roughly follows [`outline.md`](outline.md) (warranties, IV.E, sit with the UCC performance rules).

**Abbreviations.**
- **R2d** = Restatement (Second) of Contracts.
- **UCC** = Official Text of UCC Articles 1 and 2. The California Commercial Code uses the same numbering without the hyphen (UCC § 2-207 = Cal. Com. Code § 2207).
- **Civ. Code / CCP** = California Civil Code / Code of Civil Procedure.

**Essay default.** California Contracts essays are answered under general common-law principles plus UCC Article 2 unless the call asks for California law [S-077]. Where California law differs, add one sentence ("In California, …") after the general rule. See [`ca-distinctions.md`](ca-distinctions.md).

## Introductory principles and applicable law

### R-CTR-001 — Governing law: UCC Article 2 or common law
**Rule (write this):** Article 2 of the UCC governs contracts for the sale of goods, meaning things movable at the time of identification to the contract; the common law governs contracts for services, real property, employment and loans. For a contract mixing goods and services, most courts apply the law that governs its predominant purpose to the whole contract.

**Elements:** 1. A "sale": title passes from seller to buyer for a price (UCC § 2-106(1)). 2. "Goods": movable things, including specially manufactured goods, unborn young of animals, growing crops and timber to be cut (UCC §§ 2-105(1), 2-107). 3. Mixed contracts: weigh the contract language, the supplier's business, the relative value of goods and services, and how the price is allocated.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Some courts apply Article 2 only to the goods portion when the price is divided (gravamen test). The 2022 UCC amendments adopt a version of this for "hybrid transactions" [S-213] [VERIFY V-102]. The common law supplements the UCC unless displaced (UCC § 1-103(b)).

**Authority:** UCC §§ 1-103(b), 2-102, 2-105, 2-106, 2-107; *Bonebrake v. Cox*, 499 F.2d 951 (8th Cir. 1974); Cal. Com. Code § 2102 et seq.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. On a California essay, make it your first heading; selected answers treat governing law as the threshold issue [VERIFY V-071].

**Example:** A bakery buys a $40,000 oven with $2,000 of installation. The predominant purpose is the oven, so Article 2 governs the whole deal, including a dispute about the installation.

### R-CTR-002 — Merchants
**Rule (write this):** A merchant is a person who deals in goods of the kind or who, by occupation, holds itself out as having knowledge or skill peculiar to the practices or goods involved. Several Article 2 rules apply only when one party, or both, are merchants.

**Elements:** Merchant-only rules: firm offers (§ 2-205, offeror); additional terms between merchants (§ 2-207(2)); merchant's confirmation (§ 2-201(2), both); implied warranty of merchantability (§ 2-314, seller deals in goods of that kind); risk of loss on receipt (§ 2-509(3), seller); merchant buyer's duties for rejected goods (§ 2-603); commercial standards for insecurity (§ 2-609).

**Exceptions / defenses:** For §§ 2-201(2), 2-205 and 2-207, almost any business person counts, because the practices involved (answering mail, confirming orders) are general business practices. For § 2-314 the seller must deal in goods of that kind.

**Authority:** UCC § 2-104(1), (3) and cmt. 2.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A dental practice that sells its used laptop is a merchant for the § 2-201(2) confirmation rule but gives no merchantability warranty, because it does not deal in laptops.

## I.A Mutual assent

### R-CTR-003 — Formation and the objective theory of assent
**Rule (write this):** A contract is formed by mutual assent, usually an offer and an acceptance, supported by consideration or a substitute for it, with no defense to enforcement. Assent is judged objectively, by what a reasonable person in the other party's position would understand from the words and conduct, not by a party's secret intent.

**Elements:** 1. Offer. 2. Acceptance. 3. Consideration (or promissory estoppel or another substitute). 4. No defense (capacity, duress, mistake, fraud, illegality, statute of frauds).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Intent to be legally bound is not required, but a manifest intent not to be bound, or to be bound only on signing a final writing, prevents formation. A jest that a reasonable person would recognize as a jest is not assent.

**Authority:** R2d §§ 17, 18, 21, 27; *Lucy v. Zehmer*, 196 Va. 493, 84 S.E.2d 516 (1954); Cal. Civ. Code §§ 1550, 1565, 1580.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. Civil Code § 1550 lists the essentials: parties capable of contracting, their consent, a lawful object, and sufficient consideration.

**Example:** After drinks, Z writes and signs "I agree to sell my farm to L for $50,000," later claiming he was joking. L reasonably took it seriously after long negotiation, so a contract exists.

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: like 要约/承诺 analysis, but US courts ask what a reasonable person would understand, not the party's real intent (approx.).

### R-CTR-004 — Offer
**Rule (write this):** An offer is a manifestation of willingness to enter into a bargain, made so as to justify the offeree in understanding that its assent is invited and will conclude the bargain. It must show a commitment, contain reasonably definite terms, and be communicated to the offeree.

**Elements:** 1. Language of commitment ("I will sell"), not invitation ("Would you consider…?"). 2. Definite terms: at common law, parties, subject matter, price and quantity; under the UCC, quantity is the key term. 3. Communication to an identified offeree or class.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Preliminary negotiations, price lists and most quotations are invitations to deal (R2d § 26); see R-CTR-005.

**Authority:** R2d §§ 24, 26, 33.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** "I'd want at least $500,000 for the building" states a price hope. "I will sell you the building for $500,000; tell me by Friday" is an offer.

### R-CTR-005 — Advertisements, rewards and auctions
**Rule (write this):** Advertisements, catalogs and circulars are ordinarily invitations to deal, not offers, unless they are clear, definite and explicit, leave nothing open for negotiation, and specify who can accept (for example, "first come, first served"). A reward offer seeks a unilateral contract. At an auction the bidder makes the offer, and an auction is "with reserve" unless announced "without reserve."

**Elements:** Ads: specific quantity, words of commitment, an identified person or limited class who may accept. Auctions with reserve: the auctioneer may withdraw the goods until the hammer falls. Without reserve: once bids are called, the goods cannot be withdrawn unless no bid is made within a reasonable time. A bidder may retract a bid before the hammer falls.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A reward can be accepted only by someone who knows of it.

**Authority:** R2d §§ 26, 28; UCC § 2-328; *Lefkowitz v. Great Minneapolis Surplus Store*, 251 Minn. 188, 86 N.W.2d 689 (1957); *Leonard v. Pepsico, Inc.*, 88 F. Supp. 2d 116 (S.D.N.Y. 1999); *Donovan v. RRL Corp.* (2001) 26 Cal.4th 261 [S-205].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. In *Donovan*, the California Supreme Court treated a car dealer's newspaper ad as an offer in the context of a Vehicle Code pricing statute [S-205].

**Example:** "Saturday 9 a.m.: one fur coat, $1, first come, first served." The first customer to tender $1 accepts.

### R-CTR-006 — Termination of the power of acceptance
**Rule (write this):** An offeree's power to accept ends on (1) rejection or counteroffer, (2) lapse of the stated time or, if none, a reasonable time, (3) revocation by the offeror, or (4) death or incapacity of either party, even without notice. It also ends if the subject matter is destroyed or the deal becomes illegal.

**Elements:** Rejection and counteroffer take effect on receipt. A counteroffer is a rejection plus a new offer, but a mere inquiry ("Would you take less?") is not. Reasonable time depends on market volatility; an offer made in conversation usually lapses when the conversation ends.

**Exceptions / defenses:** An option contract or firm offer is not terminated by rejection, a counteroffer, or (majority) the offeror's death during its term.

**Authority:** R2d §§ 36–41, 48; Cal. Civ. Code § 1587 (revocation includes lapse, failure of a condition, and the proposer's death or insanity) [S-212].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** O offers his car to E and dies the next day. E, unaware, mails an acceptance. No contract: the offer died with O.

### R-CTR-007 — Revocation
**Rule (write this):** An offeror may revoke an offer at any time before acceptance, even after promising to hold it open, unless the offer is irrevocable. Revocation takes effect when the offeree receives it, directly from the offeror or indirectly through reliable information that the offeror has taken definite action inconsistent with the offer.

**Elements:** Direct revocation on receipt (R2d § 42). Indirect revocation (§ 43). An offer to the public is revoked by equal publicity (§ 46).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Irrevocable offers: options (R-CTR-008), merchant's firm offers (R-CTR-009), unilateral offers once performance has begun (R-CTR-010), and offers relied on (R-CTR-011).

**Authority:** R2d §§ 42, 43, 46; *Dickinson v. Dodds* (1876) 2 Ch. D. 463; Cal. Civ. Code § 1587 [S-212].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** O offers land to E "open until Friday." On Wednesday E reliably learns that O sold the land to X. E's Thursday acceptance is too late.

### R-CTR-008 — Option contracts
**Rule (write this):** An option contract is a promise to hold an offer open that is itself binding, usually because it is supported by consideration. The offeror cannot revoke during the option period, and the offeree's acceptance takes effect only when the offeror receives it.

**Elements:** 1. Promise to keep the offer open. 2. Consideration, or a signed writing that recites a purported consideration and proposes a fair exchange within a reasonable time (R2d § 87(1)(a)). 3. Acceptance received within the term.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Under the majority rule a rejection during the term does not end the option. Some courts refuse to enforce a recited consideration that was never paid; the Restatement enforces it.

**Authority:** R2d §§ 25, 37, 63(b), 87(1).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** For $100, O promises to keep open for 30 days an offer to sell a lot for $200,000. O's day-10 revocation is ineffective, and E's acceptance received on day 29 forms a contract.

### R-CTR-009 — Merchant's firm offer (UCC § 2-205)
**Rule (write this):** A merchant's signed written offer to buy or sell goods that by its terms gives assurance it will be held open is irrevocable without consideration for the time stated or, if none, a reasonable time, but never longer than three months.

**Elements:** 1. Merchant offeror. 2. Goods. 3. Signed writing. 4. Assurance it will be held open. 5. Stated or reasonable period, at most three months. 6. If the assurance is on a form supplied by the offeree, the offeror must sign it separately.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Beyond three months, irrevocability needs consideration (an option). The offeree need not be a merchant.

**Authority:** UCC § 2-205; Cal. Com. Code § 2205.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A lumber dealer signs and emails: "We will hold this price on 500 boards for 60 days." It cannot revoke for 60 days, although the buyer paid nothing.

### R-CTR-010 — Unilateral offers: beginning performance
**Rule (write this):** When an offer invites acceptance only by performance, an option contract arises once the offeree tenders or begins the invited performance. The offeror cannot then revoke, but its duty is conditional on the offeree completing performance within the stated or a reasonable time.

**Elements:** 1. An offer seeking performance, not a promise. 2. Performance actually begun, not mere preparation. 3. The offeror's duty is conditional on completion.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Preparation (buying materials) is not beginning performance but may support reliance under § 87(2). The offeree is not bound to finish. If the offer is ambiguous, the offeree may accept by promise or performance (R2d § 32), and beginning performance then binds the offeree to complete it (§ 62).

**Authority:** R2d §§ 32, 45, 62; Cal. Civ. Code § 1584.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** O promises E $5,000 "if you paint my barn." E paints half and O revokes. The revocation fails; if E finishes in a reasonable time, O owes $5,000.

### R-CTR-011 — Reliance makes an offer irrevocable (Drennan)
**Rule (write this):** An offer that the offeror should reasonably expect to induce substantial action or forbearance by the offeree before acceptance, and that does induce it, is binding as an option contract to the extent necessary to avoid injustice. Courts apply this to a subcontractor's bid that a general contractor relies on in submitting its own bid.

**Elements:** 1. Offer. 2. Foreseeable substantial reliance before acceptance. 3. Actual, reasonable reliance. 4. Injustice without enforcement.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Reliance is unreasonable if the bid is obviously mistaken. A contractor who reopens bargaining or "bid shops" may lose protection. The contractor is not itself bound to the subcontractor.

**Authority:** R2d § 87(2); *Drennan v. Star Paving Co.* (1958) 51 Cal.2d 409; contrast *James Baird Co. v. Gimbel Bros.*, 64 F.2d 344 (2d Cir. 1933).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. *Drennan* is a California Supreme Court case; cite it.

**Example:** A paving subcontractor bids $7,000; the general contractor uses it and wins the job; the subcontractor withdraws. The bid stays open for a reasonable time, and the contractor recovers the extra cost of a substitute paver.

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: the result resembles liability for 缔约过失, but US law requires an offer or promise that was relied on (approx.).

### R-CTR-012 — Acceptance and the mirror-image rule
**Rule (write this):** An acceptance is a manifestation of assent to the terms of an offer, made by the offeree with knowledge of the offer, in the manner the offer invites or requires. At common law the acceptance must mirror the offer: a reply that adds or changes terms is a rejection and a counteroffer.

**Elements:** 1. By the person the offer invites (offers are not assignable, but options are). 2. With knowledge of the offer. 3. Unequivocal assent. 4. Mirror image (common law). 5. Proper manner; the offeror is master of the offer.

**Exceptions / defenses:** An acceptance that merely requests a change, without making it a condition, is still an acceptance (R2d § 61). Terms the law would imply anyway are not new terms. For goods, UCC § 2-207 replaces the mirror-image rule.

**Authority:** R2d §§ 50, 52, 58, 59, 61; Cal. Civ. Code § 1585 (a qualified acceptance is a new proposal).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule for non-goods contracts.

**Example:** O offers land for $300,000. E replies, "I accept, provided you include the tractor." That is a counteroffer, and there is no contract unless O accepts it.

### R-CTR-013 — Battle of the forms: additional terms (§ 2-207(1)–(2))
**Rule (write this):** In a sale of goods, a definite and seasonable expression of acceptance operates as an acceptance even though it states additional or different terms, unless acceptance is expressly made conditional on assent to them. Additional terms are proposals; between merchants they become part of the contract unless the offer expressly limits acceptance to its terms, the terms materially alter the contract, or the offeror objects within a reasonable time.

**Elements:** 1. Is the reply a definite expression of acceptance? A mismatch on dickered terms such as price or quantity is not. 2. Is it expressly conditional? That requires clear language. 3. Are both parties merchants? If not, additional terms are mere proposals. 4. Between merchants, the term is in unless one of the three exceptions applies.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Material alteration means surprise or hardship, such as a warranty disclaimer or a right to cancel on late payment. Not material: interest on overdue invoices within trade practice, or a reasonable time for complaints (cmts. 4–5). Courts split on arbitration clauses.

**Authority:** UCC § 2-207(1)–(2) and cmts. 4–5; *Dorton v. Collins & Aikman Corp.*, 453 F.2d 1161 (6th Cir. 1972); Cal. Com. Code § 2207.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A buyer's purchase order says nothing about interest. The seller's acknowledgment adds interest on late payments at the trade's usual rate. Both are merchants and the buyer does not object, so the term likely joins the contract.

### R-CTR-014 — Battle of the forms: different terms and § 2-207(3)
**Rule (write this):** When the forms contain conflicting terms, most courts apply the knockout rule: the conflicting terms cancel each other and UCC gap fillers apply. If the writings do not form a contract but both parties act as though one exists, a contract arises under § 2-207(3), consisting of the terms on which the writings agree plus the UCC's supplementary terms.

**Elements:** For § 2-207(3): 1. No contract from the exchanged writings, for example because the acceptance was expressly conditional. 2. Conduct by both parties recognizing a contract, such as shipment and payment.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Minority views: treat a different term as an additional term under (2), or let the offeror's term stand. The common-law "last shot" rule does not apply under § 2-207.

**Authority:** UCC § 2-207 and cmt. 6; *Daitom, Inc. v. Pennwalt Corp.*, 741 F.2d 1569 (10th Cir. 1984).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. Apply the knockout rule, then gap fillers.

**Example:** The buyer's order demands full warranties; the seller's acknowledgment disclaims all warranties. The terms knock each other out, and the § 2-314 merchantability warranty fills the gap.

### R-CTR-015 — Mailbox rule
**Rule (write this):** An acceptance sent by a reasonable means takes effect when dispatched, even if it is lost, unless the offer provides otherwise. Revocations, rejections and counteroffers take effect only when received. An acceptance under an option contract takes effect only when received.

**Elements:** 1. Properly addressed and dispatched. 2. By an authorized or reasonable medium; otherwise effective on receipt, if timely. Sequencing: rejection sent first, then acceptance — whichever arrives first controls. Acceptance sent first, then rejection — contract on dispatch, but if the rejection arrives first and the offeror relies on it, the offeree is estopped.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Offers requiring receipt; options; instantaneous communication such as telephone, treated as face to face. How the rule applies to email is not settled [VERIFY V-114].

**Authority:** R2d §§ 40, 42, 63, 64, 66, 67; *Adams v. Lindsell* (1818) 1 B. & Ald. 681; Cal. Civ. Code §§ 1582, 1583, 1587 [S-212].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule; Civil Code § 1583 codifies the dispatch rule [S-212].

**Example:** O mails an offer on March 1. E mails an acceptance on March 3. O's revocation reaches E on March 4. The contract was formed on March 3.

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: PRC law generally treats acceptance as effective on arrival; the US default is dispatch (approx.).

### R-CTR-016 — Acceptance by promise or performance; UCC § 2-206
**Rule (write this):** Unless the offer clearly requires a particular manner, it invites acceptance in any reasonable manner, by promise or by performance. An offeree who accepts by performance must notify the offeror with reasonable diligence if the offeror would not otherwise learn of it. Under UCC § 2-206, an order for prompt shipment may be accepted by a prompt promise to ship or by prompt shipment; shipping nonconforming goods is both an acceptance and a breach unless the seller seasonably notifies the buyer that the shipment is only an accommodation.

**Elements:** 1. Manner invited by the offer. 2. Notice where needed. 3. For goods, the accommodation exception.

**Exceptions / defenses:** If notice of performance is required and not given, the offeror's duty is discharged. Under the UCC, if beginning performance is a reasonable mode, an offeror not notified within a reasonable time may treat the offer as lapsed.

**Authority:** R2d §§ 30, 32, 53, 54, 62; UCC § 2-206; Cal. Com. Code § 2206.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A buyer orders 100 blue widgets for prompt shipment, and the seller ships red ones without comment. A contract is formed and breached at the same moment. With a note saying "red sent as an accommodation," the shipment would be only a counteroffer.

### R-CTR-017 — Silence and conduct as acceptance
**Rule (write this):** Silence is generally not acceptance. It operates as acceptance only where (1) the offeree takes the benefit of offered services with a reasonable chance to reject them and reason to know compensation is expected; (2) the offeror said silence would suffice and the offeree, by staying silent, intends to accept; or (3) prior dealings make it reasonable for the offeree to give notice if it does not accept. Exercising dominion over offered goods is acceptance.

**Elements:** One of the three situations, or an act of ownership over the goods.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Unordered merchandise sent by mail may be treated as a gift (39 U.S.C. § 3009).

**Authority:** R2d § 69; Cal. Civ. Code § 1589.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A club member who for years has returned unwanted monthly selections under the club's rules is bound when she keeps a selection without returning it.

### R-CTR-018 — Implied-in-fact contracts and quasi-contract
**Rule (write this):** An implied-in-fact contract is a true contract inferred from conduct rather than words and is enforced like an express contract. A quasi-contract (contract implied in law) is not a contract but a restitution remedy: where one party confers a benefit that the other knowingly accepts in circumstances making it unjust to keep without paying, the law imposes an obligation to pay its reasonable value.

**Elements:** Quasi-contract: 1. Benefit conferred. 2. The defendant's knowledge or appreciation of it. 3. Unjust retention (the claimant was not a volunteer or officious intermeddler).

**Exceptions / defenses:** No quasi-contract recovery where an enforceable express contract covers the same subject, or where the benefit was conferred as a gift.

**Authority:** R2d §§ 4, 19; Restatement (Third) of Restitution and Unjust Enrichment § 1; Cal. Civ. Code §§ 1619–1621.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A patient who is examined and leaves owes the doctor's fee on an implied-in-fact contract. A doctor who treats an unconscious accident victim recovers the reasonable value in quasi-contract.

## I.B Indefiniteness

### R-CTR-019 — Definiteness and agreements to agree
**Rule (write this):** A contract must be reasonably certain, meaning certain enough to tell whether a breach occurred and to fashion a remedy. At common law, missing essential terms can prevent formation, though a court will supply a reasonable term if the parties clearly intended to be bound. Under the UCC, a contract does not fail for indefiniteness if the parties intended to contract and there is a reasonably certain basis for a remedy.

**Elements:** Common law: parties, subject matter, price, quantity, and for land the description and price. UCC: an intent to contract plus a basis for a remedy (quantity is essential).

**Exceptions / defenses:** An "agreement to agree" on a material term is traditionally unenforceable, but courts may enforce it if an objective standard exists. Part performance can cure vagueness.

**Authority:** R2d §§ 33, 204; UCC § 2-204(3).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A two-year lease "at a rent to be agreed" likely fails at common law. A sale of goods "at a price to be agreed" is enforced at a reasonable price (§ 2-305).

### R-CTR-020 — UCC gap fillers
**Rule (write this):** When an Article 2 contract omits a term, the Code supplies it. The price is a reasonable price at the time for delivery. Delivery is at the seller's place of business, within a reasonable time. Payment is due when and where the buyer receives the goods. Implied warranties of title and quality also apply. Quantity is the one term the UCC will not supply, except in output and requirements contracts.

**Elements:** Open price applies if nothing is said about price, the parties fail to agree on it, or an agreed market or third party fails to set it. A price to be fixed by one party must be fixed in good faith.

**Exceptions / defenses:** If the parties intended not to be bound unless they agreed on price, there is no contract.

**Authority:** UCC §§ 2-305, 2-307, 2-308, 2-309, 2-310, 2-312–2-315.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** Merchants agree on 300 chairs but never mention price, place or time. The buyer pays a reasonable price on receipt at the seller's warehouse, and pickup must occur within a reasonable time.

### R-CTR-021 — Output, requirements and exclusive dealing
**Rule (write this):** A quantity measured by the seller's output or the buyer's requirements is definite and not illusory. It means the actual output or requirements as they occur in good faith, except that no quantity unreasonably disproportionate to a stated estimate, or to normal prior output or requirements, may be tendered or demanded. An exclusive-dealing arrangement implies an obligation to use best efforts.

**Elements:** 1. Good faith. 2. No unreasonable disproportion to an estimate or past levels. 3. Best efforts for exclusive dealing.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A good-faith reduction, even to zero, is generally allowed if it is not made to escape the contract. Increases are limited by the disproportion test.

**Authority:** UCC § 2-306; *Wood v. Lucy, Lady Duff-Gordon*, 222 N.Y. 88, 118 N.E. 214 (1917).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A bakery agrees to buy all the flour it requires for a year. It has used about 1,000 bags a year. When prices spike, it demands 5,000 bags to resell. The seller need not supply the disproportionate excess.

## I.C Consideration

### R-CTR-022 — Consideration: bargained-for exchange
**Rule (write this):** Consideration is a performance or return promise that is bargained for: the promisor seeks it in exchange for its promise, and the promisee gives it in exchange for that promise. A legal detriment to the promisee (doing what it had no duty to do, or refraining from what it had a right to do) suffices. Courts do not inquire into the adequacy of consideration.

**Elements:** 1. Legal detriment or benefit. 2. Bargained for, with each side inducing the other.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A gift conditioned on a step needed to receive it is not consideration. A sham or purely nominal consideration may be disregarded, except for options and guaranties under R2d §§ 87–88. Gross inadequacy can be evidence of fraud or unconscionability.

**Authority:** R2d §§ 71, 79, 81; *Hamer v. Sidway*, 124 N.Y. 538, 27 N.E. 256 (1891); *Kirksey v. Kirksey*, 8 Ala. 131 (1845); Cal. Civ. Code §§ 1605, 1614, 1615.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. In California a written instrument is presumptive evidence of consideration (Civ. Code § 1614), and the party attacking it bears the burden (§ 1615).

**Example:** An uncle promises his nephew $5,000 if the nephew does not smoke until 21. Giving up a legal right is a bargained-for detriment.

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: PRC contract law has no consideration requirement (a gift contract can bind); US law needs an exchange or a substitute such as § 90 (approx.).

### R-CTR-023 — Illusory promises and mutuality
**Rule (write this):** A promise that leaves performance entirely to the promisor's discretion is illusory and cannot serve as consideration. A promise is not illusory if the discretion is limited by an express or implied duty of good faith or reasonable efforts, a notice requirement before termination, or an output or requirements measure.

**Elements:** Ask whether the promisor has committed to anything at all.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A termination right exercisable only on notice; a condition outside the promisor's control; a satisfaction clause (good faith).

**Authority:** R2d §§ 77, 79; *Wood v. Lucy, Lady Duff-Gordon*; UCC § 2-306.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** "I'll buy as many widgets as I may wish to order" is illusory. Each order later placed and accepted forms its own contract.

### R-CTR-024 — Pre-existing duty rule and part payment of debts
**Rule (write this):** Performing, or promising to perform, a legal duty already owed to the promisor is not consideration. So a promise to pay more for the same work, or a creditor's promise to accept part payment of a liquidated, undisputed debt in full satisfaction, is unenforceable at common law.

**Elements:** 1. An existing duty owed to the promisor. 2. No new or different performance. 3. No honest dispute.

**Exceptions / defenses:** (1) Any new or different performance (earlier payment, a different place, extra work). (2) An honest dispute about the duty or amount. (3) A modification that is fair and equitable in view of unanticipated circumstances (R2d § 89(a)). (4) Reliance (§ 89(c)). (5) A duty owed to a third party. (6) Sales of goods (UCC § 2-209(1)). (7) In California, part performance accepted in writing in satisfaction extinguishes the obligation (Civ. Code § 1524) [S-208].

**Authority:** R2d §§ 73, 89; *Alaska Packers' Ass'n v. Domenico*, 117 F. 99 (9th Cir. 1902); *Foakes v. Beer* (1884) 9 App. Cas. 605; Cal. Civ. Code § 1524 [S-208].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same common-law rule; on a California essay add § 1524 for part payments [S-208].

**Example:** Halfway through a $100,000 job, a builder says "pay me $20,000 more or I walk," and the owner agrees. With no unforeseen difficulty, the promise lacks consideration and may also be economic duress.

### R-CTR-025 — Forbearance and settlement of claims
**Rule (write this):** Forbearing to assert a claim, or surrendering it, is consideration if the claim is valid, if it is doubtful in fact or law, or if the forbearing party believes in good faith that it may fairly be determined to be valid.

**Elements:** 1. Forbearance or surrender. 2. A valid claim, or a doubtful claim, or a good-faith belief in its validity.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A claim the claimant knows to be baseless is not consideration.

**Authority:** R2d § 74.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** P believes in good faith that D's dog bit her, though liability is doubtful. P's promise not to sue supports D's promise to pay $2,000.

### R-CTR-026 — Past consideration, moral obligation and benefit received
**Rule (write this):** A promise made in recognition of a benefit already received is not supported by consideration, because nothing was bargained for. Modern exceptions enforce such a promise to the extent necessary to prevent injustice where the promisee conferred a material benefit not intended as a gift (R2d § 86). A new promise to pay a debt barred by the statute of limitations is also enforceable, as is one to pay a debt discharged in bankruptcy, subject to federal reaffirmation rules.

**Elements (§ 86):** 1. A material benefit to the promisor. 2. The promisee did not act as a volunteer. 3. The promise is not disproportionate to the benefit. 4. Injustice without enforcement.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Reaffirmation of a discharged debt must meet 11 U.S.C. § 524(c). In California, an acknowledgment or new promise that revives a time-barred debt must be in a signed writing (CCP § 360).

**Authority:** R2d §§ 82, 83, 86; *Mills v. Wyman*, 20 Mass. (3 Pick.) 207 (1825); *Webb v. McGowin*, 27 Ala. App. 82, 168 So. 196 (1935); Cal. Civ. Code § 1606 [S-207].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** on the MBE, state the traditional rule, then § 86 if the facts invite it. Civil Code § 1606 calls a moral obligation from a past benefit "good consideration," but California courts read it narrowly, usually requiring a prior legal or equitable obligation [S-207].

**Example:** W saves employer M from serious injury and is crippled doing so; M later promises W a pension for life. Under § 86 the promise is enforceable; under the strict past-consideration rule it is not.

## I.D Obligations without a bargain

### R-CTR-027 — Promissory estoppel (R2d § 90)
**Rule (write this):** A promise that the promisor should reasonably expect to induce action or forbearance by the promisee or a third person, and that does induce it, is binding if injustice can be avoided only by enforcing it. The remedy may be limited as justice requires, often to reliance damages.

**Elements:** 1. A clear and definite promise. 2. Reliance the promisor should reasonably expect. 3. Actual, reasonable and substantial reliance. 4. Injustice without enforcement.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Charitable subscriptions and marriage settlements bind without proof of reliance (§ 90(2)). There is no estoppel without a promise; *Hoffman* extended it to pre-contract assurances, but many courts are stricter.

**Authority:** R2d § 90; *Ricketts v. Scothorn*, 57 Neb. 51, 77 N.W. 365 (1898); *Hoffman v. Red Owl Stores, Inc.*, 26 Wis. 2d 683, 133 N.W.2d 267 (1965); *Feinberg v. Pfeiffer Co.*, 322 S.W.2d 163 (Mo. Ct. App. 1959); *Drennan v. Star Paving Co.* (1958) 51 Cal.2d 409.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. On an essay, argue promissory estoppel as the fallback whenever consideration is doubtful.

**Example:** A grandfather gives his granddaughter a note for $2,000 so that she "need not work." She quits her job in reliance. The promise is enforceable.

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: closest to 缔约过失责任 for pre-contract reliance, but US promissory estoppel needs a promise, not just bad-faith negotiation (approx.).

## I.E Modification

### R-CTR-028 — Modification at common law
**Rule (write this):** At common law, a modification of an existing contract needs new consideration because of the pre-existing duty rule. It is enforceable without consideration if it is fair and equitable in view of circumstances the parties did not anticipate when contracting, or to the extent a party has materially relied on it. A "no oral modification" clause does not automatically bar a later oral modification, because courts often find the clause waived.

**Elements:** 1. A modification agreement. 2. Consideration, or a § 89 substitute (unanticipated circumstances, statute, or reliance).

**Exceptions / defenses:** The parties can also rescind and make a new contract. Waiver and estoppel may bind a party who accepted different performance.

**Authority:** R2d § 89; *Angel v. Murray*, 113 R.I. 482, 322 A.2d 630 (1974); Cal. Civ. Code § 1698 [S-201].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** differs. Under Civ. Code § 1698, a written contract may be modified by a writing, by an oral agreement to the extent it has been executed, or, unless the contract expressly provides otherwise, by an oral agreement supported by new consideration. The statute of frauds applies if the contract as modified falls within it [S-201].

**Example:** A city's trash contract assumed stable housing; 400 unexpected new units appear, and the city agrees to pay $10,000 more a year. The modification is enforceable as fair and equitable.

### R-CTR-029 — Modification under UCC § 2-209
**Rule (write this):** A modification of a contract for the sale of goods needs no consideration, but it must be made in good faith. A signed agreement excluding modification except by a signed writing cannot otherwise be modified (between a merchant and a non-merchant, the clause on the merchant's form must be separately signed), and the statute of frauds must be satisfied if the contract as modified falls within it. An attempted modification that fails these requirements can still operate as a waiver, which may be retracted by reasonable notice unless the other party has materially relied on it.

**Elements:** 1. Good faith, meaning a legitimate commercial reason and not extortion (cmt. 2). 2. Compliance with any no-oral-modification clause. 3. The statute of frauds, where applicable.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Waiver and its retraction (§ 2-209(4)–(5)). Courts differ on how much of a modification must be written under § 2-209(3).

**Authority:** UCC § 2-209 and cmt. 2; Cal. Com. Code § 2209.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A steel supplier's costs jump 40% after a mill fire. The buyer orally agrees to a 10% price increase. No consideration is needed and the request was in good faith, but a writing is needed if the modified deal must satisfy § 2-201.

## II.A Capacity

### R-CTR-030 — Capacity: minors
**Rule (write this):** A contract made by a minor (under 18) is voidable at the minor's option. The minor may disaffirm before reaching majority or within a reasonable time afterward, while the adult party stays bound. The minor ratifies by affirming after majority or by failing to disaffirm within a reasonable time. A minor is liable for the reasonable value, not the contract price, of necessaries actually furnished.

**Elements:** 1. Contract made under age 18. 2. Timely disaffirmance by words or conduct. 3. On disaffirmance, the minor returns whatever consideration remains in its possession (majority rule).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Necessaries (food, shelter, clothing, medical care) bring quasi-contract liability for reasonable value. Some states make the minor account for use or depreciation. Ratification after majority ends the power to disaffirm.

**Authority:** R2d §§ 12, 14; Cal. Fam. Code §§ 6700, 6710, 6712 [VERIFY V-104].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same in substance. California codifies disaffirmance and the necessaries rule [VERIFY V-104].

**Example:** A 17-year-old buys a $3,000 motorbike on credit, wrecks it, and disaffirms. Under the majority rule he returns the wreck and owes nothing more; the seller bears the loss in value.

### R-CTR-031 — Capacity: mental incapacity and intoxication
**Rule (write this):** A contract is voidable by a person who, because of mental illness or defect, cannot understand in a reasonable manner the nature and consequences of the transaction, or who cannot act reasonably in relation to it if the other party has reason to know of the condition. Intoxication makes a contract voidable only if the other party has reason to know that the intoxicated person cannot understand or act reasonably. A person under guardianship has no capacity to contract.

**Elements:** 1. A cognitive test or a volitional test (the volitional test requires the other party's reason to know). 2. Timely avoidance.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Where the contract was on fair terms, the other party did not know of the incapacity, and performance has changed the situation, avoidance is limited as justice requires (R2d § 15(2)). Necessaries bring quasi-contract liability. Ratification is possible after capacity returns.

**Authority:** R2d §§ 13, 15, 16; Cal. Civ. Code §§ 38, 39.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. Civil Code § 38 voids contracts of a person entirely without understanding; § 39 makes contracts of a person of unsound mind, but not entirely without understanding, subject to rescission.

**Example:** A seller who had been drinking but negotiated coherently, drafted terms and signed cannot avoid the sale: the buyer had no reason to know of any incapacity.

## II.B Duress and undue influence

### R-CTR-032 — Duress
**Rule (write this):** If a party's assent is physically compelled, there is no contract. If a party's assent is induced by an improper threat that leaves no reasonable alternative, the contract is voidable. Improper threats include threats of a crime or tort, of criminal prosecution, of civil process in bad faith, and a bad-faith threat to breach an existing contract (economic duress).

**Elements:** 1. An improper threat. 2. No reasonable alternative, such as suing or buying elsewhere. 3. The threat actually induced assent.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Hard bargaining, and threats to do what one has a legal right to do (refuse to deal, sue in good faith), are not improper. Ratification after the duress ends bars avoidance.

**Authority:** R2d §§ 174, 175, 176; *Austin Instrument, Inc. v. Loral Corp.*, 29 N.Y.2d 124, 272 N.E.2d 533 (1971); Cal. Civ. Code §§ 1569, 1570, 1689(b).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** The only qualified parts supplier for a defense contract refuses to deliver unless the buyer agrees to higher prices on all orders, knowing no substitute is available in time. The price increase is voidable for economic duress.

### R-CTR-033 — Undue influence
**Rule (write this):** Undue influence is unfair persuasion of a party who is under the domination of the persuader, or who, because of a relationship of trust, is justified in assuming the persuader will not act against its welfare. A contract induced by undue influence is voidable.

**Elements:** 1. A dominant–subservient or confidential relationship, or a person made vulnerable. 2. Unfair persuasion: an unusual time or place, demands for immediate decision, no independent advice, multiple persuaders.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Ordinary persuasion and independent advice defeat the claim.

**Authority:** R2d § 177; Cal. Civ. Code § 1575; *Odorizzi v. Bloomfield School Dist.* (1966) 246 Cal.App.2d 123 [VERIFY V-110].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** An exhausted employee, just released from custody, is visited at home by two supervisors who insist he resign immediately to avoid publicity. The resignation may be rescinded for undue influence.

## II.C Mistake and misunderstanding

### R-CTR-034 — Mutual mistake
**Rule (write this):** Where both parties, when contracting, are mistaken about a basic assumption on which the contract was made, and the mistake has a material effect on the agreed exchange, the contract is voidable by the adversely affected party unless that party bears the risk of the mistake.

**Elements:** 1. A shared mistake of existing fact at formation. 2. About a basic assumption. 3. A material effect on the exchange. 4. The adversely affected party did not bear the risk. A party bears the risk where the contract allocates it (e.g., "as is"), where it knowingly proceeded with limited knowledge (conscious ignorance), or where the court reasonably allocates it.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Mistakes about value or future events are not basic-assumption mistakes. Negligence in failing to discover the mistake does not bar relief unless it amounts to bad faith (R2d § 157). The remedy is rescission with restitution, or reformation if the mistake is in the writing's expression (R-CTR-106).

**Authority:** R2d §§ 151, 152, 154, 157; *Sherwood v. Walker*, 66 Mich. 568, 33 N.W. 919 (1887); *Lenawee County Bd. of Health v. Messerly*, 417 Mich. 17, 331 N.W.2d 203 (1982); *Wood v. Boynton*, 64 Wis. 265, 25 N.W. 42 (1885) [VERIFY V-110]; Cal. Civ. Code §§ 1577, 1689(b).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. California essays have tested mistake in a sale-of-goods dispute (Feb 2026 Q2) [VERIFY V-071].

**Example:** Both parties believe a painting is a decorative copy and price it at $500; it is an original worth $80,000. Identity is a basic assumption with a material effect, but if both knew authenticity was uncertain, the seller bore the risk.

### R-CTR-035 — Unilateral mistake
**Rule (write this):** A mistake by one party about a basic assumption, with a material effect on the exchange, makes the contract voidable by that party if it does not bear the risk and either (a) enforcing the contract would be unconscionable or (b) the other party had reason to know of the mistake or caused it.

**Elements:** 1. One party's mistake about a basic assumption. 2. Material effect. 3. No risk allocation to the mistaken party. 4. Unconscionability of enforcement, or the other party's knowledge or fault.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Under *Donovan*, carelessness alone does not bar rescission; only a failure to act in good faith and fair dealing does [S-205]. A pure error of judgment, as opposed to a clerical error, rarely qualifies.

**Authority:** R2d § 153; *Donovan v. RRL Corp.* (2001) 26 Cal.4th 261 [S-205]; *Elsinore Union Elementary School Dist. v. Kastorff* (1960) 54 Cal.2d 380 [VERIFY V-110].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. California adopted § 153 in *Donovan* [S-205].

**Example:** A contractor's bid omits a $90,000 line item through an adding error, putting it 30% below every other bid, and the owner snaps it up. The contractor may rescind, because the owner had reason to know of the mistake.

### R-CTR-036 — Misunderstanding
**Rule (write this):** If the parties attach materially different meanings to a term and neither knows or has reason to know the other's meaning, or each knows or has reason to know the other's meaning, there is no contract (R2d § 20(1)). If one party knows or has reason to know the other's meaning and the other does not, the contract is enforced according to the innocent party's meaning.

**Elements:** 1. An ambiguous material term. 2. Different subjective meanings. 3. Each party's knowledge or reason to know.

**Exceptions / defenses:** If both meanings are equally reasonable and neither party is at fault, no contract is formed.

**Authority:** R2d §§ 20, 201; *Raffles v. Wichelhaus* (1864) 2 H. & C. 906; Cal. Civ. Code § 1580.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A buyer agrees to purchase "the Ellis Street lot." The seller owns two lots on two different Ellis Streets, and each party reasonably means a different one. No contract.

## II.D Fraud, misrepresentation and nondisclosure

### R-CTR-037 — Misrepresentation and fraud
**Rule (write this):** A contract is voidable if a party's assent is induced by a misrepresentation, meaning an assertion not in accord with the facts, that is either fraudulent or material, and on which the party justifiably relied. Fraud in the factum (deception about the very nature of the document signed) makes the contract void; fraud in the inducement makes it voidable.

**Elements:** 1. An assertion of fact (not opinion, unless the speaker is an expert, is in a relationship of trust, or implies facts). 2. Fraudulent (knowing or reckless, with intent to induce) or material (likely to induce a reasonable person). 3. Inducement. 4. Justifiable reliance. Tort damages for deceit also require scienter and loss.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Puffery and predictions are not assertions of fact. A promise made with no intent to perform is fraud. A merger clause does not bar proof of fraud. Remedies: rescission with restitution, or damages; under the UCC, rescission for fraud does not bar damages (§ 2-721).

**Authority:** R2d §§ 159, 162, 164, 168, 169; UCC § 2-721; Cal. Civ. Code §§ 1572, 1689(b), 1709, 1710.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. In California, fraud evidence comes in even if it contradicts the writing (*Riverisland*, R-CTR-052), and a separate tort claim may lie (R-CTR-117).

**Example:** A seller of an office building says, "The HVAC system was replaced last year," when it was only patched. That is a material misrepresentation even if made innocently, so the buyer may rescind. If the seller knew, the buyer may instead sue for fraud damages.

### R-CTR-038 — Nondisclosure and concealment
**Rule (write this):** Actively concealing a fact is equivalent to asserting that it does not exist. Mere nondisclosure counts as a misrepresentation only where disclosure is needed to correct a prior assertion; to correct the other party's mistake about a basic assumption when silence would breach good faith and fair dealing; to correct a mistake about the contents of a writing; or where the parties are in a relationship of trust and confidence.

**Elements:** 1. Concealment, or nondisclosure in one of the four situations. 2. Materiality. 3. Reliance.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Arm's-length parties generally need not volunteer information about the market or value.

**Authority:** R2d §§ 160, 161; Cal. Civ. Code § 1572(3) (suppression of a known fact); *Lingsch v. Savage* (1963) 213 Cal.App.2d 729 [VERIFY V-106].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** on the MBE, apply § 161. In California, a seller of real property must disclose known facts that materially affect value and that the buyer does not know and cannot reasonably discover [VERIFY V-106].

**Example:** A seller paints over water stains to hide a leaking roof. The concealment is treated as a misrepresentation that the roof does not leak.

## II.E Illegality, unconscionability and public policy

### R-CTR-039 — Illegality
**Rule (write this):** A contract whose consideration or performance is illegal is void and unenforceable by either party, and courts generally leave the parties where they find them. If the purpose is legal but one party knows the other intends an illegal use, the knowing party can still enforce the contract unless it facilitates a serious wrong.

**Elements:** 1. An illegal subject matter or purpose at formation. 2. Each party's knowledge and relative fault.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A party who is not *in pari delicto* (less culpable, or in the class the statute protects); withdrawal before the illegal purpose is carried out; enforcement of a divisible legal part. Supervening illegality discharges the contract (R-CTR-082). Licensing: if the licensing statute protects the public, the unlicensed party cannot enforce; if it only raises revenue, the party can.

**Authority:** R2d § 178; Cal. Civ. Code §§ 1550, 1667, 1668; Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code § 7031 (an unlicensed contractor cannot sue for compensation).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** An unlicensed electrician cannot sue for the price of wiring work where the licensing law protects public safety.

### R-CTR-040 — Unconscionability
**Rule (write this):** A court may refuse to enforce a contract or term that was unconscionable when the contract was made, enforce the rest without it, or limit its application. Most courts require both procedural unconscionability (oppression or surprise in the bargaining: adhesion, unequal power, hidden terms) and substantive unconscionability (terms unreasonably favorable to the stronger party), weighed on a sliding scale.

**Elements:** 1. Procedural element. 2. Substantive element. 3. Measured at formation. 4. A question of law for the court.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A bad bargain is not unconscionable. Commercial parties seldom succeed.

**Authority:** UCC § 2-302; R2d § 208; *Williams v. Walker-Thomas Furniture Co.*, 350 F.2d 445 (D.C. Cir. 1965); Cal. Civ. Code § 1670.5; *Armendariz v. Foundation Health Psychcare Services, Inc.* (2000) 24 Cal.4th 83.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. California uses the sliding scale (*Armendariz*), often for arbitration clauses.

**Example:** A fine-print clause in a rent-to-own form keeps a balance on every item a low-income buyer ever bought until all are paid off, so one missed payment lets the seller repossess everything. The clause is likely unconscionable.

### R-CTR-041 — Public policy: exculpatory clauses and restraints of trade
**Rule (write this):** Contract terms that violate public policy are unenforceable. Exculpatory clauses cannot excuse intentional or reckless harm, and are unenforceable where the transaction affects the public interest. Under the majority rule, a covenant not to compete is enforceable only if it is ancillary to a legitimate transaction (sale of a business or employment), reasonable in duration, geography and scope, and needed to protect a legitimate interest.

**Elements:** Exculpatory clauses: clear language, no intentional or reckless conduct, no essential public service. Noncompetes: ancillary, legitimate interest, reasonable scope.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A court may "blue pencil" an overbroad covenant in some states.

**Authority:** R2d §§ 186–188, 195; *Tunkl v. Regents of University of California* (1963) 60 Cal.2d 92; Cal. Civ. Code § 1668; Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code §§ 16600, 16600.5 [S-206]; *Edwards v. Arthur Andersen LLP* (2008) 44 Cal.4th 937.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** differs. California voids employee noncompetes (B&P § 16600, *Edwards*), with narrow statutory exceptions for the sale of a business's goodwill and the dissolution of a partnership or LLC [VERIFY V-112]. Since 2024, a void noncompete is unenforceable regardless of where or when it was signed (§ 16600.5) [S-206]. On the MBE, apply the reasonableness test.

**Example:** A hospital's admission form releasing it from all liability for negligence is void as against public policy because medical care affects the public interest.

## II.F Statute of frauds

### R-CTR-042 — Statute of frauds: covered contracts
**Rule (write this):** Certain contracts are unenforceable unless evidenced by a writing signed by the party to be charged: (1) promises made in consideration of marriage; (2) contracts that by their terms cannot be performed within one year of their making; (3) contracts transferring an interest in land, including leases over one year; (4) promises by an executor or administrator to pay estate debts from personal funds; (5) contracts for the sale of goods for $500 or more; and (6) promises to answer for another's debt (suretyship).

**Elements:** Mnemonic **MY LEGS**: Marriage, Year, Land, Executor, Goods, Surety. Ask first whether the contract is within the statute, then whether it is satisfied, then whether an exception applies.

**Exceptions / defenses:** See R-CTR-044 to R-CTR-048.

**Authority:** R2d § 110; UCC § 2-201; Cal. Civ. Code § 1624 [S-202] [VERIFY V-100]; Cal. Com. Code § 2201 [S-211].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** the California list differs slightly. Civil Code § 1624(a) adds an agreement employing an agent or broker to buy, sell or lease real estate (over one year) for compensation ((a)(4)) and an agreement that by its terms is not to be performed during the promisor's lifetime ((a)(5)) [S-202]. Its other categories are not fully confirmed [VERIFY V-100].

**Example:** An oral agreement on January 2, 2027 to employ a manager for 18 months is within the one-year provision and unenforceable against an employer who signed nothing.

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: PRC law generally lets parties choose the form of a contract; the US statute of frauds makes listed contracts unenforceable without a signed writing (approx.).

### R-CTR-043 — Statute of frauds: a sufficient writing
**Rule (write this):** The required writing need not be the contract itself. It may be one or more documents, made before or after formation, that reasonably identify the subject matter, show that a contract was made, and state the essential terms of the unperformed promises with reasonable certainty, signed by the party against whom enforcement is sought. Any mark intended to authenticate, including initials, a letterhead or an electronic signature, can be a signature.

**Elements:** 1. Subject matter. 2. Evidence that a contract was made. 3. Essential terms. 4. The defendant's signature.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Under California law, extrinsic evidence may clarify ambiguous essential terms in a memorandum but cannot supply missing ones (*Sterling*) [S-204].

**Authority:** R2d §§ 131, 132, 134; 15 U.S.C. § 7001 (E-SIGN); Cal. Civ. Code § 1633.7 (UETA); *Sterling v. Taylor* (2007) 40 Cal.4th 757 [S-204].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule; add *Sterling* on a California essay [S-204].

**Example:** A seller's signed email, "Confirming I'll sell you my lot at 12 Elm St. for $250,000," satisfies the statute against the seller, but not against the buyer, who signed nothing.

### R-CTR-044 — Statute of frauds: the one-year provision
**Rule (write this):** The one-year provision covers only contracts that by their terms cannot possibly be fully performed within one year of the date they are made. If performance within a year is possible, however unlikely, the contract is outside the statute. Measure from the making of the contract, not from the start of performance.

**Elements:** 1. The date of making. 2. The earliest possible date of full performance under the terms.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A lifetime contract is outside the statute, because the person could die within a year. Courts split on whether a right to terminate within a year counts as performance; the majority says it does not. Full performance by one party takes the contract out of the provision (R2d § 130(2)).

**Authority:** R2d § 130; Cal. Civ. Code § 1624(a)(1) [S-202].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** On January 1, E is hired for one year starting February 1. The contract cannot be performed within a year of its making and is within the statute. "Employment for as long as you live" is not.

### R-CTR-045 — Statute of frauds: suretyship and the main purpose rule
**Rule (write this):** A promise made to a creditor to pay another's debt if the debtor does not pay (a collateral promise) must be in writing. The statute does not apply to a primary promise to pay one's own debt ("send the goods to my son and bill me"), to a promise made to the debtor, or where the promisor's main purpose is to serve its own economic interest (main purpose, or leading object, rule).

**Elements:** 1. Three parties. 2. A collateral, secondary promise. 3. Made to the creditor. 4. No main-purpose exception.

**Exceptions / defenses:** The main purpose rule; an indemnity promise made to the debtor.

**Authority:** R2d § 110(1)(b); Cal. Civ. Code § 1624(a)(2) [S-202].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A parent company's officer tells a supplier, "Keep shipping to our subsidiary; if it doesn't pay, we will. We need its output for our product line." The main purpose rule makes the oral promise enforceable.

### R-CTR-046 — Statute of frauds: land and part performance
**Rule (write this):** A contract to transfer any interest in land, including a sale, mortgage, easement or lease of more than one year, is within the statute. Equity will enforce an oral land contract by specific performance where the buyer's acts unequivocally refer to the contract, usually two of: possession, payment, and substantial improvements; or where the party seeking enforcement has reasonably and materially relied so that injustice can be avoided only by enforcement.

**Elements:** 1. A land contract. 2. Part performance unequivocally referable to the contract, or substantial reliance. 3. Specific performance (an equitable remedy) is sought.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Payment alone is usually not enough, because restitution is an adequate remedy.

**Authority:** R2d §§ 125, 129; Cal. Civ. Code § 1624(a)(3) [VERIFY V-100]; *Monarco v. Lo Greco* (1950) 35 Cal.2d 621 [VERIFY V-110].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** similar. California frames the exception as equitable estoppel to prevent unconscionable injury or unjust enrichment (*Monarco*).

**Example:** A buyer orally agrees to buy a cabin, pays 20% down, moves in and builds a deck. Specific performance is available despite the statute.

### R-CTR-047 — Statute of frauds: sale of goods (UCC § 2-201)
**Rule (write this):** A contract for the sale of goods for $500 or more is not enforceable unless a writing sufficient to indicate a contract is signed by the party against whom enforcement is sought, and it is not enforceable beyond the quantity stated in the writing. Between merchants, a written confirmation sent within a reasonable time binds a recipient with reason to know its contents unless the recipient objects in writing within 10 days. No writing is needed for specially manufactured goods once the seller has substantially begun, for a contract admitted in court, or for goods paid for and accepted or received and accepted.

**Elements:** 1. Goods at $500 or more. 2. A writing that indicates a contract, states a quantity, and is signed by the defendant. 3. Or the merchant confirmation rule. 4. Or an exception in § 2-201(3).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Specially manufactured goods (unsuitable for others, substantial beginning before notice of repudiation); admission in pleadings or testimony (only up to the quantity admitted); part performance (only to the extent of goods paid for or accepted).

**Authority:** UCC § 2-201; Cal. Com. Code § 2201 [S-211] (the current text may say "record" rather than "writing" [VERIFY V-101]).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A merchant buyer phones an order for 1,000 bolts at $2 each, then sends a signed confirmation. The seller says nothing for two weeks. The seller can no longer raise the statute of frauds, though it may still prove no contract was made.

### R-CTR-048 — Statute of frauds: other routes, equal dignities and modifications
**Rule (write this):** Even without a writing, a contract within the statute may be enforced where full performance by one party takes it out of the one-year provision, or by promissory estoppel to the extent needed to avoid injustice (R2d § 139). A party who performed under an unenforceable contract may recover in restitution. An agent who signs for a principal generally needs written authority if the contract must be in writing (equal dignities rule). A modification must satisfy the statute if the contract as modified falls within it.

**Elements:** 1. Identify the exception. 2. Remember that restitution is always available for benefits conferred.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Some courts refuse to use promissory estoppel to bypass the statute except in cases of serious, unconscionable injury.

**Authority:** R2d §§ 130(2), 139, 375; UCC §§ 2-201(3), 2-209(3); Cal. Civ. Code §§ 1698(c) [S-201], 2309.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. California codifies the equal dignities rule (Civ. Code § 2309).

**Example:** A broker's oral authorization to sign a land sale for its principal does not satisfy the statute; the principal can resist enforcement.

## III.A Parol evidence

### R-CTR-049 — Parol evidence rule: integration
**Rule (write this):** When the parties adopt a writing as the final expression of their agreement (an integration), evidence of prior agreements, written or oral, or of contemporaneous oral agreements, is inadmissible to contradict its terms. If the writing is also the complete and exclusive statement of their agreement (a complete integration), such evidence is also inadmissible to add consistent additional terms. Whether and how completely a writing is integrated is a question for the judge.

**Elements:** 1. Is the writing final (at least a partial integration)? 2. Is it complete? A merger clause is strong but, under the Restatement, not conclusive evidence. Courts differ between the four-corners test and considering all relevant evidence (R2d § 214). 3. A consistent additional term is admissible against a partial integration, and even against an apparently complete one if it was agreed for separate consideration or would naturally be omitted from the writing (§ 216).

**Exceptions / defenses:** See R-CTR-050.

**Authority:** R2d §§ 209, 210, 213, 214, 216; *Mitchill v. Lath*, 247 N.Y. 377, 160 N.E. 646 (1928); *Masterson v. Sine* (1968) 68 Cal.2d 222; Cal. Code Civ. Proc. § 1856.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same core rule; California is more liberal (R-CTR-052).

**Example:** A written land sale has no merger clause. The buyer offers evidence of the seller's oral promise to remove an eyesore shed on the seller's adjoining lot. A strict court excludes it as a term that would ordinarily be written; under § 216 it is arguably a term naturally omitted.

### R-CTR-050 — Parol evidence: what the rule does not bar
**Rule (write this):** The parol evidence rule does not bar extrinsic evidence offered (1) to show that the writing is not integrated or only partially integrated; (2) to interpret an ambiguous term; (3) to prove a defense to formation or a ground for avoidance, such as fraud, duress, mistake, illegality or lack of consideration; (4) to prove an oral condition precedent to the writing taking effect; (5) to support reformation; (6) to prove a separate agreement supported by separate consideration; or (7) to prove a modification or agreement made after the writing.

**Elements:** Identify the purpose for which the evidence is offered.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Under the traditional rule, evidence of a promise directly contradicting the writing could not prove fraud. California rejected that limit in *Riverisland* (R-CTR-052).

**Authority:** R2d §§ 213, 214, 217; UCC § 2-202; Cal. Code Civ. Proc. § 1856.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A written used-car sale says "as is." The buyer offers proof that the seller orally assured him the car had never been in an accident. The evidence is admissible to prove fraud.

### R-CTR-051 — Parol evidence under UCC § 2-202
**Rule (write this):** Terms in a writing intended as a final expression may not be contradicted by a prior agreement or a contemporaneous oral agreement, but they may be explained or supplemented by course of dealing, usage of trade and course of performance, and by consistent additional terms unless the writing was intended as a complete and exclusive statement. Under the official comment, a consistent additional term is excluded only if it would certainly have been included in the writing had it been agreed.

**Elements:** 1. Final writing? 2. Contradiction or consistent supplement? 3. Trade usage and dealings come in unless carefully negated.

**Exceptions / defenses:** The same exceptions as R-CTR-050.

**Authority:** UCC § 2-202 and cmt. 3; UCC § 1-303; Cal. Com. Code § 2202.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. Note the contrast: the UCC "certainly would have been included" test admits more evidence than the common-law "naturally omitted" test.

**Example:** A written contract for 50 tons of steel says nothing about the delivery schedule. The buyer may prove a trade usage of monthly deliveries.

### R-CTR-052 — California parol evidence and interpretation
**Rule (write this):** California codifies the parol evidence rule (Code Civ. Proc. § 1856; Com. Code § 2202) and applies it liberally. A court provisionally receives extrinsic evidence to decide whether contract language is reasonably susceptible to the meaning a party urges, even if the writing looks clear on its face. Collateral oral agreements may be proved unless they would naturally have been included in the writing. Evidence of fraud is admissible even when it contradicts the writing's terms.

**Elements:** Interpretation in two steps: (1) the judge provisionally considers credible evidence of the parties' intent to decide whether the language is reasonably susceptible to the proffered meaning; (2) if it is, the evidence is admitted to interpret the contract.

**Exceptions / defenses:** The evidence must support a meaning the language can bear; it cannot rewrite the contract.

**Authority:** *Pacific Gas & Electric Co. v. G.W. Thomas Drayage & Rigging Co.* (1968) 69 Cal.2d 33; *Masterson v. Sine* (1968) 68 Cal.2d 222; *Riverisland Cold Storage, Inc. v. Fresno-Madera Production Credit Assn.* (2013) 55 Cal.4th 1169 [S-214] (overruling *Bank of America etc. Assn. v. Pendergrass* (1935) 4 Cal.2d 258); Code Civ. Proc. § 1856(g).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** differs. On the MBE, apply the Restatement and UCC tests. On a California essay, state the general rule, then add the California approach in one paragraph.

**Example:** A supplier's signed contract says "price: $10 per unit." The buyer alleges the supplier's agent falsely said the price included shipping. Under *Riverisland*, that evidence is admissible to prove fraud, although it contradicts the writing.

## III.B Interpretation

### R-CTR-053 — Interpretation: general rules
**Rule (write this):** Courts interpret a contract to give effect to the parties' mutual intention as objectively expressed. Words take their ordinary meaning, and technical terms their technical meaning. The writing is read as a whole. Specific terms control general ones, and negotiated (handwritten or typed) terms control printed form terms. A meaning that makes the contract lawful, reasonable and effective is preferred, and remaining ambiguity is construed against the drafter.

**Elements:** 1. Plain meaning. 2. Context and purpose. 3. Canons of construction. 4. Contra proferentem as the last resort.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A party who claims a special trade meaning must prove that the other party knew of it or was bound by the usage.

**Authority:** R2d §§ 201, 202, 203, 206; Cal. Civ. Code §§ 1636, 1639, 1641, 1644, 1654; *Frigaliment Importing Co. v. B.N.S. International Sales Corp.*, 190 F. Supp. 116 (S.D.N.Y. 1960).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A printed form says "no returns"; a typed addendum says "returns within 30 days for defects." The typed term controls.

### R-CTR-054 — Course of performance, course of dealing and usage of trade
**Rule (write this):** The meaning of an agreement is supplemented by course of performance (repeated performance under this contract, accepted without objection), course of dealing (conduct in the parties' earlier transactions) and usage of trade (a practice regularly observed in the trade). Where they conflict, express terms prevail over course of performance, which prevails over course of dealing, which prevails over usage of trade.

**Elements:** Hierarchy: Express terms > course of Performance > course of Dealing > Usage of trade.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A course of performance may also show a waiver or modification of an express term.

**Authority:** UCC § 1-303; R2d §§ 202(4), 203(b), 219–223.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A contract calls for delivery "by noon." For eight months the buyer accepted 2 p.m. deliveries without objection. A 2 p.m. delivery in month nine is likely not a breach (waiver or course of performance), unless the buyer gave notice that it now insists on noon.

### R-CTR-055 — Omitted and implied terms
**Rule (write this):** When the parties have not agreed on a term essential to determining their rights and duties, the court supplies a term that is reasonable in the circumstances. Courts commonly imply duties of good faith and cooperation, reasonable efforts under exclusive arrangements, and performance within a reasonable time. Under the UCC, gap fillers and implied warranties supply missing terms.

**Elements:** 1. An omitted term. 2. The parties intended to contract. 3. A reasonable term can be supplied.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A court will not imply a term that contradicts an express term.

**Authority:** R2d § 204; *Wood v. Lucy, Lady Duff-Gordon*; UCC §§ 2-305–2-310, 2-314, 2-315; Cal. Civ. Code § 1657.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** An exclusive sales agent's contract does not say the agent must market the product. The court implies a promise to use reasonable efforts, which also supplies consideration.

## IV.E Warranties (sale of goods)

### R-CTR-056 — Warranty of title
**Rule (write this):** In every sale of goods, the seller warrants that the title conveyed is good, that its transfer is rightful, and that the goods are free of any security interest or lien the buyer did not know of. A merchant regularly dealing in goods of the kind also warrants that they are free of any rightful infringement claim. The title warranty is excluded only by specific language or by circumstances showing the seller does not claim title.

**Elements:** 1. Sale of goods. 2. A defect in title, or an undisclosed lien. 3. No specific exclusion.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A general "as is" clause does not disclaim the title warranty. Sales by sheriffs or executors signal that no title is warranted.

**Authority:** UCC § 2-312.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A dealer sells a car that turns out to be stolen, and the true owner reclaims it. The buyer recovers from the dealer for breach of the title warranty.

### R-CTR-057 — Express warranties
**Rule (write this):** An express warranty is created by any affirmation of fact or promise relating to the goods, any description of the goods, or any sample or model, that becomes part of the basis of the bargain; the goods must conform to it. No formal words or intent to warrant are needed, but statements of value, opinion or commendation (puffery) do not create warranties.

**Elements:** 1. An affirmation of fact, promise, description, sample or model. 2. Relating to the goods. 3. Part of the basis of the bargain (reliance is presumed in most courts).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Puffery ("top quality," "best on the market"). A statement made after the sale can become a warranty as a modification (§ 2-209).

**Authority:** UCC § 2-313; Cal. Com. Code § 2313.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** "This mower cuts three acres an hour" is a warranty. "Best mower on the market" is puffery.

### R-CTR-058 — Implied warranty of merchantability
**Rule (write this):** Unless excluded, a contract for the sale of goods carries an implied warranty that the goods are merchantable if the seller is a merchant with respect to goods of that kind. Merchantable goods must at least pass without objection in the trade, be fit for the ordinary purposes for which such goods are used, be adequately packaged and labeled, and conform to any promises on the label.

**Elements:** 1. A merchant seller of goods of that kind. 2. Goods not fit for ordinary purposes (or failing another § 2-314 standard). 3. The defect existed at tender. 4. Causation and damages. 5. Notice of breach (§ 2-607(3)).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Disclaimer (R-CTR-060); the buyer's misuse; examination before purchase (§ 2-316(3)(b)).

**Authority:** UCC § 2-314; Cal. Com. Code § 2314.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A restaurant-supply dealer sells a commercial dishwasher that cannot keep water hot enough to sanitize dishes. It is not fit for ordinary purposes.

### R-CTR-059 — Implied warranty of fitness for a particular purpose
**Rule (write this):** Where the seller, at the time of contracting, has reason to know of a particular purpose for which the buyer needs the goods, and that the buyer is relying on the seller's skill or judgment to select or furnish suitable goods, there is an implied warranty that the goods are fit for that purpose. The seller need not be a merchant.

**Elements:** 1. A particular (not ordinary) purpose. 2. The seller's reason to know of it. 3. The buyer's actual reliance on the seller's skill or judgment. 4. Goods unfit for that purpose.

**Exceptions / defenses:** No reliance if the buyer insisted on a particular brand or specification. Disclaimer by a conspicuous writing.

**Authority:** UCC § 2-315.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A buyer tells a hardware clerk she needs paint for the inside of a swimming pool, and the clerk picks a product. If it peels under water, the fitness warranty is breached.

### R-CTR-060 — Disclaimer of warranties
**Rule (write this):** Language that negates an express warranty is inoperative to the extent it is inconsistent with the warranty. The implied warranty of merchantability may be disclaimed by language that mentions merchantability and, if written, is conspicuous. The fitness warranty may be disclaimed only by a conspicuous writing. "As is," "with all faults" or similar language excludes all implied warranties, and there is no implied warranty for defects that an examination should have revealed if the buyer examined the goods, or refused to, before contracting.

**Elements:** 1. Which warranty? 2. Was the right form used (mention of merchantability; writing; conspicuousness)? 3. Was the disclaimer part of the bargain, not delivered after the sale?

**Exceptions / defenses:** Federal consumer law bars a supplier that gives a written warranty to a consumer from disclaiming implied warranties (15 U.S.C. § 2308). California's Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act (Civ. Code § 1790 et seq.) restricts disclaimers in retail consumer sales.

**Authority:** UCC §§ 1-201(b)(10), 2-316; 15 U.S.C. § 2308.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same UCC rule. Raise the consumer statutes only on consumer facts.

**Example:** A fine-print line on the back of an invoice, "no implied warranties," is not conspicuous and does not mention merchantability, so it fails to disclaim merchantability.

### R-CTR-061 — Warranty claims: notice, who may sue, limitations
**Rule (write this):** A buyer who has accepted goods must notify the seller of a breach within a reasonable time after discovering it, or be barred from any remedy. Under UCC § 2-318 (Alternative A, the narrowest version), a seller's warranty extends to members of the buyer's family or household and to guests who may reasonably be expected to use the goods and are personally injured. A warranty action must be brought within four years after tender of delivery (the parties may shorten this to no less than one year), unless the warranty explicitly extends to future performance.

**Elements:** 1. Timely notice. 2. A protected plaintiff. 3. Suit within the limitations period.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Alternatives B and C extend to any natural person, or any person, who may reasonably be expected to use the goods.

**Authority:** UCC §§ 2-607(3)(a), 2-318, 2-725.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. Product injuries also raise tort strict liability (Torts).

**Example:** A buyer discovers in March that a boiler's welds are defective but complains only the next January, after the seller's supplier went bankrupt. The buyer risks losing all remedies for lack of reasonable notice.

## IV.A Conditions

### R-CTR-062 — Express conditions
**Rule (write this):** A condition is an event, not certain to occur, that must occur before a performance becomes due (a condition precedent), or whose occurrence discharges a duty (a condition subsequent). An express condition, created by words such as "if," "provided that," "on condition that" or "subject to," must be strictly satisfied; substantial performance is not enough. Where it is doubtful whether language creates a condition or a promise, courts read it as a promise, to avoid forfeiture.

**Elements:** 1. Conditional language. 2. Did the condition occur? 3. If not, is it excused (R-CTR-064)? Satisfaction clauses: personal taste uses a subjective, honest-satisfaction test; commercial or mechanical fitness uses an objective, reasonable-person test.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Excuse (R-CTR-064). A party with control over a condition must make good-faith efforts to make it happen.

**Authority:** R2d §§ 224, 225, 227, 228 [VERIFY V-111], 230; Cal. Civ. Code § 1442 (a condition involving a forfeiture is strictly interpreted against the party it benefits).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** "Buyer's duty is subject to buyer obtaining a 30-year loan at no more than 7% by June 1." If the buyer, after good-faith efforts, can obtain only 7.25%, the condition fails and the buyer is excused.

### R-CTR-063 — Constructive conditions and order of performance
**Rule (write this):** Courts imply constructive conditions to secure the exchange: each party's substantial performance of its earlier-due duties is a condition of the other party's duty. Where performances can be rendered at the same time, they are concurrent conditions, and a party must tender its own performance to put the other in breach. Where one performance takes time and the other does not (work versus payment), the performance that takes time must come first.

**Elements:** 1. Order of performance (contract terms, then the default rules). 2. Substantial performance or tender by the party claiming breach.

**Exceptions / defenses:** The parties may agree on progress payments or another order.

**Authority:** R2d §§ 234, 237; *Kingston v. Preston* (K.B. 1773).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. For goods, see perfect tender (R-CTR-072).

**Example:** At a land closing, the deed and the price are concurrent conditions; a seller who never tendered a deed cannot hold the buyer in breach.

### R-CTR-064 — Excuse of conditions
**Rule (write this):** The non-occurrence of a condition is excused, and the duty becomes due, where (1) the party the condition protects waives it by words or conduct; (2) that party is estopped; (3) that party wrongfully prevents or hinders its occurrence, breaching the implied duty to cooperate; (4) that party repudiates; (5) the condition becomes impossible or impracticable and is not a material part of the exchange; or (6) enforcing it would cause a disproportionate forfeiture and its occurrence was not a material part of the agreed exchange.

**Elements:** Identify the condition, its beneficiary, and the excuse.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A waiver of a condition that is a material part of the agreed exchange needs consideration (R2d § 84). A waiver of a future condition may be retracted by notice received while a reasonable time remains for the condition to occur, unless the other party has materially changed position in reliance (§ 84(2)).

**Authority:** R2d §§ 84, 229, 245 [VERIFY V-111], 255, 271 [VERIFY V-111]; Cal. Civ. Code §§ 1511, 1512 [VERIFY V-105], 3275.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. Civil Code § 3275 gives relief from forfeiture to a party who makes full compensation, except for grossly negligent, willful or fraudulent breach.

**Example:** A policy requires notice of a claim within 30 days. The insured gives notice on day 33, and the insurer suffered no prejudice. A court may excuse the condition to avoid disproportionate forfeiture.

## IV.C Breach

### R-CTR-065 — Substantial performance
**Rule (write this):** At common law, a party who has substantially performed, meaning its deviations are not material, may enforce the other party's return promise, less damages for the defects. A party whose breach is material cannot. The doctrine does not apply to express conditions or to sales of goods (perfect tender), and a willful deviation weighs heavily against substantial performance.

**Elements:** 1. Material-breach factors (R-CTR-066). 2. Good faith. 3. A deduction for the defect.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A willful breacher may be limited to restitution (R-CTR-109).

**Authority:** R2d §§ 237, 241; *Jacob & Youngs, Inc. v. Kent*, 230 N.Y. 239, 129 N.E. 889 (1921); *Plante v. Jacobs*, 10 Wis. 2d 567, 103 N.W.2d 296 (1960).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A builder finishes a house but installs pipe of an equal-quality brand other than the one specified. The owner must pay the price less the (nominal) loss in value.

### R-CTR-066 — Material vs. minor breach
**Rule (write this):** A breach is material if it deprives the injured party of a substantial part of the benefit it reasonably expected. The injured party may then suspend its own performance and, if the breach is not cured in time, terminate and sue for total breach. A minor (partial) breach gives a claim for damages only, and the injured party must continue to perform.

**Elements:** R2d § 241 factors: (a) how much of the expected benefit is lost; (b) whether damages can compensate; (c) the forfeiture the breaching party would suffer; (d) the likelihood of cure; (e) the breaching party's good faith. Time: delay is usually minor unless the contract makes time of the essence or the circumstances (perishables, seasonal goods) show that timing was vital.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A party who terminates for a minor breach is itself in material breach.

**Authority:** R2d §§ 237, 241, 242, 243.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. California essays have centered on material breach and time of the essence (Jul 2016 Q3) [VERIFY V-071].

**Example:** An excavator finishes a six-month job two weeks late. The contract has no time-of-the-essence clause, and the owner's delay costs are modest. The breach is likely minor, so the owner's termination is the material breach.

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: like 法定解除 for a breach that defeats the contract's purpose, but US law asks about materiality and cure, and a wrongful termination is itself a breach (approx.).

### R-CTR-067 — Divisible contracts
**Rule (write this):** A contract is divisible if the performances on each side can be split into corresponding pairs of part performances that the parties treated as agreed equivalents. A party who completes one part may recover the agreed equivalent for that part, even if it breaches another part, less the other party's damages.

**Elements:** 1. Performances apportionable into parts. 2. Each pair treated as agreed equivalents.

**Exceptions / defenses:** If the parties treated the deal as a single whole, the contract is entire.

**Authority:** R2d §§ 183, 240.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. For installment sales of goods, see R-CTR-076.

**Example:** A contract to paint three houses at $5,000 each. The painter finishes one and quits. It recovers $5,000 less the owner's damages from the breach.

### R-CTR-068 — Anticipatory repudiation
**Rule (write this):** An anticipatory repudiation is a clear and unequivocal statement, or a voluntary affirmative act, made before performance is due, showing that a party will not or cannot perform. The non-repudiating party may (1) treat it as a total breach, suspend performance and sue at once; (2) suspend performance and await performance for a commercially reasonable time; or (3) urge retraction. It may not keep performing to run up damages, and it must mitigate.

**Elements:** 1. Performance not yet due. 2. An unequivocal statement or act. 3. The aggrieved party's chosen response.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Where the aggrieved party has fully performed and the repudiator's only remaining duty is to pay money in installments, the aggrieved party generally must wait for each installment to fall due (R2d § 243) [VERIFY V-109]. An ambiguous statement is not a repudiation but may justify a demand for assurance (R-CTR-070).

**Authority:** R2d §§ 250, 253; UCC § 2-610; *Hochster v. De La Tour* (1853) 2 E. & B. 678; Cal. Civ. Code § 1440 [VERIFY V-105].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. A buyer's UCC market damages after a repudiation are measured when the buyer learned of the breach, and courts disagree on exactly when that is (§§ 2-713, 2-723).

**Example:** In March, a supplier tells a restaurant, "We won't deliver the walk-in freezer due in June." The restaurant may buy a substitute now and sue for the difference.

### R-CTR-069 — Retraction of repudiation
**Rule (write this):** A repudiating party may retract its repudiation until its next performance is due, unless the aggrieved party has since cancelled, materially changed position, or otherwise indicated that it considers the repudiation final, including by suing. A retraction restores the repudiator's rights, with allowance for any delay, and must include any assurance the other party justifiably demanded.

**Elements:** 1. Timely retraction. 2. No reliance or acceptance of the repudiation by the aggrieved party.

**Exceptions / defenses:** —

**Authority:** R2d § 256; UCC § 2-611.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A seller repudiates on May 1 and retracts on May 5. The buyer has not yet covered or sued, so the contract is reinstated.

### R-CTR-070 — Adequate assurance of performance
**Rule (write this):** When reasonable grounds for insecurity arise about the other party's performance, a party may demand adequate assurance of due performance, in writing under the UCC, and may suspend its own performance, if commercially reasonable, until it receives it. Failure to give adequate assurance within a reasonable time (under the UCC, no more than 30 days after receiving a justified demand) is a repudiation.

**Elements:** 1. Reasonable grounds for insecurity (between merchants, judged by commercial standards). 2. A demand (written under the UCC). 3. Adequate assurance, or none within the time allowed.

**Exceptions / defenses:** An unjustified demand, or suspension without reasonable grounds, is itself a breach.

**Authority:** UCC § 2-609; R2d § 251.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A buyer hears credible reports that the seller's only factory has burned down. It demands written assurance and may withhold its prepayment until the assurance arrives.

## IV.D Good faith

### R-CTR-071 — Good faith and fair dealing
**Rule (write this):** Every contract imposes on each party a duty of good faith and fair dealing in its performance and enforcement. Under the UCC, good faith means honesty in fact and the observance of reasonable commercial standards of fair dealing. The duty protects the parties' reasonable expectations and limits how discretion is exercised (satisfaction clauses, output and requirements terms, termination rights), but it cannot override an express term.

**Elements:** 1. A contract. 2. Conduct that frustrates the other party's right to the benefits of the agreement, or a dishonest or unreasonable exercise of discretion.

**Exceptions / defenses:** An express contractual right exercised as written is generally not a breach.

**Authority:** R2d § 205; UCC §§ 1-201(b)(20), 1-304; *Carma Developers (Cal.), Inc. v. Marathon Development California, Inc.* (1992) 2 Cal.4th 342; *Guz v. Bechtel National, Inc.* (2000) 24 Cal.4th 317.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same contract rule. In California, breach of the covenant gives tort damages only in insurance cases (*Foley v. Interactive Data Corp.* (1988) 47 Cal.3d 654; *Freeman & Mills, Inc. v. Belcher Oil Co.* (1995) 11 Cal.4th 85).

**Example:** A franchisor with discretion to approve transfers refuses one solely to capture the franchisee's goodwill for itself. That is a breach of the covenant.

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: narrower than 诚信原则; the US covenant applies only within an existing contract's performance and enforcement (approx.).

## IV.F Other performance matters (UCC)

### R-CTR-072 — Perfect tender and rejection
**Rule (write this):** Under Article 2, if the goods or the tender of delivery fail in any respect to conform to the contract, the buyer may reject the whole, accept the whole, or accept any commercial units and reject the rest. Rejection must come within a reasonable time after delivery or tender and is ineffective unless the buyer seasonably notifies the seller. After rejecting, the buyer must hold the goods with reasonable care for the seller and must not act as their owner.

**Elements:** 1. A nonconformity. 2. Timely rejection. 3. Seasonable notice, stating curable defects (§ 2-605). 4. Proper handling of the rejected goods (§§ 2-602–2-604).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Installment contracts (R-CTR-076); the seller's right to cure (R-CTR-073); an agreed limitation of remedies (§ 2-719); in shipment contracts, a delivery-arrangement defect justifies rejection only if material delay or loss results (§ 2-504); good faith.

**Authority:** UCC §§ 2-601, 2-602, 2-603, 2-604, 2-605.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** 1,000 ceramic mugs arrive and 20 are chipped. The buyer may reject all, accept all, or keep the 980 good mugs and reject the 20.

### R-CTR-073 — Seller's right to cure
**Rule (write this):** When the buyer rejects a nonconforming tender, the seller may cure (1) if the time for performance has not expired, by seasonably notifying the buyer and making a conforming delivery within the contract time; or (2) after the time has expired, if the seller had reasonable grounds to believe the tender would be acceptable, with or without a money allowance, by seasonable notice and a conforming tender within a further reasonable time.

**Elements:** 1. Rejection. 2. Notice of intent to cure. 3. A conforming tender in time.

**Exceptions / defenses:** —

**Authority:** UCC § 2-508.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** Delivery is due by March 10. The seller delivers the wrong model on March 1, and the buyer rejects. The seller may deliver the right model by March 10.

### R-CTR-074 — Acceptance of goods
**Rule (write this):** A buyer accepts goods when, after a reasonable opportunity to inspect, it signifies that they conform or that it will keep them despite a nonconformity, fails to make an effective rejection, or does any act inconsistent with the seller's ownership. Acceptance obliges the buyer to pay the contract price and ends the right to reject. The buyer must notify the seller of any breach within a reasonable time after discovering it or lose all remedies, but it may still recover damages for the nonconformity.

**Elements:** 1. A reasonable opportunity to inspect (§ 2-513). 2. One of the three forms of acceptance.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Revocation of acceptance (R-CTR-075).

**Authority:** UCC §§ 2-513, 2-606, 2-607.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A buyer uses a shipment of fabric to make garments, then complains about the color. Using the fabric was an act inconsistent with the seller's ownership, so the buyer accepted it and is limited to damages.

### R-CTR-075 — Revocation of acceptance
**Rule (write this):** A buyer may revoke acceptance of goods whose nonconformity substantially impairs their value to the buyer, if it accepted them (1) on the reasonable assumption that the nonconformity would be cured, and it was not seasonably cured; or (2) without discovering the nonconformity, where acceptance was reasonably induced by the difficulty of discovery or by the seller's assurances. Revocation must occur within a reasonable time after the buyer discovers, or should have discovered, the ground, and before any substantial change in the goods not caused by their own defects. It is effective only when the buyer notifies the seller.

**Elements:** 1. Substantial impairment. 2. Acceptance on an assumption of cure, or a latent defect or the seller's assurances. 3. Timely notice.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A revoking buyer has the same rights and duties as a rejecting buyer.

**Authority:** UCC § 2-608.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. Revocation is a common move in essays about defective equipment.

**Example:** A restaurant's new commercial dishwasher breaks down five times in its first month, and the seller's repairs fail. The buyer may revoke acceptance, recover the price paid, and cover.

### R-CTR-076 — Installment contracts
**Rule (write this):** An installment contract requires or authorizes delivery in separate lots to be separately accepted. The buyer may reject an installment only if its nonconformity substantially impairs the value of that installment and cannot be cured. The whole contract is breached only when the default on one or more installments substantially impairs the value of the whole contract.

**Elements:** 1. Separate lots. 2. Substantial impairment of the installment, and no cure. 3. Substantial impairment of the whole, for cancellation.

**Exceptions / defenses:** If the defect does not impair the whole and the seller gives adequate assurance of cure, the buyer must accept the installment.

**Authority:** UCC § 2-612.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. This is a substantial-impairment test, not perfect tender.

**Example:** One of twelve monthly coffee shipments is slightly under weight. The buyer cannot cancel the contract and may only recover damages or demand cure for that shipment.

### R-CTR-077 — Delivery terms: shipment and destination contracts
**Rule (write this):** In a shipment contract, the seller must put conforming goods in a carrier's possession, make a reasonable contract for their transport, deliver any necessary documents, and promptly notify the buyer. In a destination contract, the seller must tender conforming goods at the named destination. A contract involving carriage is presumed to be a shipment contract. "F.O.B. [seller's city]" makes a shipment contract, and "F.O.B. [buyer's city]" a destination contract. Absent agreement and carriage, delivery is at the seller's place of business.

**Elements:** 1. Is a carrier involved? 2. The F.O.B. or other trade term. 3. The seller's duties under § 2-504 or § 2-503.

**Exceptions / defenses:** C.I.F. and C. & F. terms create shipment contracts.

**Authority:** UCC §§ 2-308, 2-319, 2-320, 2-503, 2-504.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** "F.O.B. Seller's dock, Oakland." The seller's duty ends when it properly delivers the goods to the carrier in Oakland and notifies the buyer.

### R-CTR-078 — Risk of loss absent breach
**Rule (write this):** Absent breach, risk of loss follows the parties' agreement. Otherwise: (1) under a shipment contract, risk passes to the buyer when the goods are duly delivered to the carrier; (2) under a destination contract, when the goods are duly tendered at the destination; (3) for goods held by a bailee and delivered without being moved, when the buyer receives a negotiable document of title or the bailee acknowledges the buyer's right to the goods; (4) in all other cases, on the buyer's receipt of the goods if the seller is a merchant, and on tender of delivery if not.

**Elements:** Order of analysis: agreement → breach (R-CTR-079) → carrier → bailee → merchant/non-merchant.

**Exceptions / defenses:** —

**Authority:** UCC § 2-509.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. California essays have tested risk of loss in transit under an F.O.B. term (Jul 2022 Q1) [VERIFY V-071].

**Example:** A merchant seller tells the buyer, "Your sofa is ready; pick it up." The store burns down before pickup. The risk was still on the merchant seller, because the buyer had not received the sofa.

### R-CTR-079 — Risk of loss when a party breaches
**Rule (write this):** If the seller's tender or delivery is so nonconforming that the buyer may reject, the risk stays on the seller until cure or acceptance. If the buyer rightfully revokes acceptance, it may treat the risk as having been on the seller from the start, to the extent its own insurance falls short. If the buyer repudiates or otherwise breaches as to conforming goods already identified to the contract, the seller may treat the risk as resting on the buyer for a commercially reasonable time, to the extent the seller's insurance falls short.

**Elements:** 1. Which party breached? 2. The insurance-deficiency rules.

**Exceptions / defenses:** —

**Authority:** UCC § 2-510.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A seller ships the wrong model F.O.B. seller's city, and it is destroyed in transit. The risk never passed, because the tender gave the buyer a right to reject.

### R-CTR-080 — Identification and casualty to identified goods
**Rule (write this):** Goods are identified when the contract is made, if they are existing and specified, or later when the seller ships, marks or otherwise designates them as the contract goods. Identification gives the buyer a special property and an insurable interest. If the contract requires goods identified when it is made, and they suffer a casualty without either party's fault before risk passes to the buyer, the contract is avoided if the loss is total. If the loss is partial, the buyer may avoid the contract or take the goods with an allowance in price.

**Elements:** 1. Goods identified at formation. 2. Casualty without fault. 3. Before risk passed.

**Exceptions / defenses:** If the goods were not identified at formation (fungible goods from general stock), the seller must find other goods unless § 2-615 excuses it.

**Authority:** UCC §§ 2-501, 2-613.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A non-merchant contracts to sell "the 1965 roadster in my garage," and lightning destroys the garage before tender. The contract is avoided, and neither party is liable.

### R-CTR-081 — Title: voidable title and entrustment
**Rule (write this):** A purchaser generally acquires only the title its transferor had. But a person with voidable title, such as one who got the goods by fraud or a bad check, can pass good title to a good-faith purchaser for value. And entrusting goods to a merchant who deals in goods of that kind gives the merchant power to transfer all the entruster's rights to a buyer in the ordinary course of business. A thief has void title and passes none.

**Elements:** 1. Voidable or void title? 2. A good-faith purchaser for value, or a buyer in the ordinary course from an entrusted merchant.

**Exceptions / defenses:** —

**Authority:** UCC § 2-403.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** An owner leaves her watch with a jeweler for repair, and the jeweler, who also sells used watches, sells it to a customer. The customer takes good title; the owner's remedy is against the jeweler.

## IV.G Impossibility, impracticability and frustration

### R-CTR-082 — Impossibility
**Rule (write this):** A party's duty is discharged if, after contracting, performance becomes objectively impossible (no one could perform) through an event whose non-occurrence was a basic assumption of the contract, without the party's fault, and the party did not assume the risk. The classic triggers are destruction of the specific subject matter, death or incapacity of a person whose personal performance is required, and supervening illegality.

**Elements:** 1. A supervening event. 2. Objective impossibility. 3. Non-occurrence was a basic assumption. 4. No fault. 5. No assumption of the risk.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Subjective inability, such as lack of money, is no excuse. A builder whose half-built structure burns must rebuild, but a contractor repairing an existing building is discharged if it burns. Temporary impossibility only suspends the duty. Either party may recover restitution for benefits conferred.

**Authority:** R2d §§ 261–264, 272, 377; *Taylor v. Caldwell* (1863) 3 B. & S. 826.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A concert hall rented for four nights burns down before the first concert, through no one's fault. Both parties are discharged.

### R-CTR-083 — Impracticability
**Rule (write this):** A party's performance is excused where, after contracting, it is made impracticable (possible only at excessive and unreasonable difficulty or expense) by an event whose non-occurrence was a basic assumption of the contract, without the party's fault, and the party did not assume the risk. A cost increase alone, even a large one, ordinarily is not enough, and foreseeable risks are usually allocated to the party who could have guarded against them.

**Elements:** 1. A supervening event. 2. Extreme and unreasonable difficulty or expense. 3. Basic assumption. 4. No fault. 5. No assumption of the risk, express or implied. UCC: an excused seller must allocate production fairly among customers and give seasonable notice; the buyer may then terminate or take its allocation (§§ 2-615, 2-616).

**Exceptions / defenses:** A force majeure clause is read by its terms. If an agreed carrier or manner of delivery becomes unavailable but a commercially reasonable substitute exists, the substitute must be tendered and accepted (§ 2-614).

**Authority:** R2d § 261; UCC §§ 2-614, 2-615, 2-616; *Transatlantic Financing Corp. v. United States*, 363 F.2d 312 (D.C. Cir. 1966); *Mineral Park Land Co. v. Howard* (1916) 172 Cal. 289 [VERIFY V-110].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A farmer contracts to sell the crop "grown on my Field 7," and a flood destroys it. The farmer is excused. A rise in the market price of wheat does not excuse a seller of wheat in general.

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: 情势变更 lets a PRC court renegotiate or modify a contract; US impracticability only discharges the duty and is narrow (approx.).

### R-CTR-084 — Frustration of purpose
**Rule (write this):** Where, after contracting, a party's principal purpose is substantially frustrated without its fault by an event whose non-occurrence was a basic assumption of the contract, that party's remaining duties are discharged, unless the language or circumstances indicate otherwise. Both parties must have understood the purpose, and the frustration must be substantial, not merely a less profitable deal.

**Elements:** 1. A principal purpose known to both. 2. Substantial frustration. 3. Basic assumption. 4. No fault. 5. The risk was not allocated to the party seeking relief.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Courts apply it narrowly; foreseeable events usually defeat it.

**Authority:** R2d § 265; *Krell v. Henry* [1903] 2 K.B. 740; *Lloyd v. Murphy* (1944) 25 Cal.2d 48 [VERIFY V-110].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. *Lloyd v. Murphy* shows California's narrow approach: a car-showroom lease was not discharged by wartime limits on car sales, because the premises remained usable and the risk was foreseeable.

**Example:** A room rented at a high price solely to watch a parade is not needed once the parade is cancelled. The renter is discharged if the purpose was known to both parties.

## IV.H Discharge

### R-CTR-085 — Mutual rescission
**Rule (write this):** The parties may discharge their remaining duties by agreeing to rescind. When both sides still owe performance, each party's surrender of its rights is consideration for the other's. An oral rescission is generally effective unless the rescission itself must be in writing, as when it re-transfers an interest in land or the contract requires a signed writing (UCC § 2-209(2)).

**Elements:** 1. Mutual assent to rescind. 2. Executory duties on both sides, or other consideration.

**Exceptions / defenses:** If one party has fully performed, its agreement to rescind needs consideration (or operates as a release). Rescission cannot cut off a third-party beneficiary's vested rights.

**Authority:** R2d § 283; Cal. Civ. Code §§ 1689(a), 1698(d).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** Before either party has performed, a buyer and seller of a boat agree to call the deal off. Both are discharged.

### R-CTR-086 — Release
**Rule (write this):** A release is a writing or record by which the obligee immediately discharges the obligor's duty. At common law it required consideration or a seal, but modern statutes often enforce a signed release without consideration. In California, a general release does not extend to claims the releasing party did not know or suspect to exist when signing, unless that protection is expressly waived.

**Elements:** 1. A writing or record. 2. Intent to discharge. 3. Consideration, or a statute dispensing with it.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A release obtained by fraud or duress is voidable.

**Authority:** R2d § 284; UCC § 1-306; Cal. Civ. Code §§ 1541, 1542.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule; add Civil Code § 1542 on a California essay.

**Example:** A customer signs a written release of "all claims" over a late delivery. She later discovers a latent defect she did not suspect. In California, § 1542 preserves that claim unless the release expressly waived it.

### R-CTR-087 — Accord and satisfaction
**Rule (write this):** An accord is an agreement by which the obligee promises to accept a substitute performance in satisfaction of the obligor's existing duty. Satisfaction is the performance of the accord, which discharges both the original duty and the accord. Until performance, the original duty is only suspended, and if the obligor breaches the accord, the obligee may enforce either the original duty or the accord. Consideration usually comes from a good-faith dispute about the amount owed or from a different performance.

**Elements:** 1. A disputed (unliquidated) claim or a different performance. 2. An agreement to accept a substitute. 3. Performance of it.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Part payment of an undisputed, liquidated debt is not consideration for an accord (but see Civil Code § 1524 [S-208]). UCC § 3-311: if a check for a disputed claim, tendered in good faith and conspicuously marked as full satisfaction, is cashed by the claimant, the claim is discharged, subject to statutory exceptions.

**Authority:** R2d § 281; UCC § 3-311; Cal. Civ. Code §§ 1521, 1523, 1524 [S-208]; Cal. Com. Code § 3311.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule; on a California essay add Civil Code § 1524 [S-208].

**Example:** A client honestly disputes a $10,000 bill and sends a check for $7,000 marked "payment in full of disputed invoice." The contractor cashes it, so the claim is discharged.

### R-CTR-088 — Substituted contract and novation
**Rule (write this):** A substituted contract is a new agreement that the obligee accepts in immediate satisfaction of the original duty, discharging that duty at once (unlike an executory accord). A novation is a substituted contract that brings in a new party, most often a new obligor who replaces the original one with the obligee's consent, so the original obligor is released.

**Elements:** 1. A valid new agreement. 2. Intent to discharge the old duty immediately. 3. For a novation, the obligee's assent to the new party.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Without the obligee's assent, a delegation leaves the original obligor liable (R-CTR-116).

**Authority:** R2d §§ 279, 280; Cal. Civ. Code §§ 1530, 1531.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** The landlord, the tenant and an incoming business agree that the business takes over the lease and the tenant is released. That is a novation.

## V.A–V.D Damages

### R-CTR-089 — Expectation damages: the general formula
**Rule (write this):** The injured party recovers expectation damages that put it in the position it would have occupied had the contract been performed: the loss in value of the other party's performance, plus incidental and consequential damages, minus any cost or loss the injured party avoided by not having to perform.

**Elements:** Loss in value + other loss (incidental, consequential) − cost avoided − loss avoided.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Limited by foreseeability, certainty and mitigation (R-CTR-092, R-CTR-093, R-CTR-095). No recovery greater than full performance would have produced.

**Authority:** R2d §§ 344, 347; *Hawkins v. McGee*, 84 N.H. 114, 146 A. 641 (1929); Cal. Civ. Code §§ 3300, 3358.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A buyer agrees to buy a machine for $50,000. The seller breaches, and a comparable machine costs $58,000. Expectation damages are $8,000 plus incidental costs.

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: similar to 可得利益 with a foreseeability limit; US law adds a strict certainty requirement and bars punitive damages for breach (approx.).

### R-CTR-090 — Construction contract damages
**Rule (write this):** When a builder breaches, the owner recovers the reasonable cost of completing or repairing the work, unless that cost is clearly disproportionate to the value it would add (economic waste); then the owner recovers the diminution in market value. When the owner breaches, the builder recovers its expected profit plus the costs it has already incurred (equivalently, the contract price minus the cost it saved by not finishing), less payments received. A builder that has finished recovers the unpaid contract price.

**Elements:** Owner's claim: cost of completion, or diminution in value if economic waste. Builder's claim: profit + costs incurred − payments received.

**Exceptions / defenses:** After the owner repudiates, the builder must stop work; it cannot recover costs incurred afterward.

**Authority:** R2d § 348; *Jacob & Youngs, Inc. v. Kent*; *Groves v. John Wunder Co.*, 205 Minn. 163, 286 N.W. 235 (1939); *Peevyhouse v. Garland Coal & Mining Co.*, 382 P.2d 109 (Okla. 1962); *Rockingham County v. Luten Bridge Co.*, 35 F.2d 301 (4th Cir. 1929).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** An owner wrongfully cancels an $800,000 job. The builder's total cost would have been $650,000; it has spent $250,000 and been paid $100,000. Its damages: $150,000 profit + $250,000 costs − $100,000 paid = $300,000.

### R-CTR-091 — Employment contract damages
**Rule (write this):** A wrongfully discharged employee recovers the salary for the rest of the contract term, less what the employee earned, or with reasonable effort could have earned, in substantially similar employment. The employee need not accept a different or inferior job. An employer injured by an employee's breach recovers the extra cost of hiring a reasonable replacement.

**Elements:** 1. Remaining contract pay. 2. Minus actual or reasonably available comparable earnings (the employer bears the burden of proof).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Specific performance of personal services is unavailable (R-CTR-104).

**Authority:** R2d § 350; *Parker v. Twentieth Century-Fox Film Corp.* (1970) 3 Cal.3d 176.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** An actress fired from a lead role in a musical need not take a substitute role in a western drama to mitigate, because it is different and inferior employment.

### R-CTR-092 — Consequential damages and foreseeability (Hadley)
**Rule (write this):** Damages are recoverable only for losses that were foreseeable, when the contract was made, as a probable result of breach. A loss is foreseeable if it arises naturally, in the ordinary course of things, from such a breach (general damages), or if it results from special circumstances the breaching party had reason to know of when contracting (special or consequential damages).

**Elements:** 1. Causation. 2. Foreseeability at the time of contracting, either in the ordinary course or through notice of special circumstances. 3. Certainty (R-CTR-093). 4. Mitigation (R-CTR-095).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Under Article 2, a buyer recovers consequential losses only if cover or other means could not reasonably have prevented them (§ 2-715(2)(a)). A seller has no consequential damages under Article 2, only incidental damages (§ 2-710). A clause excluding consequential damages is valid unless unconscionable (R-CTR-097).

**Authority:** *Hadley v. Baxendale* (1854) 9 Ex. 341, 156 Eng. Rep. 145; R2d § 351; UCC § 2-715(2); Cal. Civ. Code § 3300; *Lewis Jorge Construction Management, Inc. v. Pomona Unified School Dist.* (2004) 34 Cal.4th 960 [S-209].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. California follows *Hadley* through Civil Code § 3300 and *Lewis Jorge*, which treats lost profits on future contracts as special damages that must have been foreseeable at formation [S-209] [VERIFY V-103].

**Example:** An oven supplier delivers 10 days late. The bakery's lost profits on ordinary sales are foreseeable to an oven supplier. Its lost $200,000 catering contract, which the supplier never knew of, is not.

### R-CTR-093 — Certainty
**Rule (write this):** Damages must be proved with reasonable certainty. The plaintiff need not prove the amount precisely, but speculative losses are not recoverable. An established business can usually prove lost profits from its track record. Lost profits of a new or unestablished business were traditionally barred and today are recoverable only on reasonably certain evidence, such as comparable businesses or reliable projections. Where expectation is too uncertain, the plaintiff may recover reliance damages instead.

**Elements:** 1. A rational basis for the amount. 2. Fact of loss proved with reasonable certainty.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Nominal damages where no loss is proved (R-CTR-098).

**Authority:** R2d §§ 349, 352; Cal. Civ. Code § 3301.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A promoter's planned first-ever festival is cancelled when the venue breaches. Its projected profits are likely too speculative, so it recovers its reliance expenditures.

### R-CTR-094 — Incidental damages
**Rule (write this):** Incidental damages are the reasonable costs the injured party incurs in responding to the breach, such as inspecting, transporting, storing and caring for rejected goods, and arranging cover or resale. They are recoverable in addition to direct damages.

**Elements:** 1. Expenses caused by the breach. 2. Reasonable in amount.

**Exceptions / defenses:** —

**Authority:** UCC §§ 2-710, 2-715(1); R2d § 347.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A buyer pays $400 to store rejected goods and $250 in brokerage fees to find substitutes. Both are incidental damages.

### R-CTR-095 — Mitigation (avoidable consequences)
**Rule (write this):** The injured party cannot recover for losses it could have avoided by reasonable efforts, without undue risk, burden or humiliation, after learning of the breach. It can recover the reasonable costs of trying to mitigate, even if the attempt fails. An injured party that keeps performing after a clear repudiation cannot recover the added costs.

**Elements:** 1. A loss avoidable by reasonable effort. 2. The breaching party proves the failure to mitigate. 3. Reasonable mitigation costs are recoverable.

**Exceptions / defenses:** The standard is reasonableness, not perfection. An employee need not accept a different or inferior job. A lost-volume seller's resale does not reduce its damages (R-CTR-100).

**Authority:** R2d § 350; *Rockingham County v. Luten Bridge Co.*; *Parker v. Twentieth Century-Fox Film Corp.*; UCC §§ 2-704(2), 2-712, 2-715(2)(a).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** After the county repudiates a bridge contract, the builder keeps building. It cannot recover for the work done after the repudiation.

### R-CTR-096 — Liquidated damages vs. penalty
**Rule (write this):** A contract term fixing damages for breach is enforceable if the amount is reasonable in light of the anticipated or actual loss caused by the breach and the difficulty of proving that loss. A term fixing an unreasonably large amount is an unenforceable penalty, and the injured party is limited to its proven actual damages.

**Elements:** Traditional test: 1. Damages were difficult to estimate when contracting. 2. The amount was a reasonable forecast. 3. Its purpose was compensation, not punishment. The parties' label ("penalty" or "liquidated damages") does not control.

**Exceptions / defenses:** If the clause is valid, the plaintiff recovers the stated amount even if actual damages are lower (majority); some courts refuse where there was no loss at all. A party cannot recover both liquidated and actual damages for the same breach.

**Authority:** R2d § 356; UCC § 2-718(1); Cal. Civ. Code § 1671 [S-200].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** differs on the burden. Under Civil Code § 1671(b), a liquidated damages clause in most contracts is valid unless the challenger proves it was unreasonable under the circumstances existing when the contract was made. Under § 1671(c)–(d), in specified consumer contracts and residential leases, it is void unless fixing actual damages would be impracticable or extremely difficult [S-200] [VERIFY V-107].

**Example:** A construction contract sets $1,000 per day of delay, roughly the owner's hard-to-predict lost rent; that is valid. $50,000 per day on a one-week job is a penalty.

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: a PRC court can adjust 违约金 up or down; a US court does not rewrite the clause, but enforces it or strikes it as a penalty (approx.).

### R-CTR-097 — Limitation of remedies (UCC § 2-719)
**Rule (write this):** An Article 2 contract may limit the buyer's remedies, for example to repair or replacement of defective parts, but the limited remedy is exclusive only if the parties expressly agree that it is. If an exclusive or limited remedy fails of its essential purpose, for instance because the seller cannot or will not repair within a reasonable time, the buyer may use the Code's general remedies. Consequential damages may be limited or excluded unless that is unconscionable: limiting damages for personal injury from consumer goods is prima facie unconscionable, while limiting commercial loss is not.

**Elements:** 1. Is the limited remedy expressly exclusive? 2. Did it fail of its essential purpose? 3. Is the consequential-damages exclusion unconscionable?

**Exceptions / defenses:** Courts differ on whether a consequential-damages exclusion survives when the repair remedy fails.

**Authority:** UCC § 2-719.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A tractor warranty limits the remedy to "repair or replacement of parts, exclusively." After five failed repair attempts in two months, the remedy has failed of its essential purpose, and the buyer may revoke acceptance and seek damages.

### R-CTR-098 — No punitive or emotional-distress damages; nominal damages; attorney's fees
**Rule (write this):** Punitive damages are not recoverable for breach of contract unless the conduct is also an independent tort for which punitive damages are available. Damages for emotional distress are excluded unless the breach also caused bodily harm or the contract or breach is of a kind that makes serious emotional disturbance particularly likely. A plaintiff who proves breach but no loss recovers nominal damages. Each side pays its own attorney's fees unless a contract or statute provides otherwise.

**Elements:** Check for an independent tort, an emotionally sensitive contract (for example, funeral services), or a fee clause or fee statute.

**Exceptions / defenses:** —

**Authority:** R2d §§ 346(2), 353, 355; Cal. Civ. Code §§ 1717, 3294, 3360; *Erlich v. Menezes* (1999) 21 Cal.4th 543.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. In California, Civil Code § 1717 makes a one-sided contractual attorney-fee clause reciprocal, and *Erlich* denies emotional-distress damages for the negligent construction of a home.

**Example:** A homeowner distraught over a leaky new house recovers repair costs but not damages for distress.

## UCC seller's and buyer's remedies

### R-CTR-099 — Seller's remedies under the UCC
**Rule (write this):** When the buyer wrongfully rejects, wrongfully revokes acceptance, fails to pay or repudiates, the seller may withhold or stop delivery, identify conforming goods to the contract, cancel, and recover either (1) the contract price minus the price obtained in a commercially reasonable resale (§ 2-706) or (2) the contract price minus the market price at the time and place for tender (§ 2-708(1)), in each case plus incidental damages and minus expenses saved; or, in limited cases, (3) the full price (§ 2-709). If the seller discovers the buyer is insolvent, it may refuse delivery except for cash and, within limits, reclaim goods already delivered (§ 2-702).

**Elements:** Resale must be in good faith and commercially reasonable. A private resale requires reasonable notice to the buyer. The seller keeps any profit made on resale.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Article 2 gives the seller no consequential damages.

**Authority:** UCC §§ 2-702, 2-703, 2-704, 2-705, 2-706, 2-708(1), 2-710.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A buyer repudiates a $10,000 contract. The seller resells in good faith, after notice, for $8,000 and spends $300 on storage. The seller recovers $2,300, less any expenses saved.

### R-CTR-100 — Lost-volume seller
**Rule (write this):** Where the market-price measure would not put the seller in as good a position as performance, typically because the seller has an unlimited supply and would have made both the broken sale and the resale (a lost-volume seller), the seller recovers the profit, including reasonable overhead, it would have made from full performance, plus incidental damages.

**Elements:** 1. Capacity to make both sales. 2. Both sales would have been profitable. 3. The resale would have happened anyway.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A seller of a unique item is not a lost-volume seller.

**Authority:** UCC § 2-708(2); *Neri v. Retail Marine Corp.*, 30 N.Y.2d 393, 285 N.E.2d 311 (1972).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A boat dealer with plenty of stock sells a boat; the buyer backs out, and the dealer sells the same boat to another customer at the same price. The dealer still recovers its lost profit on the first sale.

### R-CTR-101 — Action for the price
**Rule (write this):** The seller may recover the full contract price, plus incidental damages, only (1) for goods the buyer has accepted; (2) for conforming goods lost or damaged within a commercially reasonable time after the risk of loss passed to the buyer; or (3) for goods identified to the contract that the seller cannot resell at a reasonable price after reasonable effort, such as custom-made goods. The seller must hold any goods still in its control for the buyer.

**Elements:** One of the three situations.

**Exceptions / defenses:** —

**Authority:** UCC § 2-709.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A buyer refuses 500 uniforms embroidered with its own logo. The seller cannot resell them and recovers the full price.

### R-CTR-102 — Buyer's remedies: cover and market damages
**Rule (write this):** When the seller fails to deliver or repudiates, or the buyer rightfully rejects or justifiably revokes acceptance, the buyer may cancel, recover any price paid, and either (1) cover, by making in good faith and without unreasonable delay a reasonable purchase of substitute goods, and recover the cover price minus the contract price; or (2) recover the market price when the buyer learned of the breach minus the contract price. In either case the buyer adds incidental and consequential damages and subtracts expenses saved. If the goods are unique, or cover is unavailable, the buyer may seek specific performance or replevin.

**Elements:** 1. A triggering breach. 2. Cover (reasonable, prompt, in good faith) or market. 3. Market price is measured at the place of tender, or at the place of arrival after rejection or revocation.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Failing to cover does not bar market damages, but it can bar consequential losses that cover would have prevented. A buyer holding rejected goods has a security interest in them for payments made and expenses (§ 2-711(3)).

**Authority:** UCC §§ 2-711, 2-712, 2-713, 2-715, 2-716.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** The contract price is $20,000. The seller fails to deliver, and the buyer reasonably buys substitutes for $23,000 within a week. The buyer recovers $3,000 plus incidental damages.

### R-CTR-103 — Buyer's damages for accepted goods
**Rule (write this):** A buyer who has accepted goods and given timely notice may recover damages for the nonconformity. For breach of warranty, the measure is the value the goods would have had as warranted minus their actual value, both measured at the time and place of acceptance, plus incidental and consequential damages, including personal injury and property damage proximately caused by the breach. On notice to the seller, the buyer may deduct these damages from any part of the price still due.

**Elements:** 1. Acceptance. 2. Notice (§ 2-607(3)). 3. Value as warranted minus actual value. 4. Incidental and consequential damages.

**Exceptions / defenses:** —

**Authority:** UCC §§ 2-714, 2-715, 2-717.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A generator warranted at 10 kW produces 7 kW. As warranted it was worth $12,000; as delivered, $8,000. The buyer who keeps it recovers $4,000.

## V.E–V.H Equitable and restitutionary remedies

### R-CTR-104 — Specific performance and injunction
**Rule (write this):** Specific performance is available when (1) there is a valid contract with sufficiently certain terms; (2) the plaintiff has performed, or is ready, willing and able to perform, its conditions; (3) the legal remedy is inadequate, as for land, unique goods, or losses that cannot be measured with reasonable certainty; (4) enforcement is feasible; and (5) no equitable defense applies, such as laches, unclean hands, unconscionability, mistake, or sale to a bona fide purchaser. Courts will not order specific performance of a personal-services contract, but may enjoin a person with unique skills from working for a competitor during the contract term (a negative injunction) if that is reasonable.

**Elements:** Mnemonic **CCIFD**: Contract; Conditions satisfied; Inadequate legal remedy; Feasibility; no Defenses.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Modern courts do not require mutuality of remedy if the plaintiff's own performance is assured.

**Authority:** R2d §§ 357, 359, 360, 364, 367; UCC § 2-716; Cal. Civ. Code §§ 3384, 3386, 3387, 3390, 3391 [VERIFY V-108]; *Lumley v. Wagner* (1852) 1 De G.M. & G. 604.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. California presumes damages inadequate for breach of an agreement to transfer real property, and the presumption is conclusive for a single-family home the buyer intends to occupy (§ 3387). Specific performance is unavailable against a party who did not receive adequate consideration or for whom the contract is not just and reasonable (§ 3391) [VERIFY V-108].

**Example:** A collector agreed to buy a one-of-a-kind antique chess piece. The seller refuses to deliver. Damages are inadequate for a unique chattel, so specific performance is available (UCC § 2-716).

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: 继续履行 is an ordinary remedy in PRC law; US specific performance is exceptional and equitable (approx.).

### R-CTR-105 — Rescission as a remedy
**Rule (write this):** Rescission unwinds the contract and restores the parties to their pre-contract positions. It is available for any ground that makes a contract voidable (misrepresentation, qualifying mistake, duress, undue influence, incapacity), as an alternative to damages for a material breach, and by mutual agreement. The rescinding party must act promptly after discovering the ground, give notice, and return or offer to return what it received. It loses the right if it affirms the contract after learning the facts, delays unreasonably, or third parties acquire rights.

**Elements:** 1. A ground. 2. Prompt notice. 3. Restoration or an offer to restore. 4. No affirmation, unreasonable delay or bona fide purchaser.

**Exceptions / defenses:** The injured party generally elects between rescission and expectation damages. Under the UCC, rescission for fraud does not bar damages (§ 2-721).

**Authority:** R2d §§ 7, 164, 175, 376, 380–381 [VERIFY V-111]; UCC § 2-721; Cal. Civ. Code §§ 1689, 1691, 1692.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. Under Civil Code § 1691, the rescinding party must promptly give notice and restore, or offer to restore, everything of value it received.

**Example:** Two months after buying a warehouse, a buyer learns that the seller lied about its zoning. She promptly notifies the seller and offers to reconvey. Rescission with return of the price is available.

### R-CTR-106 — Reformation
**Rule (write this):** Where a writing fails to express the parties' actual agreement because of a mutual mistake in its expression, or because of one party's fraud, or a unilateral mistake the other party knew of, the court may reform the writing to state the agreement actually made. Reformation is not barred by the parol evidence rule, the statute of frauds, or the claimant's negligence, but it is denied where it would prejudice a good-faith purchaser or another third party who relied on the writing.

**Elements:** 1. A prior agreement. 2. A writing that departs from it. 3. Mutual mistake in expression, or fraud. 4. Clear and convincing proof (most courts). 5. No prejudice to a bona fide third party.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Laches; a bona fide purchaser.

**Authority:** R2d §§ 155, 157, 166; Cal. Civ. Code § 3399.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** The parties agree on a price of $250,000, and the typist writes $205,000. The seller may reform the deed of sale.

### R-CTR-107 — Reliance damages
**Rule (write this):** As an alternative to expectation damages, the injured party may recover its reliance interest: expenditures made in preparing for or performing the contract, less any loss the breaching party proves the injured party would have suffered had the contract been performed.

**Elements:** 1. Expenditures in reliance. 2. A deduction for a proven losing contract.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A party cannot recover both expectation and reliance damages for the same loss.

**Authority:** R2d §§ 344(b), 349; *Sullivan v. O'Connor*, 363 Mass. 579, 296 N.E.2d 183 (1973).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. Reliance is the usual measure for promissory estoppel and for uncertain profits.

**Example:** A franchisee spends $60,000 preparing a site before the franchisor wrongfully cancels. Its profits are too speculative to prove, so it recovers the $60,000.

### R-CTR-108 — Restitution for the non-breaching party
**Rule (write this):** A party injured by a total breach or repudiation may, instead of damages, recover restitution of any benefit it conferred by part performance or reliance. The measure is the reasonable value of the benefit (what it would cost to obtain it from someone in the claimant's position) or, where appropriate, the increase in the other party's wealth. Restitution is not available where the injured party has fully performed and the breaching party's only remaining duty is to pay a definite sum of money.

**Elements:** 1. A total breach or repudiation. 2. A benefit conferred. 3. Measurement.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Restitution is also available where the contract is unenforceable under the statute of frauds, voidable, or discharged by impracticability or frustration (R2d §§ 375–377). Courts split on whether restitution may exceed the contract price.

**Authority:** R2d §§ 344(c), 370, 371, 373, 375–377.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A contractor builds 40% of a garage before the owner repudiates. The contract would have lost money, so the contractor elects restitution of the reasonable value of the work done.

### R-CTR-109 — Restitution for the breaching party
**Rule (write this):** A party who has materially breached and cannot recover on the contract may still recover in restitution the value of any benefit it conferred in excess of the loss its breach caused. Under the UCC, a buyer whose breach leads the seller to withhold delivery recovers its payments beyond the amount of a valid liquidated-damages clause or, absent such a clause, beyond the lesser of 20% of the total price or $500, subject to offset for the seller's proven damages.

**Elements:** 1. Benefit conferred. 2. Minus the injured party's damages.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Some courts deny recovery to a willful breacher; the modern trend allows it. A valid liquidated-damages clause or forfeitable deposit limits recovery.

**Authority:** R2d § 374; *Britton v. Turner*, 6 N.H. 481 (1834); UCC § 2-718(2)–(3); *Freedman v. Rector, Wardens & Vestrymen of St. Matthias Parish* (1951) 37 Cal.2d 16 [S-210]; Cal. Civ. Code § 3275.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. In California, even a willfully defaulting land buyer can recover the part of its deposit that exceeds the seller's damages (*Freedman*) [S-210].

**Example:** A buyer pays a $2,000 deposit on $15,000 of goods and then repudiates. There is no liquidated-damages clause, and the seller resells at no loss. The buyer recovers $1,500: $2,000 minus the lesser of $3,000 (20%) or $500.

## VI Third-party rights

### R-CTR-110 — Third-party beneficiaries: intended vs. incidental
**Rule (write this):** A third party may enforce a contract only if it is an intended beneficiary, meaning the contracting parties intended it to benefit, either because performance will satisfy a debt the promisee owes it (a creditor beneficiary) or because the promisee intends to give it the benefit as a gift (a donee beneficiary). An incidental beneficiary, who benefits only indirectly, has no rights under the contract.

**Elements:** Factors: the beneficiary is named; performance runs directly to it; its relationship to the promisee; express language. California test (*Goonewardene*): (1) the third party would in fact benefit; (2) a motivating purpose of the contracting parties was to benefit it; and (3) letting it sue is consistent with the contract's objectives and the parties' reasonable expectations [S-203].

**Exceptions / defenses:** Members of the public under government contracts are usually incidental beneficiaries.

**Authority:** R2d § 302; *Lawrence v. Fox*, 20 N.Y. 268 (1859); *Seaver v. Ransom*, 224 N.Y. 233, 120 N.E. 639 (1918); *H.R. Moch Co. v. Rensselaer Water Co.*, 247 N.Y. 160, 159 N.E. 896 (1928); Cal. Civ. Code § 1559; *Goonewardene v. ADP, LLC* (2019) 6 Cal.5th 817 [S-203].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** use § 302 on the MBE; on a California essay add the *Goonewardene* test [S-203].

**Example:** B buys A's business and promises A to pay A's $50,000 debt to C. C is an intended (creditor) beneficiary and may sue B.

### R-CTR-111 — Third-party beneficiaries: vesting, defenses and who may sue
**Rule (write this):** Until an intended beneficiary's rights vest, the promisor and promisee may modify or rescind the contract without the beneficiary's consent. Rights vest when the beneficiary (1) manifests assent at the request of the promisor or promisee, (2) sues on the promise, or (3) materially changes position in justifiable reliance. The beneficiary may sue the promisor, who may raise against it any defense arising from the contract that it could raise against the promisee. A creditor beneficiary may also sue the promisee on the original debt, and the promisee may sue the promisor for breach.

**Elements:** 1. Vesting. 2. Defenses from the contract (fraud, failure of consideration, nonperformance by the promisee). 3. The correct defendant.

**Exceptions / defenses:** The contract may reserve a power to change the beneficiary (life insurance). The promisor cannot assert against the beneficiary its claims or defenses against the promisee that arise outside the contract (e.g., set-off of an unrelated debt).

**Authority:** R2d §§ 304, 309, 311.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** Before C learns of the deal, A and B agree that B will pay A instead of C. C's rights had not vested, so the modification binds C.

### R-CTR-112 — Assignment of rights: what is assignable
**Rule (write this):** A party may assign its contract rights unless the assignment would materially change the obligor's duty, materially increase its burden or risk, or materially impair its chance of receiving return performance; is forbidden by statute or public policy; or is validly precluded by the contract. An assignment requires a present intent to transfer the right. No writing or consideration is needed unless another rule requires one, and partial assignments are allowed.

**Elements:** 1. A present transfer. 2. An assignable right. 3. No effective prohibition.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Not assignable: rights to personal services, and some rights under requirements contracts and insurance. Rights to payment of money are freely assignable (UCC § 9-406(d)).

**Authority:** R2d §§ 317, 324; UCC § 2-210; Cal. Civ. Code § 954.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A contractor assigns to its bank the right to receive $100,000 due from the owner. The assignment is valid without the owner's consent.

### R-CTR-113 — Anti-assignment clauses
**Rule (write this):** A clause prohibiting assignment of "the contract" is read, unless the circumstances indicate otherwise, as barring only delegation of duties. A clause prohibiting assignment of rights generally gives the obligor a damages claim if the right is assigned, but does not make the assignment ineffective unless the clause expressly says that any assignment is void. Clauses restricting assignment of rights to payment are generally ineffective.

**Elements:** 1. The clause's wording ("void" or "invalid" versus a bare prohibition). 2. The type of right.

**Exceptions / defenses:** —

**Authority:** R2d § 322; UCC §§ 2-210, 9-406(d).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A clause says "This contract may not be assigned." The seller assigns its right to payment to a factor. The assignment is effective, and the clause bars only delegation of the seller's duties.

### R-CTR-114 — Assignee's rights, defenses and notice
**Rule (write this):** The assignee stands in the assignor's shoes. It takes the right subject to every defense and claim the obligor has against the assignor that arises from the contract, and to any other defense or claim that accrues before the obligor receives notice of the assignment. Until the obligor receives notice, it may pay or otherwise deal with the assignor and be discharged; after notice, payment to the assignor does not discharge it.

**Elements:** 1. The defense's source and timing. 2. When the obligor received notice.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A waiver-of-defense clause or the holder-in-due-course doctrine may protect the assignee (outside the core outline).

**Authority:** R2d §§ 336, 338; UCC §§ 9-404, 9-406(a).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** The owner pays the contractor in full before learning of the assignment to the bank. The owner is discharged, and the bank must look to the contractor.

### R-CTR-115 — Gratuitous and successive assignments
**Rule (write this):** An assignment for value is irrevocable. A gratuitous assignment is revocable by the assignor's death, by a later assignment of the same right, or by notice to the assignee or the obligor, unless the assignee has received performance, the assignment is in a signed writing or accompanied by delivery of a symbolic document, or the assignee has reasonably relied. Between successive assignees for value, the first generally prevails, unless a later assignee for value without notice first obtains payment, a judgment, a novation, or delivery of a tangible token (majority rule); some states follow a first-to-give-notice rule.

**Elements:** 1. For value or gratuitous? 2. Revocation events. 3. Priority.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Article 9 filing rules govern priority for sales of accounts.

**Authority:** R2d §§ 332, 342.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A orally gives his niece his right to a $5,000 payment from B, then dies before B pays. The gratuitous oral assignment is revoked by A's death.

### R-CTR-116 — Delegation of duties
**Rule (write this):** A party may delegate performance of its duties unless the obligee has a substantial interest in having the original promisor perform (personal skill, judgment or trust), the contract prohibits delegation, or a statute or public policy forbids it. Delegation does not release the delegating party, which remains liable if the delegate fails to perform, unless the obligee agrees to a novation. The delegate becomes liable to the obligee only if it assumes the duty, that is, promises for consideration to perform it, which makes the obligee an intended beneficiary of that promise.

**Elements:** 1. A delegable duty. 2. No prohibition. 3. Assumption by the delegate (for the delegate's liability). 4. A novation (for the delegator's release).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Under the UCC, the obligee may treat a delegation as reasonable grounds for insecurity and demand assurance from the delegate.

**Authority:** R2d §§ 318, 328; UCC § 2-210.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A famous portrait painter cannot delegate a commission to a student, but a lawn-mowing company can delegate routine mowing, and it remains liable if the delegate does a poor job.

## California overlay: tort and contract

### R-CTR-117 — Economic loss rule and fraud during performance (California)
**Rule (write this):** A contracting party generally cannot recover tort damages for a mere breach of contract; the economic loss rule confines purely economic losses from a broken promise to contract remedies. A tort claim lies where the conduct also violates an independent duty, for example fraud that induced the contract or, under *Rattagan*, fraudulent concealment during performance, if the tort's elements are established independently of the contract and the conduct exposes the plaintiff to a risk of harm beyond what the parties contemplated when contracting.

**Elements:** 1. A breach. 2. An independent tort duty, with every element established apart from the contract. 3. Harm beyond what the parties contemplated.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Merely failing to perform as promised, even intentionally, is not a tort.

**Authority:** *Robinson Helicopter Co., Inc. v. Dana Corp.* (2004) 34 Cal.4th 979; *Rattagan v. Uber Technologies, Inc.* (2024) 17 Cal.5th 1 [S-132]; *Erlich v. Menezes* (1999) 21 Cal.4th 543; Cal. Civ. Code § 3294.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** on the MBE, contract remedies only, with no punitive damages for breach (R2d § 355). On a California essay, raise the tort claim when the facts show deceit, and note that punitive damages are available for the tort (Civ. Code § 3294).

**Example:** A parts supplier knowingly ships parts with falsified inspection certificates while performing the contract, causing the buyer to install unsafe components. The buyer may sue in fraud as well as contract.
