# Contracts — comprehensive outline

**What this file is.** This is the complete reference for Contracts. It follows the NCBE MBE Subject Matter Outline numbering (I–VI) [S-040] and opens with the State Bar essay outline's "introductory principles" section [S-088] [VERIFY V-024].
- Each topic gives the rules with authority, the splits, and how the topic is tested.
- Essay-ready wording lives in [`rule-statements.md`](rule-statements.md); rule IDs (R-CTR-###) point there.
- California points are flagged **CA note**. The full comparison table is in [`ca-distinctions.md`](ca-distinctions.md).

**Two rules of the road.**
1. **MBE.** Assume the Official Text of UCC Articles 1 and 2 applies where appropriate. About a quarter of the 25 scored Contracts questions are UCC-based, and about half come from categories I (formation) and IV (performance, breach, discharge) [S-040].
2. **California essays.** Answer under general common-law principles plus UCC Article 2 unless the call asks for California law [S-077]. California's Commercial Code mirrors Article 2 section by section (UCC § 2-207 = Com. Code § 2207). A one-line "In California, …" adds credit where the law differs.

**Abbreviations.** R2d = Restatement (Second) of Contracts; UCC = Uniform Commercial Code (Official Text); Civ. Code / CCP = California Civil Code / Code of Civil Procedure; K = contract; Π/Δ = plaintiff/defendant.

## Introductory principles/applicable law (UCC Article 2 vs. common law)

### Sources of contract law
- **Common law.** Judge-made law, summarized (but not enacted) in the **Restatement (Second) of Contracts**. The MBE follows the R2d where it states a majority or modern rule.
- **UCC Article 1** (general provisions) and **Article 2** (sales of goods). They are enacted statutes. Unless displaced by a particular provision, the common law and equity supplement the Code (UCC § 1-103(b)).
- **California.** Civil Code Division 3 (§§ 1427 ff.: obligations, contracts, damages, specific performance) codifies much of the common law. The California Commercial Code, Division 2, enacts Article 2. Essays use general principles unless the call says otherwise [S-077].
- **UCC Article 9 (fixtures) — scope note.** The State Bar essay outline is reported to list Article 9 as to fixtures only [S-088] [VERIFY V-024]. A fixture is a good that has become so related to particular real property that an interest in it arises under real-property law (UCC § 9-102(a)(41)). Priority between a fixture secured party and real-property claimants is governed by UCC § 9-334. On a Contracts essay, the practical point is narrower: a contract to sell minerals or a structure **to be severed by the seller**, or growing crops or timber, is a sale of goods (UCC § 2-107).

### Does Article 2 apply? (R-CTR-001)
1. **Goods.** All things movable at the time of identification to the contract (UCC § 2-105(1)). This includes specially manufactured goods, unborn young of animals, growing crops and timber to be cut (§ 2-107). It excludes money in which the price is paid, investment securities, things in action, real estate, services and intangibles.
2. **Sale.** The passing of title from seller to buyer for a price (§ 2-106(1)). Leases of goods fall under Article 2A, which is outside the MBE outline.
3. **Mixed (hybrid) contracts.**
   - **Predominant purpose test** (majority): the whole contract follows its predominant purpose (*Bonebrake v. Cox*, 499 F.2d 951 (8th Cir. 1974)). Factors: the contract language ("buyer," "seller," "goods" vs. "services"); the supplier's business; the relative value of goods and services; how the price is allocated.
   - **Gravamen test** (minority): apply the law that fits the part of the contract in dispute.
   - **2022 UCC amendments:** for a hybrid transaction whose goods aspects do not predominate, apply Article 2 only to the provisions relating primarily to the goods [S-213]. Whether California has enacted this, and whether the MBE tests it, is unconfirmed [VERIFY V-102].
   - **Exam move:** say both tests if they point different ways, then pick one.
4. **Merchants** (R-CTR-002; UCC § 2-104). Some rules apply only to merchants:
   - firm offers (§ 2-205);
   - additional terms between merchants (§ 2-207(2));
   - merchant's confirmation (§ 2-201(2));
   - merchantability (§ 2-314);
   - risk of loss (§ 2-509(3));
   - rejected goods (§ 2-603);
   - commercial-standards good faith.

### Types of contracts and terminology
| Term | Meaning | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Bilateral | Promise for a promise | Formed when the return promise is made |
| Unilateral | Promise for performance | Formed only on full performance; option arises once performance begins (R-CTR-010) |
| Express / implied-in-fact | Assent by words / by conduct | Both are real contracts (R-CTR-018) |
| Quasi-contract | Implied in law; restitution | Remedy for unjust enrichment, not a contract |
| Void | No contract at all (e.g., illegal subject matter, fraud in the factum, physical duress) | Neither party can enforce |
| Voidable | Valid until avoided by the protected party (minor, fraud victim, duress victim) | Protected party may ratify |
| Unenforceable | Valid but cannot be enforced in court (e.g., statute of frauds) | Restitution still available |

### How tested
- **Essays.** Governing law is almost always the **first heading**. It appears in about a third of the past Contracts essays indexed in Phase 3 (Jul 2014 Q1, Feb 2015 Q1, Feb 2016 Q6, Jul 2022 Q1, Feb 2024 Q5) [VERIFY V-071].
  - Mixed goods/services fact patterns (landscape design plus plants; a cooler built and installed) reward a two-sided predominant-purpose analysis.
- **MBE.** The question often turns on spotting that the UCC applies: no consideration is needed for a modification, there is no mirror-image rule, and firm offers bind without consideration.

## I Formation of contracts (essay: offer, acceptance, consideration)

### I.A Mutual assent (offer, acceptance, termination; unilateral, bilateral and implied-in-fact contracts)

#### I.A.1 Objective theory (R-CTR-003)
- Assent is judged by **outward manifestations** as a reasonable person in the other party's position would understand them (R2d §§ 18, 19; *Lucy v. Zehmer*, 196 Va. 493 (1954)).
- **Intent to be legally bound** is not required, but a manifest intent **not** to be bound prevents a contract (R2d § 21). Examples: "This is not a binding agreement"; a letter of intent "subject to a definitive agreement."
- If the parties agree on terms but plan to sign a formal writing later, a contract may already exist. The question is whether they intended to be bound before signing (R2d § 27). Factors: the size and complexity of the deal, whether terms remain open, and usage.
- **Social and family arrangements** raise a factual question of intent.

#### I.A.2 Offer (R-CTR-004, R-CTR-005)
- **Definition (R2d § 24):** a manifestation of willingness to bargain that justifies the offeree in understanding that its assent is invited and will conclude the bargain.
- **Three tests:**
  1. **Commitment.** "I will sell," not "I am thinking of selling" or "Would you pay…?"
  2. **Definiteness.** See I.B.
  3. **Communication** to the offeree. A person who acts without knowing of an offer (for example, returns a lost dog unaware of a reward) cannot accept it.
- **Invitations to deal:**
  - price quotes, catalogs, circulars and most advertisements (R2d § 26);
  - **exception:** an ad that is specific, leaves nothing to negotiate, and limits who may accept (*Lefkowitz*, 251 Minn. 188 (1957));
  - an ad no reasonable person would take seriously (*Leonard v. Pepsico*, 88 F. Supp. 2d 116 (S.D.N.Y. 1999)).
  - A **price quotation** may be an offer if it is detailed, addressed to a particular buyer, and invites immediate acceptance.
- **CA note:** *Donovan v. RRL Corp.* (2001) 26 Cal.4th 261 treated a dealer's newspaper ad as an offer, in light of a Vehicle Code provision on advertised vehicle prices [S-205].
- **Auctions** (R2d § 28; UCC § 2-328):
  - the bidder is the offeror and the fall of the hammer is acceptance;
  - **with reserve** is the default: the auctioneer may withdraw goods until the hammer falls;
  - **without reserve:** once bids are called for, the article cannot be withdrawn unless no bid is made within a reasonable time;
  - a bidder may retract its bid until the hammer falls.
- **Rewards:** an offer for a unilateral contract. The offeree must know of the offer; most courts deny recovery to someone who acted in ignorance of it.

#### I.A.3 Termination of the power of acceptance (R-CTR-006, R-CTR-007)
| Method | Effective when | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Rejection | Received (R2d § 38, § 40) | Words or conduct showing the offeree won't accept |
| Counteroffer | Received | Rejection plus a new offer (R2d § 39). An inquiry or a "request for a better deal" while keeping the offer under advisement is **not** a rejection. Under the UCC, § 2-207 may make a reply with new terms an acceptance. |
| Lapse | At the stated time, or after a reasonable time | Conversation offers lapse at the end of the conversation (R2d § 41). A late acceptance is a counteroffer (§ 70). |
| Revocation | Received by the offeree (§ 42) | Indirect: reliable information of the offeror's inconsistent action (§ 43; *Dickinson v. Dodds*). Public offers: equal publicity (§ 46). |
| Death or incapacity of either party | Immediately, without notice (§ 48) | Does not apply to option contracts. **CA note:** Civ. Code § 1587 lists "death or insanity of the proposer" [S-212] |
| Destruction of the subject matter; supervening illegality | Immediately | |

- **Revocability default:** an offer is freely revocable, even if it says "open for 10 days," unless one of the four irrevocability doctrines applies (I.A.4).
- **Exam fact pattern:** an offer mailed, then the offeror dies, then the acceptance is mailed. No contract, because death terminated the offer. Past California essays have tested the effect of a party's death on a pending deal and on contracts mailed after death (Jul 2019 Q5) [VERIFY V-071].

#### I.A.4 Irrevocable offers
1. **Option contract** (R-CTR-008).
   - Consideration for the promise to hold open; R2d § 87(1)(a) also enforces a signed writing reciting a purported consideration and a fair exchange.
   - Acceptance takes effect on **receipt** (§ 63(b)).
   - Rejection does not end the option (§ 37).
2. **Merchant's firm offer** (R-CTR-009; UCC § 2-205).
   - Merchant + signed writing + assurance + goods.
   - Irrevocable for the stated time or a reasonable time, but no more than 3 months.
   - A form supplied by the offeree must be separately signed by the offeror.
3. **Part performance of a unilateral offer** (R-CTR-010; R2d § 45).
   - An option arises once the offeree begins (not merely prepares) performance.
   - The offeree is not bound to finish.
   - The offeror's duty is conditional on completion.
4. **Reasonable detrimental reliance** (R-CTR-011; R2d § 87(2)).
   - Applies where the offeror should foresee substantial reliance before acceptance.
   - Classic case: the subcontractor bid (*Drennan v. Star Paving Co.* (1958) 51 Cal.2d 409), contrasted with *James Baird Co. v. Gimbel Bros.*, 64 F.2d 344 (2d Cir. 1933).
   - The general contractor is not bound to the subcontractor and can still bid-shop, but reopening negotiations may forfeit protection.

#### I.A.5 Acceptance (R-CTR-012, R-CTR-016, R-CTR-017)
- **Who:** only the offeree (or members of the class the offer invites). Offers are personal and cannot be assigned; options can be.
- **Knowledge:** the offeree must know of the offer when acting.
- **Manner:** the offeror is master of the offer. If the offer prescribes an exclusive manner, only that manner works. Otherwise, any reasonable manner is fine (R2d § 30).
  - **Ambiguous offers** invite acceptance by promise **or** performance (§ 32). Beginning performance accepts and implies a promise to finish (§ 62).
  - **Offers seeking performance only** are unilateral: acceptance occurs on complete performance, with an option once begun (§ 45).
  - **Notice:** for acceptance by performance, notice is needed if the offeror has no adequate means of learning of the performance (§ 54). Acceptance by promise must be communicated (§ 56).
- **Mirror-image rule** (common law): the acceptance must match the offer exactly. Additional or different terms make it a counteroffer (§§ 58, 59). Exceptions:
  - "I accept; please ship by Friday if possible" (a request, not a condition) is an acceptance (§ 61);
  - terms the law already implies are not "new."
  - **CA note:** Civ. Code § 1585 (a qualified acceptance is a new proposal).
- **Silence** (R2d § 69) is not acceptance except:
  1. taking the benefit of services with a reasonable opportunity to reject and reason to know compensation is expected;
  2. the offeror invited silence and the offeree intends to accept;
  3. prior dealings make notice of rejection reasonable;
  4. exercising dominion over offered goods.
  - Unordered merchandise sent by mail may be kept as a gift (39 U.S.C. § 3009).
- **UCC § 2-206:**
  - Acceptance in any manner reasonable in the circumstances.
  - An order for prompt shipment can be accepted by a prompt promise to ship or by prompt shipment.
  - Shipping **nonconforming** goods is acceptance **and** breach, unless the seller seasonably notifies the buyer that the shipment is an accommodation (then it is a counteroffer).
  - Beginning performance accepts, but the offeror may treat the offer as lapsed if not notified within a reasonable time (§ 2-206(2)).

#### I.A.6 The battle of the forms — UCC § 2-207 (R-CTR-013, R-CTR-014)
**Step 1 — Is there a contract on the writings?**
- A definite and seasonable expression of acceptance is an acceptance even with additional or different terms.
- **Exception:** the acceptance is "expressly made conditional on assent to the additional or different terms." Courts require clear language (*Dorton v. Collins & Aikman*, 453 F.2d 1161 (6th Cir. 1972)). If it is expressly conditional, no contract is formed on the writings; go to Step 3.
- A response that diverges on the dickered terms (price, quantity, item) is not a definite expression of acceptance.

**Step 2 — What are the terms?**
- **Additional terms** (§ 2-207(2)):
  - If either party is a non-merchant, the terms are proposals that the offeror must expressly accept.
  - Between merchants, the terms become part of the contract **unless** (a) the offer expressly limits acceptance to its terms, (b) they **materially alter** the contract (surprise or hardship), or (c) the offeror has objected or objects within a reasonable time.
  - Material alterations (cmt. 4): disclaimers of warranties, a high late-cancellation right, unusually short complaint periods.
  - Not material (cmt. 5): reasonable interest on overdue invoices, a reasonable time for complaints, seller's credit terms within trade practice.
  - Arbitration clauses: courts split.
- **Different (conflicting) terms:**
  - **Majority — knockout rule:** both conflicting terms drop out and Code gap fillers apply (*Daitom, Inc. v. Pennwalt Corp.*, 741 F.2d 1569 (10th Cir. 1984); cmt. 6).
  - Minority views: treat different terms like additional terms (the offer's term stands), or the offeror's term controls.
  - The **MBE** uses the knockout rule.

**Step 3 — Contract by conduct (§ 2-207(3)).** Where the writings do not form a contract but both parties act as though one exists (shipment, acceptance, payment), the terms are those on which the writings agree plus UCC gap fillers. The "last shot" doctrine does not apply.

**Rolling contracts ("terms later," shrink-wrap, click-wrap):**
- Some courts treat the vendor as the offeror and the buyer's retention of goods beyond a return period as acceptance of the enclosed terms (*ProCD, Inc. v. Zeidenberg*, 86 F.3d 1447 (7th Cir. 1996); *Hill v. Gateway 2000, Inc.*, 105 F.3d 1147 (7th Cir. 1997)).
- Others apply § 2-207 and treat the later terms as proposals (*Klocek v. Gateway, Inc.*, 104 F. Supp. 2d 1332 (D. Kan. 2000)).
- Online terms bind only if a reasonable user had notice and manifested assent (*Specht v. Netscape Communications Corp.*, 306 F.3d 17 (2d Cir. 2002)).

#### I.A.7 Timing — the mailbox rule (R-CTR-015)
| Communication | Effective | Authority |
|---|---|---|
| Acceptance (properly dispatched, reasonable medium) | On dispatch, even if lost | R2d §§ 63, 66; *Adams v. Lindsell* (1818); **CA** Civ. Code § 1583 [S-212] |
| Acceptance by an unreasonable medium or misaddressed | On receipt, if it arrives within the time a proper dispatch would have | R2d § 67 |
| Acceptance under an option | On receipt | R2d § 63(b) |
| Revocation | On receipt | R2d § 42; **CA** Civ. Code § 1587 [S-212] |
| Rejection / counteroffer | On receipt | R2d § 40 |
| Offer stating that acceptance is effective only on receipt | On receipt | Offeror is master of the offer |

**Tricky sequences:**
1. **Rejection sent, then acceptance sent.** Whichever **arrives first** controls (R2d § 40). A rejection-first sequence makes the later "acceptance" a counteroffer unless the acceptance arrives first.
2. **Acceptance sent, then rejection sent.** The contract is formed on dispatch. If the rejection arrives first and the offeror relies on it (for example, sells to someone else), the offeree is **estopped** from enforcing.
3. **Acceptance sent, then revocation received.** A contract exists, because the acceptance was effective on dispatch.
4. **Instantaneous communication** (phone, video) is treated like parties in each other's presence (R2d § 64). How the mailbox rule applies to **email** is unsettled [VERIFY V-114]. Read the facts for any stated rule.

#### I.A.8 Unilateral, bilateral, implied-in-fact contracts; quasi-contract (R-CTR-018)
- **Bilateral vs. unilateral.** Ask what the offer seeks: a promise, or a performance. Modern law presumes a bilateral or either-way offer in doubtful cases (R2d § 32). Unilateral offers show up mainly in rewards, prizes, "if you do X I will pay Y," and some employer bonus plans.
- **Implied-in-fact contract.** Assent inferred from conduct. Example: a patient accepts medical treatment with reason to know of the fee. It is enforced like an express contract (**CA:** Civ. Code §§ 1619–1621).
- **Quasi-contract (implied in law).** A restitution remedy for unjust enrichment, measured by the reasonable value of the benefit (Restatement (Third) of Restitution and Unjust Enrichment § 1). It is used:
  - when no contract exists;
  - when the contract is unenforceable (SOF, incapacity) or void;
  - when a party's performance is discharged.
  - **No recovery** for volunteers or officious intermeddlers, or where a valid express contract covers the same ground.
  - **Emergency services** are an exception: a professional who renders emergency aid recovers.

#### How I.A is tested
- **MBE:** sequencing (mailbox rule), revocation of offers that promise to stay open (only options and firm offers survive), unilateral contracts and § 45, § 2-207 between merchants, auctions and ads, acceptance by shipment of nonconforming goods.
- **Essays:** "Was a binding contract formed?" (Jul 2022 Q1; Jul 2019 Q5; Jul 2012 Q4) [VERIFY V-071].
  - Write offer → acceptance → consideration in order.
  - For goods, put § 2-207 and the merchant's firm offer under formation.

### I.B Indefiniteness or absence of terms (R-CTR-019, R-CTR-020, R-CTR-021)
- **Common law.** Terms must be reasonably certain, enough to identify a breach and give a remedy (R2d § 33).
  - Essential terms: the parties, the subject matter, the price, the quantity; for land, the description and price; for employment, the duration and pay may be implied (at-will).
  - Courts fill reasonable gaps where intent to be bound is clear (§ 204). A missing price for services is filled with a reasonable price.
- **Agreements to agree.** "Rent to be agreed" is traditionally fatal. Modern courts enforce such terms if an objective standard or a method exists (market, an arbitrator).
- **Vague terms** ("a fair share of profits") may be too indefinite, but part performance can supply meaning.
- **UCC § 2-204(3):** a contract does not fail for indefiniteness if the parties intended to contract and there is a reasonably certain basis for an appropriate remedy. Gap fillers:

| Missing term | Gap filler | UCC § |
|---|---|---|
| Price | Reasonable price at the time for delivery; a price to be set by one party must be set in good faith | 2-305 |
| Delivery place | Seller's place of business (or residence); identified goods located elsewhere — that place | 2-308 |
| Delivery time | Reasonable time; an indefinite-duration contract is terminable on reasonable notice | 2-309 |
| Payment | Due at the time and place the buyer receives the goods | 2-310 |
| Single vs. several lots | Single delivery | 2-307 |
| Quality | Implied warranties of merchantability and fitness, if their conditions are met | 2-314, 2-315 |
| Quantity | **Not supplied** (except output/requirements) | 2-201, 2-306 |

- **Output and requirements contracts** (§ 2-306).
  - The quantity is the actual good-faith output or requirements, without unreasonable disproportion to an estimate or prior norms.
  - Good-faith decreases are allowed; increases are capped by the disproportion test.
  - Exclusive dealing implies best efforts (*Wood v. Lucy, Lady Duff-Gordon*, 222 N.Y. 88 (1917)).
- **How tested.** MBE: § 2-305 open price; requirements spikes; the "agreement to agree" at common law vs. the UCC. Essays: oral supply deals with open terms (Feb 2024 Q5) [VERIFY V-071].

### I.C Consideration (R-CTR-022 to R-CTR-026)
**Core rule.** A bargained-for exchange of a legal detriment (or benefit) (R2d § 71; *Hamer v. Sidway*, 124 N.Y. 538 (1891)). Each side's promise or performance must induce the other's.

| Situation | Consideration? | Authority |
|---|---|---|
| Inadequate but real exchange ($1 for a car) | Yes; courts do not weigh adequacy (but gross inadequacy may signal fraud, unconscionability, or a sham) | R2d § 79 |
| Recited nominal consideration never paid | Traditional: sham, no. R2d: enough for **options and guaranties** | R2d §§ 71, 87(1), 88 |
| Conditional gift ("come live near me and I'll give you a place") | No, if the condition is just a step to receive the gift | *Kirksey v. Kirksey*, 8 Ala. 131 (1845) |
| Illusory promise ("I'll buy what I want") | No | R2d § 77 |
| Promise subject to good faith, best efforts, notice of termination, or output/requirements | Yes | *Wood v. Lucy*; UCC § 2-306 |
| Pre-existing duty owed to the promisor | No (but see exceptions) | R2d § 73; *Alaska Packers*, 117 F. 99 (9th Cir. 1902) |
| Pre-existing duty owed to a **third party** | Yes, under the R2d (modern view) | R2d § 73 |
| Forbearance on a valid or good-faith doubtful claim | Yes | R2d § 74 |
| Past consideration / moral obligation | No (but § 86 and statutes) | *Mills v. Wyman* (1825) |
| Part payment of a liquidated, undisputed debt | No (*Foakes v. Beer*). **CA:** written acceptance of part performance extinguishes the debt (Civ. Code § 1524) [S-208] | |
| Part payment of a disputed or unliquidated debt | Yes (accord) | R2d § 74; R-CTR-087 |

**Pre-existing duty exceptions (R-CTR-024):**
1. New or different consideration, however slight (earlier payment, payment in a different medium or place, extra work).
2. An honest dispute about the scope of the duty.
3. Modification fair and equitable in light of unanticipated circumstances (R2d § 89(a); *Angel v. Murray*, 113 R.I. 482 (1974)).
4. Material reliance on the modification (§ 89(c)).
5. UCC goods: no consideration needed, but good faith required (§ 2-209(1)).
6. Mutual rescission followed by a new contract (a fiction many courts accept).

**Past consideration and its exceptions (R-CTR-026):**
- **Material benefit rule** (R2d § 86; *Webb v. McGowin*, 27 Ala. App. 82, 168 So. 196 (1935)). A promise made in recognition of a benefit previously received is binding to the extent necessary to prevent injustice. Not if the benefit was a gift, or to the extent the promise is disproportionate.
- **Debts barred by the statute of limitations** (R2d § 82) are binding on a new promise. **CA:** the acknowledgment must be in a signed writing (CCP § 360).
- **Debts discharged in bankruptcy** (R2d § 83): reaffirmation must meet 11 U.S.C. § 524(c).
- **CA note.** Civil Code § 1606 makes "a moral obligation originating in some benefit conferred upon the promisor" good consideration. Courts confine it largely to cases with a prior legal or equitable obligation, plus idea-submission cases [S-207].
- **CA presumption.** A written instrument is presumptive evidence of consideration, and the burden is on the party attacking it (Civ. Code §§ 1614, 1615).

**How tested.** MBE: the pre-existing duty rule (contractor demands more), § 86 rescuer cases, illusory promises, forbearance on doubtful claims. Essays: consideration is usually one short paragraph unless the facts are a gratuitous promise; then argue promissory estoppel.

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: PRC law enforces gratuitous contracts (e.g., gift contracts, with a pre-delivery revocation right); US law needs consideration or a recognized substitute (approx.).

### I.D Obligations enforceable without a bargained-for exchange (R-CTR-027, R-CTR-011, R-CTR-026, R-CTR-018)
1. **Promissory estoppel** (R2d § 90).
   - Elements: a promise; foreseeable reliance; actual, reasonable reliance; injustice without enforcement.
   - The remedy is flexible, often reliance damages.
   - Charitable subscriptions and marriage settlements bind without proof of reliance (§ 90(2)).
   - Leading cases: *Ricketts v. Scothorn*, 57 Neb. 51 (1898) (gift note relied on); *Feinberg v. Pfeiffer Co.*, 322 S.W.2d 163 (Mo. Ct. App. 1959) (pension promise); *Hoffman v. Red Owl Stores*, 26 Wis. 2d 683 (1965) (pre-contract assurances).
2. **Reliance on an offer** (§ 87(2); *Drennan*), covered in I.A.4.
3. **Promises for benefit received** (§ 86) and promises to pay barred or discharged debts (§§ 82, 83), covered in I.C.
4. **Restitution / quasi-contract** for unjust enrichment, covered in I.A.8 and V.G.
5. **Promises under seal.** Historically binding without consideration. Most states, including California, have abolished or limited the seal's effect. Not tested in practice.

**CA note.** California applies § 90, including to subcontractor bids (*Drennan*). On an essay, raise promissory estoppel **whenever consideration is doubtful** or the statute of frauds blocks enforcement (R2d § 139).

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: pre-contract reliance losses in PRC law fall under 缔约过失责任 (culpa in contrahendo); the US analogue requires a promise relied on, not merely bad-faith negotiation (approx.).

### I.E Modification of contracts (R-CTR-028, R-CTR-029)
| Issue | Common law | UCC Article 2 | California |
|---|---|---|---|
| Consideration | Needed (pre-existing duty), unless R2d § 89 | Not needed; good faith required (§ 2-209(1), cmt. 2) | Civ. Code § 1698: oral modification of a written contract needs new consideration (§ 1698(c)) unless executed (§ 1698(b)) [S-201] |
| "No oral modification" clause | Often treated as waivable; courts enforce later oral modifications | Enforced (§ 2-209(2)); a merchant's form must be separately signed by a non-merchant | § 1698(c) applies "unless the contract otherwise expressly provides" [S-201] |
| Statute of frauds | Applies if the contract as modified is within the statute | § 2-209(3) | § 1698(c) |
| Failed modification as waiver | Waiver or estoppel | § 2-209(4); retractable unless reliance (§ 2-209(5)) | § 1698(d) preserves waiver, estoppel, novation and rescission doctrines [S-201] |

- **Good faith under § 2-209.** The test is a legitimate commercial reason, such as a cost spike or market shift. Using leverage to extort a modification ("pay more or I won't deliver") is ineffective (cmt. 2). Overlap: economic duress (II.B).
- **Exam pattern.** A mid-performance demand for more money.
  - Common law: no consideration, unless unforeseen difficulty.
  - UCC: enforceable if in good faith; check the statute of frauds.
- **How tested.** MBE tests both regimes side by side. Essays: modification often appears inside a breach question ("Was the price increase enforceable?").

## II Defenses to enforceability (essay: contract defenses)

> Essay order for defenses: first ask whether a contract was formed (Category I), then whether a defense defeats it. Group defenses under one heading, "Defenses," with a sub-heading for each defense the facts raise. California essays have tested mistake, the statute of frauds and parol evidence together (Feb 2026 Q2), and misrepresentation with rescission (Feb 2018 Q1) [VERIFY V-071].

### II.A Incapacity to contract (R-CTR-030, R-CTR-031)
| Category | Effect | Key points |
|---|---|---|
| **Minors** (under 18) | Voidable by the minor | Disaffirm before, or within a reasonable time after, majority. Ratify after majority expressly or by inaction. Necessaries: quasi-contract liability for reasonable value. On disaffirmance, return what remains (majority); some states charge for use or depreciation. A minor who misrepresents age may be estopped in some states. **CA:** Fam. Code §§ 6700, 6710, 6712 [VERIFY V-104] |
| **Persons under guardianship** (adjudicated) | Void (R2d § 13) | Liability in quasi-contract for necessaries |
| **Mental illness or defect** | Voidable (R2d § 15) | **Cognitive test:** unable to understand the nature and consequences of the transaction. **Volitional test:** unable to act reasonably, **and** the other party has reason to know. Fair contract, other party without knowledge, and changed circumstances: avoidance only on equitable terms (§ 15(2)). **CA:** Civ. Code §§ 38, 39 |
| **Intoxication** | Voidable only if the other party has reason to know (R2d § 16) | Prompt disaffirmance on sobering; restitution required |

- **How tested.** MBE: minor disaffirming after the goods are destroyed; ratification by keeping goods after turning 18; necessaries. Essays: rare.

### II.B Duress and undue influence (R-CTR-032, R-CTR-033)
- **Physical duress** (R2d § 174): no assent at all; the contract is void.
- **Improper threat** (§§ 175, 176): the contract is voidable if the threat is improper **and** leaves no reasonable alternative. Improper threats:
  - a crime or tort;
  - criminal prosecution;
  - civil process in bad faith;
  - breach of the duty of good faith under an existing contract with the recipient;
  - threats whose resulting exchange is unfair and that use power for illegitimate ends.
- **Economic duress.** A wrongful threat to withhold performance where substitutes are unavailable and suing is inadequate (*Austin Instrument, Inc. v. Loral Corp.*, 29 N.Y.2d 124 (1971)). It is the twin of the pre-existing duty rule in modification cases.
- **Duress by a third party** makes the contract voidable unless the other party, in good faith and without reason to know, gave value or relied materially.
- **Undue influence** (R2d § 177): unfair persuasion of a person who is under the persuader's domination or in a relationship of trust. Indicators: discussion at an unusual time or place, insistence on immediate decision, no advisers, multiple persuaders, extreme emphasis on untoward consequences of delay. **CA:** Civ. Code § 1575; *Odorizzi v. Bloomfield School Dist.* (1966) 246 Cal.App.2d 123 [VERIFY V-110].
- **Remedy.** Rescission and restitution. Ratification after the pressure ends bars relief.

### II.C Mistake and misunderstanding (R-CTR-034, R-CTR-035, R-CTR-036)
**Mutual mistake (R2d § 152)** — the contract is voidable by the adversely affected party if:
1. both parties were mistaken, **at formation**, about an existing fact (not a prediction or a value judgment);
2. the fact was a **basic assumption** on which the contract was made;
3. the mistake has a **material effect** on the agreed exchange; and
4. the party seeking relief does **not bear the risk** (§ 154). It bears the risk if:
   - (a) the contract allocates it (an "as is" clause: *Lenawee County Bd. of Health v. Messerly*, 417 Mich. 17 (1982));
   - (b) it knew its knowledge was limited and treated that as enough (conscious ignorance: *Wood v. Boynton*, 64 Wis. 265 (1885), an uncut stone of unknown nature [VERIFY V-110]); or
   - (c) the court allocates the risk to it as reasonable.

Classic contrast: *Sherwood v. Walker*, 66 Mich. 568 (1887). A cow both parties believed barren turned out to be with calf, and rescission was allowed because the mistake concerned the cow's very nature.

**Unilateral mistake (R2d § 153)** — the same elements 1–4 as to one party, **plus** either:
- (a) enforcement would be unconscionable; or
- (b) the other party had reason to know of the mistake or caused it.

Typical cases: clerical errors in bids (*Elsinore Union Elementary School Dist. v. Kastorff* (1960) 54 Cal.2d 380 [VERIFY V-110]) and misprinted prices. **CA:** *Donovan v. RRL Corp.* adopted § 153 and held that ordinary negligence does not bar rescission; only a failure to act in good faith and fair dealing does [S-205].

**Negligence** in failing to discover the mistake does not bar relief unless it amounts to a failure to act in good faith (R2d § 157).

**Mistake in expression** (the writing misstates the agreement) is cured by **reformation**, not rescission (V.E; R-CTR-106).

**Misunderstanding (R2d § 20; *Raffles v. Wichelhaus*, 1864, the two ships "Peerless"):**
| Who knew what | Result |
|---|---|
| Neither knew or had reason to know of the other's different meaning | No contract |
| Both knew of the difference | No contract |
| One knew or had reason to know the other's meaning; the other did not | Contract on the **innocent** party's meaning (§ 201(2)) |

**CA notes:** Civil Code § 1577 defines mistake of fact; § 1689(b) allows rescission for mistake; § 1580 says consent is not mutual unless the parties agree on the same thing in the same sense.

**How tested.**
- MBE: the "as is" clause allocating risk; value mistakes (no relief); bid errors the other side should have noticed.
- Essays: mistake about the identity or authenticity of goods (Feb 2026 Q2) [VERIFY V-071]. Argue both sides on "basic assumption" vs. "value," and on who bore the risk.

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: PRC 重大误解 allows revocation of a juristic act; the US mutual-mistake test adds the **risk-allocation** step, which often decides the case (approx.).

### II.D Fraud, misrepresentation, and nondisclosure (R-CTR-037, R-CTR-038)
**Misrepresentation as a defense (rescission) (R2d §§ 159–164):**
1. An **assertion not in accord with the facts**. Words or conduct count; active concealment (§ 160) counts.
2. **Fraudulent** (the maker knows it is false or lacks confidence in it, and intends to induce assent) **or material** (likely to induce a reasonable person, or the maker knows it would induce this person) (§ 162).
3. It **induced** assent.
4. Reliance was **justified** (§ 164). Reliance on an obvious falsehood is not justified, but a duty to investigate is generally not imposed.

**Fraud in the factum vs. inducement.**
- In the factum (deceived about the very nature of the document): void (§ 163).
- In the inducement: voidable.

**Opinions, predictions, law.** Generally not actionable (§§ 168, 169), unless the speaker:
- has special expertise;
- is in a relationship of trust; or
- implies facts justifying the opinion.

Puffery is not actionable. A promise made without intent to perform is fraud (promissory fraud). **CA:** Civ. Code § 1572(4).

**Nondisclosure (§ 161)** is equivalent to an assertion only where disclosure:
- (a) is needed to prevent a prior assertion from being misleading, or to correct one that has become untrue;
- (b) would correct the other's mistake about a basic assumption, and silence is a failure to act in good faith;
- (c) would correct a mistake about a writing's contents; or
- (d) is owed because of a relationship of trust.

**CA notes.**
- Civil Code § 1572 defines actual fraud, including suppression of a known fact (§ 1572(3)).
- Civil Code §§ 1709–1710 define deceit.
- A seller of real property must disclose known facts materially affecting value that the buyer cannot reasonably discover (*Lingsch v. Savage* (1963) 213 Cal.App.2d 729) [VERIFY V-106].
- Fraud evidence is admissible despite an integration clause, even if it contradicts the writing (*Riverisland*, III.A) [S-214].
- A tort action may lie for fraudulent concealment during performance (*Rattagan*, R-CTR-117) [S-132].

**Remedies.**
- **Rescission and restitution** (contract) — an innocent material misrepresentation is enough.
- **Damages in deceit** (tort) — requires scienter and loss. California permits punitive damages for fraud (Civ. Code § 3294).
- **Reformation** where the misrepresentation concerned the writing's contents (§ 166).
- UCC § 2-721: rescission for fraud does not bar a damages claim.

**How tested.** Essays: "Can the buyer rescind for misrepresentation or nondisclosure?" (Feb 2018 Q1) [VERIFY V-071]. Write out the elements, then the remedy (rescission requirements: notice, restoration, promptness), then defenses (affirmation, delay, "as is" or merger clause).

### II.E Illegality, unconscionability, and public policy (R-CTR-039, R-CTR-040, R-CTR-041)
**Illegality (R2d § 178).**
- **Illegal subject matter** (a contract to commit a crime or tort): void. Neither party can enforce, and courts leave the parties where they find them (*in pari delicto*).
- **Legal subject matter, illegal purpose known to the other party:** enforceable by the innocent party. A party who knew of the purpose can enforce unless the purpose involves serious moral turpitude, or it actively facilitated the illegality.
- **Exceptions:**
  - a party not equally at fault, or in the class the law protects (e.g., a borrower under a usury law);
  - withdrawal before the illegal act (*locus poenitentiae*);
  - severance of a divisible legal part.
- **Licensing statutes:**
  - regulatory (protecting the public, such as contractor, lawyer or doctor licenses): an unlicensed party cannot enforce;
  - revenue-raising: enforceable.
  - **CA:** Bus. & Prof. Code § 7031 bars unlicensed contractors from suing for compensation.
- **Supervening illegality** discharges duties (IV.G).

**Unconscionability (UCC § 2-302; R2d § 208).**
- **Procedural:** oppression or surprise, such as adhesion contracts, inequality of bargaining power, fine print, high-pressure tactics, or language barriers.
- **Substantive:** overly harsh or one-sided terms, such as excessive price, cross-collateral clauses (*Williams v. Walker-Thomas Furniture Co.*, 350 F.2d 445 (D.C. Cir. 1965)), or remedy limitations that leave no real remedy.
- Most courts require **both** elements, on a **sliding scale**: more of one means less of the other is needed.
- Measured **at formation**; a question of law for the court.
- **Remedies:** refuse to enforce the whole, sever the clause, or limit its application.
- **CA:** Civ. Code § 1670.5; *Armendariz v. Foundation Health Psychcare Services* (2000) 24 Cal.4th 83 (sliding scale; often used for arbitration clauses).

**Public policy.**
- **Exculpatory clauses.** They cannot excuse intentional, reckless or (in many states) grossly negligent harm (R2d § 195). They are unenforceable where the service affects the public interest.
  - **CA:** Civ. Code § 1668; *Tunkl v. Regents of University of California* (1963) 60 Cal.2d 92 (hospital admission release void).
- **Covenants not to compete** (R2d §§ 186–188). Majority rule: enforceable if ancillary to a sale of a business or employment, protecting a legitimate interest (goodwill, trade secrets, customer relationships), and reasonable in time, place and scope. Courts may blue-pencil an overbroad covenant.
  - **CA differs sharply:** Bus. & Prof. Code § 16600 voids contracts restraining anyone from a lawful profession, trade or business, with narrow exceptions (sale of business goodwill; dissolution of a partnership or LLC) [VERIFY V-112].
  - *Edwards v. Arthur Andersen LLP* (2008) 44 Cal.4th 937 rejected a "narrow restraint" exception.
  - Since January 1, 2024, § 16600.5 makes such contracts unenforceable regardless of where and when signed and creates a private right of action [S-206].
- **Other policies.** Restraints on marriage, contracts to influence public officials, gambling (by statute).

**How tested.** MBE: unlicensed party's claim; unconscionable remedy limitation; noncompete reasonableness. Essays: occasionally; a noncompete on a California essay is a chance to show § 16600.

### II.F Statute of frauds (R-CTR-042 to R-CTR-048)
**Three-step analysis:**
1. Is the contract **within** the statute?
2. If so, is it **satisfied** (a writing, or a UCC alternative)?
3. If not, does an **exception** make it enforceable anyway?

**Step 1 — Covered contracts: "MY LEGS" (R2d § 110).**
| Category | Scope and limits |
|---|---|
| **M**arriage | A promise made in consideration of marriage (a prenuptial transfer). Not mutual promises to marry. **CA:** premarital agreements must be in writing (Fam. Code § 1611) |
| **Y**ear | Cannot **by its terms** be performed within one year of making (R2d § 130). Lifetime contracts and indefinite-duration contracts are **outside**. Full performance by one side takes the contract out (§ 130(2)). **CA:** Civ. Code § 1624(a)(1) [S-202] |
| **L**and | Any interest in land: sale, mortgage, easement, lease over one year (R2d § 125). Not licenses or short leases. **CA:** § 1624(a)(3) [VERIFY V-100] |
| **E**xecutor | An executor's or administrator's promise to pay estate debts **from personal funds** |
| **G**oods | Sale of goods for **$500 or more** (UCC § 2-201) |
| **S**uretyship | A promise **to a creditor** to answer for the debt of another (collateral) (R2d § 110(1)(b)). **Not** a primary promise, a promise to the debtor, or a main-purpose (leading object) case. **CA:** § 1624(a)(2) [S-202] |

**CA additions** (Civ. Code § 1624):
- agreements authorizing or employing an agent or broker to buy, sell or lease (over one year) real estate, or to find a buyer, seller or lessee, for compensation ((a)(4)) [S-202];
- agreements that by their terms are not to be performed during the promisor's lifetime ((a)(5)) [S-202];
- reportedly, an agreement by a purchaser of real property to pay a debt secured by the property, and certain commercial loan commitments over $100,000 — both unconfirmed [VERIFY V-100].

**Step 2 — Satisfying the statute.**
- **Common-law memorandum** (R2d § 131): any writing or writings (§ 132) that reasonably identify the subject matter, show a contract was made, state the essential terms with reasonable certainty, and are **signed by the party to be charged** (§ 134; any authenticating mark).
  - It may be made after the contract, need not be delivered, and may be in an email or text.
  - Electronic signatures count (E-SIGN, 15 U.S.C. § 7001; **CA** UETA, Civ. Code § 1633.7).
  - **CA:** extrinsic evidence may clarify ambiguous essential terms but cannot supply missing ones (*Sterling v. Taylor* (2007) 40 Cal.4th 757) [S-204].
- **UCC § 2-201(1).** A writing "sufficient to indicate" a contract, signed by the defendant. It need state only **quantity**, and the contract is not enforceable beyond the quantity shown. **CA:** Com. Code § 2201 [S-211] (current text may use "record" [VERIFY V-101]).
- **Merchant's confirmation** (§ 2-201(2)). Between merchants, a written confirmation sent within a reasonable time, which the recipient has reason to know of, satisfies the statute **against the recipient** unless it objects in writing within 10 days of receipt. The sender must still prove the contract.

**Step 3 — Exceptions.**
| Exception | Applies to | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Part performance (possession + payment or improvements; unequivocally referable) | Land | Specific performance (R2d § 129). **CA:** estoppel to prevent unconscionable injury or unjust enrichment (*Monarco v. Lo Greco* (1950) 35 Cal.2d 621) [VERIFY V-110] |
| Full performance by one party | One-year provision (majority) | Enforceable |
| Promissory estoppel | All (R2d § 139), though some courts refuse | Enforced as justice requires |
| Specially manufactured goods | Goods (§ 2-201(3)(a)) | Not suitable for others **and** the seller substantially began before notice of repudiation |
| Admission in court (pleading or testimony) | Goods (§ 2-201(3)(b)); some courts at common law | Up to the quantity admitted |
| Goods paid for and accepted, or received and accepted | Goods (§ 2-201(3)(c)) | Only to that extent |
| Main purpose rule | Suretyship | Promisor's leading object is its own economic benefit |
| Restitution | All (R2d § 375) | Value of the benefit conferred |

**Related rules.**
- **Equal dignities.** An agent's authority to sign a contract that must be in writing must itself be in writing (**CA:** Civ. Code § 2309).
- **Modifications.** If the contract as modified is within the statute, the modification must satisfy it (UCC § 2-209(3); **CA** Civ. Code § 1698(c) [S-201]).
- **Rescission** of a land contract may itself need a writing if it re-transfers an interest.
- **Effect of noncompliance.** The contract is unenforceable, not void. Only the party who did not sign may raise the defense, and the defense is waived if not pleaded.

**How tested.** MBE: one-year computation (from making); suretyship versus a primary promise; the merchant's confirmation; specially manufactured goods; admission. Essays: an oral agreement for the sale of goods or land (Feb 2024 Q5; Feb 2026 Q2) [VERIFY V-071]. Always say which category, then the writing, then the exceptions.

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: PRC law generally lets parties choose the form of a contract (written form is required only where a statute says so); the US statute of frauds instead targets listed categories, and the sanction is unenforceability (approx.).

## III Contract content and meaning

### III.A Parol evidence (R-CTR-049 to R-CTR-052)
**The rule.** If the parties intend a writing as the final expression of their agreement (an **integration**), evidence of **prior** agreements (oral or written) or **contemporaneous oral** agreements cannot **contradict** it (R2d § 213). If the writing is also **complete and exclusive** (a complete integration), such evidence cannot even **add consistent terms**. The rule does **not** apply to agreements made **after** the writing.

**Who decides.** The judge decides integration as a preliminary question (R2d §§ 209, 210).

**Is the writing complete?**
| Approach | Test | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Four corners / Williston (traditional; *Mitchill v. Lath*, 247 N.Y. 377 (1928)) | Look at the writing; if it appears complete, it is. A collateral term is admissible only if it would not ordinarily be expected in such a writing | Excludes more |
| R2d / Corbin (majority modern) | Consider all relevant evidence (§ 214). A consistent additional term is admissible if agreed for **separate consideration** or one that **might naturally be omitted** from the writing (§ 216(2)) | Admits more |
| UCC § 2-202 cmt. 3 | A consistent additional term is excluded only if it would **certainly** have been included had it been agreed | Admits most |
| **CA** (CCP § 1856; *Masterson v. Sine* (1968) 68 Cal.2d 222) | Collateral agreement admissible unless it would **naturally** have been made part of the writing. A merger clause is strong but not conclusive evidence | Admits more |

**Merger (integration) clause.** "This writing is the entire agreement." Under the R2d it is strong evidence of complete integration but not conclusive, and it is ineffective if the writing is obviously incomplete or the clause is boilerplate in an adhesion contract.

**What the rule does not bar (R-CTR-050):**
1. Evidence to decide **whether** the writing is integrated, and how completely.
2. **Interpretation** of ambiguous terms (see III.B; in California, of any term reasonably susceptible to the proffered meaning).
3. **Defenses and avoidance:** fraud, duress, undue influence, mistake, illegality, lack or failure of consideration (R2d § 214(d)).
4. An **oral condition precedent** to the writing taking effect (§ 217) — "this deal is off unless my partner approves."
5. **Reformation** for mutual mistake in expression or fraud.
6. A **separate agreement** with separate consideration.
7. **Subsequent** modifications (subject to a no-oral-modification clause and the statute of frauds).
8. Course of dealing, usage of trade and course of performance, to explain or supplement (UCC §§ 1-303, 2-202(a)).

**Fraud and the integration clause — California.**
- The older California rule (*Bank of America etc. Assn. v. Pendergrass* (1935) 4 Cal.2d 258) excluded evidence of fraudulent promises directly at variance with the writing.
- *Riverisland Cold Storage, Inc. v. Fresno-Madera Production Credit Assn.* (2013) 55 Cal.4th 1169 overruled *Pendergrass*: evidence offered to prove fraud is admissible under CCP § 1856(g) even if it contradicts the writing [S-214].

**Interpretation evidence — California (PG&E).** *Pacific Gas & Electric Co. v. G.W. Thomas Drayage & Rigging Co.* (1968) 69 Cal.2d 33 rejected the plain-meaning rule. The test is whether the offered evidence is relevant to prove a meaning to which the language is **reasonably susceptible**, and the court provisionally receives the evidence to decide that question. The federal Ninth Circuit criticized the approach but applied it (*Trident Center v. Connecticut General Life Ins. Co.*, 847 F.2d 564 (9th Cir. 1988)).

**How tested.**
- MBE: whether a merger clause bars an oral side agreement; whether the evidence is offered to contradict, to supplement or to interpret; the oral condition precedent; the subsequent-modification trap.
- Essays (Feb 2026 Q2) [VERIFY V-071]: identify the writing, decide integration (partial vs. complete, with the merger clause), state the purpose of the evidence, apply the exception, and note California's liberal approach.

### III.B Interpretation (R-CTR-053, R-CTR-054, R-CTR-036)
- **Goal.** Give effect to the parties' mutual intention, objectively manifested (R2d §§ 201, 202; **CA** Civ. Code § 1636). If possible, ascertain it from the writing alone (Civ. Code § 1639), but California admits evidence under *PG&E*.
- **Whose meaning prevails** (R2d § 201):
  - if the parties attach the same meaning, that meaning;
  - if they attach different meanings, the meaning of the party who did not know or have reason to know of the other's meaning;
  - otherwise, no contract (misunderstanding, II.C).
- **Canons** (R2d §§ 202, 203):
  - read the contract as a whole (Civ. Code § 1641);
  - ordinary meaning of words (Civ. Code § 1644), technical meaning of technical terms;
  - interpretations that make the contract lawful, reasonable and effective are preferred;
  - specific over general;
  - **negotiated or handwritten over typed over printed**;
  - express terms over course of performance, over course of dealing, over usage of trade (UCC § 1-303(e));
  - *ejusdem generis* and *expressio unius* as aids;
  - **contra proferentem**, construing ambiguity against the drafter, as the last resort (R2d § 206; Civ. Code § 1654).
- **Trade usage** binds a party who knew or had reason to know of it. A party claiming a trade meaning against a newcomer must show actual knowledge or that the usage was so general that knowledge is presumed (*Frigaliment Importing Co. v. B.N.S. International Sales Corp.*, 190 F. Supp. 116 (S.D.N.Y. 1960), the "chicken" case).
- **Course of performance** can show the meaning of terms, or a waiver or modification (UCC § 1-303(f)).

### III.C Omitted and implied terms (R-CTR-055, R-CTR-020, R-CTR-071, R-CTR-056 to R-CTR-059)
- **Court-supplied terms.** When the parties fail to agree on an essential term, the court supplies a term reasonable in the circumstances (R2d § 204).
  - A reasonable time (**CA** Civ. Code § 1657).
  - A reasonable price.
  - Reasonable efforts in exclusive deals (*Wood v. Lucy*).
  - A duty to cooperate and not to hinder (prevention doctrine, IV.B).
- **Implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing** in every contract (R2d § 205; UCC § 1-304). See IV.D.
- **UCC gap fillers** (I.B table) and **implied warranties** (IV.E).
- **Employment.** Absent a term, employment is at will; California presumes at-will employment by statute (Lab. Code § 2922).
- **Limits.** Courts will not imply a term that contradicts an express one (*Carma Developers (Cal.), Inc. v. Marathon Development California, Inc.* (1992) 2 Cal.4th 342), or that rewrites the bargain.

## IV Performance, breach, and discharge (essay: performance; breach; discharge)

> Essay map for performance and breach: (1) What did each party promise, and in what order? (2) Were there conditions, express or constructive, and did they occur or were they excused? (3) Did a party breach? Was the breach material or minor (common law), or nonconforming (UCC)? Was there an anticipatory repudiation? (4) Is performance excused (impossibility, impracticability, frustration)? (5) Were duties discharged? Then go to Remedies.

### IV.A Conditions (R-CTR-062, R-CTR-063)
- **Promise vs. condition.**
  - A **promise** is a commitment; its breach gives a claim for damages.
  - A **condition** is an event that must occur before a duty becomes due (precedent) or that cuts off a duty (subsequent) (R2d §§ 224, 230). Its non-occurrence means no duty, but no damages either, unless someone also promised that it would occur.
  - A term can be both ("Seller promises to deliver by May 1, and buyer's duty to pay is conditioned on timely delivery").
- **Express conditions.** Created by language such as "if," "on condition that," "provided that," "subject to" or "unless."
  - They require **strict compliance**; substantial performance does not satisfy them.
  - In doubtful cases, courts read the term as a promise or as a constructive condition, to avoid forfeiture (R2d § 227; **CA** Civ. Code § 1442).
  - Common examples: financing contingencies, approval or permit conditions, notice requirements, an architect's certificate, satisfaction clauses.
- **Satisfaction clauses** (R2d § 228 [VERIFY V-111]):
  - for taste, fancy or personal judgment (a portrait, a custom suit), the test is **subjective** honest dissatisfaction, in good faith;
  - for commercial value, quality or mechanical fitness, the test is **objective**: would a reasonable person be satisfied?
  - Third-party satisfaction (an architect or engineer) is excused if the third party is dishonest, incapacitated or acts in bad faith.
- **Constructive (implied-in-law) conditions** (R2d §§ 234, 237). Each party's duty is conditioned on the other's **material** performance of earlier-due duties (*Kingston v. Preston*).
  - **Order of performance:**
    - the parties' agreement controls;
    - if both can be performed at once, they are **concurrent conditions** (land sale: deed and price), and a party must **tender** to put the other in breach;
    - if one performance takes time, it must come first (work before pay, absent progress payments).
- **Conditions vs. covenants in essays.** For each term, say whether it is a condition or a promise and why. A time-of-performance clause is usually a promise, not an express condition, unless the language says so (Jul 2016 Q3) [VERIFY V-071].

### IV.B Excuse of conditions (R-CTR-064)
| Excuse | Rule | Authority |
|---|---|---|
| **Waiver** | The party protected by a condition may waive it by words or conduct (accepting late payments). A waiver of a non-material condition needs no consideration. A waiver of a **future** condition is retractable by notice received while a reasonable time remains for the condition to occur, unless the other party has materially changed position in reliance | R2d § 84 |
| **Estoppel** | A party who led the other to believe the condition would not be insisted on, inducing reliance | Equity |
| **Prevention / hindrance** | A party who wrongfully prevents or hinders a condition cannot rely on its failure (implied duty to cooperate) | R2d § 245 [VERIFY V-111]; **CA** Civ. Code § 1511 [VERIFY V-105] |
| **Repudiation** | Excuses the other party's conditions | R2d § 255 |
| **Impossibility of a non-material condition** | Excused if it is not a material part of the exchange | R2d § 271 [VERIFY V-111] |
| **Disproportionate forfeiture** | A court may excuse a condition whose occurrence was not a material part of the exchange to avoid forfeiture (late notice under an insurance policy without prejudice) | R2d § 229; **CA** Civ. Code § 3275 |
| **Divisibility / substantial performance** | For constructive conditions only | IV.C |

### IV.C Breach (material and partial breach; anticipatory repudiation) (R-CTR-065 to R-CTR-070)
**1. Material vs. minor breach (common law).**
- Material breach (R2d § 241) — weigh:
  - (a) the extent the injured party is deprived of the expected benefit;
  - (b) whether damages can adequately compensate;
  - (c) the forfeiture the breaching party would suffer;
  - (d) the likelihood of cure, given assurances;
  - (e) the breaching party's good faith and fair dealing.
- **Effects.**
  - Material breach: the injured party may **suspend** performance and, once the time for cure passes (§ 242), **terminate** and sue for **total breach** (all damages, past and future) (§ 243).
  - Minor breach: damages only. The injured party must keep performing, and terminating would itself be a material breach.
- **Time of the essence.** A delay is usually a minor breach unless:
  - the contract makes time of the essence (expressly, or by repeated emphasis on dates);
  - the nature of the deal makes timing critical (perishables, seasonal goods, market-sensitive items); or
  - the delay is long enough to defeat the purpose.
  - Note that repeated references to deadlines without an express clause support **both** sides (Jul 2016 Q3) [VERIFY V-071].

**2. Substantial performance (common law; R-CTR-065).**
- A party that has substantially performed may recover the contract price less damages for the defects (*Jacob & Youngs, Inc. v. Kent*, 230 N.Y. 239 (1921): equal-quality pipe of a different brand; *Plante v. Jacobs*, 10 Wis. 2d 567 (1960)).
- A **willful** deviation weighs against it.
- It does not apply to **express** conditions or to **sales of goods** (perfect tender, IV.F).
- A party who has not substantially performed may still recover in restitution (V.H).

**3. Divisible contracts (R2d § 240; R-CTR-067).** Recover for each completed part at its agreed equivalent. Ask whether the parties would have agreed to each pair as a separate exchange.

**4. Anticipatory repudiation (R2d §§ 250, 253; UCC § 2-610; R-CTR-068).**
- **What counts:**
  - a clear, unequivocal statement that the party will not or cannot perform, made before performance is due;
  - a voluntary act that makes performance impossible (selling the promised land to a third party);
  - insisting on terms the contract does not require ("I'll deliver only if you pay 20% more").
- **Not repudiation:** expressions of doubt, requests to renegotiate, a statement of inability conditioned on events. These may justify a demand for **adequate assurance**.
- **Options for the aggrieved party:**
  - (1) treat it as a total breach and sue now (*Hochster v. De La Tour*, 1853);
  - (2) suspend performance and wait for a commercially reasonable time (UCC § 2-610(a));
  - (3) urge retraction.
  - In all cases the aggrieved party may suspend its own performance (§ 2-610(c)), and **must mitigate**: it cannot complete performance to run up damages (*Rockingham County v. Luten Bridge Co.*, 35 F.2d 301 (4th Cir. 1929)).
- **Exception:** a party that has fully performed, and is owed only installment money payments, generally cannot accelerate; it must sue as each installment falls due (R2d § 243 [VERIFY V-109]).
- **CA:** Civ. Code § 1440 [VERIFY V-105].
- **Retraction (§ 256; UCC § 2-611; R-CTR-069):** permitted until the aggrieved party has materially changed position, said it treats the repudiation as final, or sued.
- **Adequate assurance (§ 251; UCC § 2-609; R-CTR-070):**
  - requires reasonable grounds for insecurity;
  - the demand must be written under the UCC;
  - the demanding party may suspend performance;
  - failure to respond within a reasonable time (30 days maximum under the UCC) is a repudiation.
  - Between merchants, reasonableness is judged by commercial standards.

**5. UCC breach.** For goods, use perfect tender (§ 2-601) with its limits — cure, installment contracts, revocation (IV.F) — rather than material breach.

**How tested.**
- **This is the most-tested area on California Contracts essays.** Breach appears in at least five past essays (Jul 2014 Q1, Feb 2015 Q1, Jul 2016 Q3, Jul 2018 Q1, and the breach-of-contract opening in Feb 2012 Q4) [VERIFY V-071].
  - Calls are often two-sided: "Did either party breach, and what can each recover?"
  - Analyze each party's conduct in **chronological order**, deciding at each step whether a breach occurred and whether it was material, because an early material breach excuses later non-performance by the other side.
- MBE: repudiation vs. a request to renegotiate; whether a party can sue before the date of performance; retraction; demanding assurance.

### IV.D Obligations of good faith and fair dealing (R-CTR-071)
- **Every contract** carries an implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing in performance and enforcement (R2d § 205; UCC § 1-304).
  - Good faith (UCC § 1-201(b)(20)) means honesty in fact and observance of reasonable commercial standards of fair dealing.
- **Where it bites:**
  - discretionary powers (pricing, approval, termination);
  - satisfaction clauses;
  - output and requirements contracts (§ 2-306);
  - modifications (§ 2-209);
  - conditions within a party's control (efforts to obtain financing or permits);
  - evasion of the contract's spirit.
- **Limits:**
  - it does not create obligations inconsistent with express terms (*Carma Developers*, 2 Cal.4th 342);
  - it does not impose substantive duties beyond the contract's terms (*Guz v. Bechtel National, Inc.* (2000) 24 Cal.4th 317);
  - there is no free-standing duty of good faith in **negotiations** (contrast PRC 缔约过失).
- **CA — tort remedy limited.**
  - Breach of the covenant gives **contract** damages only.
  - Tort ("bad faith") damages are limited to insurance contracts. *Foley v. Interactive Data Corp.* (1988) 47 Cal.3d 654 refused them for employment. *Freeman & Mills, Inc. v. Belcher Oil Co.* (1995) 11 Cal.4th 85 overruled the *Seaman's* tort of bad-faith denial of a contract's existence.
  - See R-CTR-117 for the economic loss rule.

### IV.E Express and implied warranties in sale-of-goods contracts (R-CTR-056 to R-CTR-061)
| Warranty | Who gives it | Created by | Disclaimer (§ 2-316) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Title** (§ 2-312) | Any seller; a merchant also warrants against infringement claims | Every sale | Only specific language or circumstances (sheriff's sale). A general "as is" is not enough |
| **Express** (§ 2-313) | Any seller | Affirmation of fact or promise, description, sample or model that is part of the basis of the bargain. Puffery excluded | Disclaimer inconsistent with the warranty is inoperative; the parol evidence rule may exclude prior oral warranties if the writing is fully integrated |
| **Merchantability** (§ 2-314) | A merchant for goods of that kind | Implied | Must mention "merchantability"; if written, conspicuous. Or "as is," "with all faults." Or examination (§ 2-316(3)) |
| **Fitness for a particular purpose** (§ 2-315) | Any seller | The seller has reason to know of the particular purpose and of the buyer's reliance on its skill or judgment | Conspicuous writing; general language suffices. Or "as is" or examination |

- **Merchantability standards** (§ 2-314(2)): pass without objection in the trade; fair average quality (fungibles); **fit for ordinary purposes**; even kind and quality; adequately packaged and labeled; conform to label promises. Food cases use the reasonable-expectation or foreign-natural tests (Torts overlap).
- **Cumulation** (§ 2-317): warranties are construed as consistent and cumulative; where they conflict, express warranties displace inconsistent implied warranties, except fitness.
- **Conspicuous** (§ 1-201(b)(10)): a reasonable person ought to have noticed it (headings in capitals, contrasting type).
- **Timing:** a disclaimer delivered after the contract (on an invoice or in the box) is not part of the bargain unless agreed (§ 2-209 modification).
- **Notice of breach:** within a reasonable time after discovery, or the buyer loses all remedies (§ 2-607(3)(a)).
- **Who may sue** (§ 2-318): Alternative A covers the buyer's family, household and guests who are personally injured; Alternatives B and C are broader. Product injuries also raise tort strict liability.
- **Limitations:** 4 years from tender of delivery (§ 2-725), which the parties may shorten to 1 year. The claim accrues on tender unless the warranty explicitly extends to future performance.
- **Consumer overlays:** Magnuson-Moss (15 U.S.C. § 2308: no disclaimer of implied warranties by a written warrantor). **CA:** Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act, Civ. Code § 1790 et seq.
- **Remedy limits:** UCC § 2-719 (repair or replace; failure of essential purpose; consequential-damages exclusions), covered in V.C.
- **How tested.** Essays (Feb 2021 Q2; Torts crossover Jul 2023 Q2) [VERIFY V-071]:
  - identify each warranty;
  - analyze any disclaimer;
  - then the buyer's options (reject or revoke, or keep and sue under § 2-714), notice, and damages.

### IV.F Other performance matters (cure, identification, notice, risk of loss) (R-CTR-072 to R-CTR-081)
**1. Seller's tender and delivery (§§ 2-503, 2-504, 2-308, 2-319).**
- **Tender:** put and hold conforming goods at the buyer's disposition, with notice, at a reasonable hour.
- **Shipment contract:**
  - deliver to a carrier;
  - make a reasonable transport contract;
  - send documents;
  - notify the buyer promptly.
  - A failure to notify or to make a proper contract is a ground for rejection only if material delay or loss results.
- **Destination contract:** tender at the destination.
- **Trade terms:**
  - "F.O.B. seller's city" = shipment contract; "F.O.B. buyer's city" = destination contract;
  - "F.A.S. vessel" = deliver alongside the vessel;
  - C.I.F. and C. & F. are shipment contracts (§ 2-320).
  - **Presumption:** when carriage is involved and no term says otherwise, the contract is a shipment contract.

**2. Buyer's inspection and payment (§§ 2-513, 2-310, 2-511).**
- The buyer may inspect before payment or acceptance, except under C.O.D. or payment-against-documents terms.
- Payment is due at the time and place of receipt.
- Tender of payment by check is sufficient unless the seller demands legal tender and gives time to obtain it.

**3. Perfect tender, rejection and its limits (§§ 2-601, 2-602 to 2-605; R-CTR-072).**
- The buyer may reject for **any** nonconformity, in whole or by commercial unit.
- **Rejection procedure:**
  - within a reasonable time;
  - seasonable notice;
  - particularize the defects the seller could have cured (§ 2-605), or lose the right to rely on them.
- **After rejection:**
  - the buyer must hold the goods with reasonable care and follow the seller's reasonable instructions (merchant buyer, § 2-603);
  - it may store, reship or resell them for the seller's account if there are no instructions (§ 2-604);
  - it has a security interest in the goods for payments made and expenses (§ 2-711(3)).
- **Limits on perfect tender:**
  - **cure** (§ 2-508);
  - **installment** contracts (§ 2-612);
  - the parties' agreement (a repair-or-replace limitation, § 2-719);
  - shipment-term defects (§ 2-504);
  - **good faith** (no pretextual rejection to escape a falling market).

**4. Cure (§ 2-508; R-CTR-073).** Before the contract time expires: notice plus a conforming delivery by the deadline. After it expires: only if the seller had reasonable grounds to believe the tender would be acceptable (for example, a newer model, or prior dealings accepting substitutes), with notice and within a further reasonable time.

**5. Acceptance (§§ 2-606, 2-607; R-CTR-074).**
- **How a buyer accepts:**
  - signifying acceptance after a reasonable opportunity to inspect;
  - failing to reject in time; or
  - acting inconsistently with the seller's ownership (using, reselling or altering the goods beyond what inspection requires).
- **Consequences:**
  - pay the contract price;
  - no rejection;
  - notify of breach within a reasonable time or be barred;
  - the burden of proving breach shifts to the buyer;
  - damages remain available under § 2-714.

**6. Revocation of acceptance (§ 2-608; R-CTR-075).**
- **Requirements:**
  - **substantial impairment** of value to the buyer (a subjective-objective test);
  - acceptance either on a reasonable assumption of cure that did not happen, or without discovery because the defect was latent or the seller gave assurances;
  - within a reasonable time after discovery;
  - before a substantial change in the goods' condition not caused by the defect;
  - notice to the seller.
- **Effect:** the same as rejection — the buyer cancels, recovers the price, and covers or claims market damages.
- **Continued use after revocation** may be allowed if reasonable (courts divide; often an offset is applied).

**7. Installment contracts (§ 2-612; R-CTR-076).**
- **Reject an installment** only for **substantial impairment** of that installment that cannot be cured. If the seller gives adequate assurance of cure, the buyer must accept.
- **Cancel the whole** only if the defect substantially impairs the value of the **whole** contract.
- The buyer must avoid reinstating the contract: accepting a later installment without seasonable notice of cancellation, or suing only for past installments, reinstates it.

**8. Identification, insurable interest, casualty (§§ 2-501, 2-613; R-CTR-080).**
- **Identification:**
  - at contracting, for existing specified goods;
  - for future goods, when the seller ships, marks or designates them;
  - crops when planted; young animals when conceived.
- **Casualty** to goods identified at contracting, without fault, before risk passes:
  - total loss: the contract is **avoided**;
  - partial loss: the buyer may avoid or accept with an allowance.
- For goods not identified at contracting, the seller must deliver other goods unless § 2-615 excuses it.

**9. Risk of loss (§§ 2-509, 2-510; R-CTR-078, R-CTR-079).**

Order of analysis:
1. **Agreement** of the parties (a risk term, or F.O.B. and other trade terms).
2. **Breach**:
   - nonconforming tender: risk stays on the seller until cure or acceptance (§ 2-510(1));
   - rightful revocation: the seller bears the loss to the extent the buyer's insurance falls short (§ 2-510(2));
   - buyer's repudiation or breach as to identified conforming goods: the buyer bears the loss for a commercially reasonable time, to the extent the seller's insurance falls short (§ 2-510(3)).
3. **Carrier**:
   - shipment contract: risk passes on due delivery to the carrier;
   - destination contract: on due tender at the destination (§ 2-509(1)).
4. **Bailee** (warehouse), goods not moved: risk passes on the buyer's receipt of a negotiable document, on the bailee's acknowledgment, or after the buyer has a reasonable time to present a non-negotiable document (§ 2-509(2)).
5. **Everything else** (§ 2-509(3)):
   - merchant seller: risk passes on the buyer's **receipt** (physical possession);
   - non-merchant seller: on **tender**.

> Risk of loss is not the same as title. Title passes under § 2-401 (on completion of the seller's delivery obligations unless otherwise agreed), but it does not decide risk.

**10. Title, voidable title and entrustment (§ 2-403; R-CTR-081).**
- A good-faith purchaser for value takes good title from a holder of **voidable** title: one who obtained the goods by deception about identity, by a dishonored check, in a cash sale, or by larceny-type fraud.
- A thief has **void** title.
- **Entrustment** of goods to a merchant who deals in that kind of goods empowers the merchant to transfer all the entruster's rights to a buyer in the ordinary course of business.

**11. Seller's rights when the buyer is insolvent (§ 2-702).**
- Refuse delivery except for cash.
- Reclaim goods received on credit by demanding them within 10 days after receipt. The 10-day limit does not apply if the buyer misrepresented its solvency in writing within 3 months before delivery.
- Reclamation is subject to the rights of a buyer in the ordinary course or other good-faith purchaser.

**How tested.**
- **MBE favorites:** risk-of-loss sequences (merchant vs. non-merchant; F.O.B.), cure, revocation, installment contracts, and the buyer who used the goods (acceptance).
- **Essays:** perfect tender plus risk of loss under an F.O.B. term (Jul 2022 Q1); revocation of acceptance of defective equipment (Feb 2021 Q2) [VERIFY V-071].

### IV.G Impossibility, impracticability, and frustration of purpose (R-CTR-082 to R-CTR-084)
**Common elements:**
1. A **supervening** event, occurring after formation. For events that already existed at formation, see R2d § 266 or treat as mistake.
2. Its non-occurrence was a **basic assumption** of the contract.
3. The party seeking excuse was **not at fault**.
4. That party did **not assume the risk**, expressly or impliedly — foreseeable risks and fixed-price contracts suggest it did.

| Doctrine | Test | Classic cases | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Impossibility** | Objectively impossible: no one could perform | *Taylor v. Caldwell* (1863) (music hall burned) | Triggers: destruction of the specific subject matter (§ 263); death or incapacity of a person necessary for **personal** services (§ 262); supervening illegality (§ 264). A builder of a new structure that burns must rebuild; a repairer of an existing structure is discharged |
| **Impracticability** | Excessive and unreasonable difficulty or expense | *Transatlantic Financing Corp. v. United States*, 363 F.2d 312 (D.C. Cir. 1966) (Suez closure; longer route not impracticable); *Mineral Park Land Co. v. Howard* (1916) 172 Cal. 289 (extreme cost) [VERIFY V-110] | **UCC § 2-615:** the seller is excused for delay or non-delivery caused by a contingency or by good-faith compliance with government regulation; it must allocate fairly among customers and notify. The buyer may terminate or modify (§ 2-616). Substitute performance is required if an agreed carrier or facility fails (§ 2-614). Market price swings are **not** enough |
| **Frustration of purpose** | The principal purpose, known to both parties, is substantially frustrated | *Krell v. Henry* [1903] 2 K.B. 740 (coronation room); *Lloyd v. Murphy* (1944) 25 Cal.2d 48 (lease not frustrated; value not totally destroyed; risk foreseeable) [VERIFY V-110] | The performance is still possible but worthless to one side. Very narrow |

- **Temporary** impracticability suspends the duty. **Partial** impracticability allows partial performance and allocation.
- **Force majeure clauses** are construed by their terms and may expand or narrow the default doctrines.
- **Consequences.** The duties are discharged, and either party may recover **restitution** for benefits conferred, or reliance in some cases (R2d §§ 272, 377).

**How tested.** MBE: price increases (no excuse); destruction of a specific subject vs. generic goods; a crop failure on a designated field; a supplier's own source failing (no excuse unless the source was contemplated as exclusive). Essays: frustration and impossibility appear in the State Bar's performance outline [S-088] [VERIFY V-024]; weave them into breach defenses.

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: PRC 情势变更 lets a court modify or terminate after a fundamental change, and 不可抗力 is a statutory defense; US doctrines only **discharge**, are narrow, and are often displaced by a contractual force majeure clause (approx.).

### IV.H Discharge of duties (accord and satisfaction, substituted contract, novation, rescission, release) (R-CTR-085 to R-CTR-088)
| Method | Rule | Authority |
|---|---|---|
| **Full performance** | Discharges the performing party's duty | — |
| **Mutual rescission** | An agreement to end the contract. Consideration is the mutual surrender of rights while both sides are executory. Oral is OK unless the SOF or a § 2-209(2) clause applies. Cannot defeat vested third-party beneficiary rights | R2d § 283; **CA** Civ. Code § 1689(a) |
| **Release** | A writing discharging a duty. Consideration is needed at common law; under the UCC a signed record suffices for claims arising from breach | R2d § 284; UCC § 1-306; **CA** Civ. Code §§ 1541, 1542 (a general release does not cover unknown claims unless waived) |
| **Accord and satisfaction** | An accord is an executory agreement to accept a substitute performance; satisfaction is its performance. Before satisfaction the original duty is suspended. If the debtor breaches the accord, the creditor may sue on the original duty or the accord. Requires a good-faith dispute or a new performance. A full-payment check on a disputed claim, if cashed, is an accord and satisfaction (UCC § 3-311) | R2d § 281; **CA** Civ. Code §§ 1521, 1523, 1524 [S-208] |
| **Substituted contract** | A new contract immediately replaces the old duty | R2d § 279 |
| **Novation** | A substituted contract with a new party, usually a new obligor; the original obligor is released with the obligee's consent | R2d § 280; **CA** Civ. Code §§ 1530, 1531 |
| **Account stated** | Agreement on a balance due becomes a new promise | — |
| **Material breach / repudiation** | Discharges the injured party's remaining duties (after the time for cure) | R2d §§ 242, 253 |
| **Impossibility, impracticability, frustration** | Discharge (IV.G) | R2d §§ 261, 265 |
| **Non-occurrence of a condition** | The duty never arises or is discharged | R2d § 225 |
| **Statute of limitations** | Bars the remedy, not the right. UCC: 4 years from tender (§ 2-725). **CA:** written contracts 4 years (CCP § 337); oral 2 years (CCP § 339) | — |
| **Merger into a judgment** | The claim merges into the judgment | — |

**How tested.** MBE: accord vs. substituted contract (does the creditor's breach-of-accord claim revive the original debt?); full-payment checks; novation vs. delegation. Essays: discharge appears in the State Bar outline [S-088] [VERIFY V-024], and "was the contract discharged?" often follows impossibility or frustration facts.

## V Remedies

> Essay map for remedies (the most-tested Contracts call: "What damages can each party recover?"):
> 1. Expectation damages: state the formula, compute with the facts' numbers, then add incidental and consequential damages.
> 2. Limits: foreseeability, certainty, mitigation.
> 3. A liquidated-damages clause, if any.
> 4. Alternatives: reliance, restitution.
> 5. Equitable remedies: specific performance, rescission, reformation.
> 6. The breaching party's restitution.
>
> Contracts + Remedies is a top crossover pair (4 essays) [S-079]. Expectation damages are the most-tagged Contracts issue (7 essays) [VERIFY V-071].

### V.A Expectation interest (direct, incidental, and consequential damages) (R-CTR-089 to R-CTR-091, R-CTR-094, R-CTR-099 to R-CTR-103)
- **Purpose.** Put the injured party where full performance would have put it (R2d §§ 344(a), 347; *Hawkins v. McGee*, 84 N.H. 114 (1929); **CA** Civ. Code §§ 3300, 3358).
- **Formula.** Loss in value + other loss (incidental + consequential) − cost avoided − loss avoided.

**Direct (general) damages by contract type:**
| Contract | Non-breaching party | Measure |
|---|---|---|
| Sale of goods | Buyer (no delivery, or rightful rejection or revocation) | Cover − contract price (§ 2-712), or market − contract price at the time the buyer learned of the breach (§ 2-713); plus incidental and consequential damages, minus expenses saved; plus refund of any price paid |
| Sale of goods | Buyer (accepted goods) | Value as warranted − value as delivered at acceptance (§ 2-714(2)), plus incidental and consequential damages |
| Sale of goods | Seller | Contract − resale (§ 2-706); contract − market at tender (§ 2-708(1)); lost profit for a lost-volume seller (§ 2-708(2)); price (§ 2-709); plus incidental damages (§ 2-710). **No consequential damages** |
| Land | Buyer | Market value − contract price at the time of breach, plus incidental damages (some states limit a good-faith seller's liability; Real Property) |
| Land | Seller | Contract price − market value at the time of breach |
| Construction | Owner | Cost to complete or repair (*Groves v. John Wunder Co.*, 205 Minn. 163 (1939)), unless economic waste, then diminution in value (*Jacob & Youngs*; *Peevyhouse v. Garland Coal & Mining Co.*, 382 P.2d 109 (Okla. 1962)); plus delay damages (often liquidated) |
| Construction | Builder | Before starting: lost profit. Mid-performance: profit + costs incurred − payments (= price − cost saved − payments). Completed: price |
| Employment | Employee | Remaining salary − actual or reasonably obtainable earnings in comparable work (*Parker v. Twentieth Century-Fox Film Corp.* (1970) 3 Cal.3d 176) |
| Employment | Employer | Extra cost of a replacement |

- **Incidental damages** (R-CTR-094): reasonable costs of dealing with the breach — inspection, storage, transportation of rejected goods, costs of cover or resale (§§ 2-710, 2-715(1)).
- **Consequential damages** (R-CTR-092): losses beyond the value of the performance itself, such as lost profits on collateral deals or injury to person or property, if foreseeable at formation (V.B).
- **Nominal damages** for breach without loss (R2d § 346(2); **CA** Civ. Code § 3360).
- **Not recoverable:** punitive damages for breach (R2d § 355; **CA** Civ. Code § 3294 limits punitives to obligations "not arising from contract"). Emotional distress is excluded except where bodily harm results or the contract's nature makes distress particularly likely (R2d § 353; *Erlich v. Menezes* (1999) 21 Cal.4th 543). Attorney's fees are excluded absent a contract or statute (**CA** Civ. Code § 1717 makes contractual fee clauses reciprocal).

### V.B Causation, certainty, and foreseeability (R-CTR-092, R-CTR-093)
- **Causation.** The breach must cause the loss ("but for," and a substantial factor).
- **Foreseeability (*Hadley v. Baxendale* (1854) 9 Ex. 341).**
  - **First limb, general damages:** losses arising naturally, in the usual course, from the breach.
  - **Second limb, special damages:** losses from special circumstances communicated to, or known by, the breaching party **at contracting**.
  - Codified in R2d § 351, UCC § 2-715(2)(a) and **CA** Civ. Code § 3300. *Lewis Jorge Construction Management, Inc. v. Pomona Unified School Dist.* (2004) 34 Cal.4th 960 applies *Hadley*: lost profits on future contracts are special damages that must have been foreseeable at formation [S-209] [VERIFY V-103].
  - The "tacit agreement" test (the breaching party must have agreed to bear the loss) is rejected by the UCC (§ 2-715 cmt. 2).
  - A court may limit damages for foreseeable loss where a disproportionate recovery would be unjust (R2d § 351(3)).
- **Certainty** (R2d § 352; **CA** Civ. Code § 3301).
  - The fact of loss must be proven with reasonable certainty, while the amount allows reasonable estimation.
  - **Lost profits:**
    - an established business uses its history;
    - a new business is traditionally barred (the "new business rule"), but modern courts allow proof by comparable businesses, market data or expert projections.
  - If expectation is too uncertain, fall back to reliance (§ 349) or restitution.
- **Essay technique.** For each item of damages, label it (direct, incidental or consequential), then test foreseeability and certainty in one or two sentences each, using the facts on what the breaching party knew at contracting.

### V.C Liquidated damages and penalties, and limitation of remedies (R-CTR-096, R-CTR-097)
- **Liquidated damages.**
  - **Test:** valid if reasonable in light of the anticipated or actual loss and the difficulty of proof (R2d § 356(1); UCC § 2-718(1)). The traditional formulation:
    1. damages difficult to estimate at formation;
    2. the amount a reasonable forecast;
    3. not intended as a penalty.
  - **Penalty:** void, and the plaintiff recovers actual damages instead. Labels do not control. A single lump sum for any breach, large or trivial, suggests a penalty.
  - **If valid:** the plaintiff recovers the stated amount, without proof of actual loss (majority), and no more. Some courts refuse when it is clear no loss occurred ("second look").
  - **Deposits** forfeited on a buyer's default are tested as liquidated damages. Under the UCC, absent a valid clause, the seller keeps the lesser of 20% or $500 (§ 2-718(2)(b)).
  - **CA:** Civ. Code § 1671.
    - In most contracts, the clause is **presumed valid**, and the challenger must prove it was unreasonable under the circumstances when made (§ 1671(b)).
    - In specified consumer contracts and residential leases, it is void unless actual damages would be impracticable or extremely difficult to fix (§ 1671(c)–(d)) [S-200] [VERIFY V-107].
    - Residential purchase deposits have their own rules (Civ. Code § 1675 et seq.) [VERIFY V-107].
- **Limitation of remedies (UCC § 2-719).**
  - The parties may substitute or limit remedies (repair or replace, return and refund). The limited remedy is **exclusive** only if expressly agreed.
  - **Failure of essential purpose:** if the limited remedy fails (repeated unsuccessful repairs), the buyer gets general Code remedies.
  - **Consequential-damages exclusions** are valid unless unconscionable. Personal injury from consumer goods is prima facie unconscionable; commercial loss is not.
  - Courts split on whether a consequential-damages exclusion survives the failure of a repair remedy (independent vs. dependent view).
- **Common-law analogues.** Exculpatory and limitation clauses are tested for public policy and unconscionability (II.E).

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: a PRC court may adjust 违约金 that is excessive or too low; a US court will not rewrite the clause, but enforces it or strikes it as a penalty (approx.).

### V.D Avoidable consequences and mitigation of damages (R-CTR-095)
- **Rule.** No recovery for losses the injured party could have avoided by reasonable efforts without undue risk, burden or humiliation (R2d § 350). The **breaching party bears the burden** of proving what could have been avoided. Reasonable mitigation expenses are recoverable even if unsuccessful.
- **Applications:**
  - **Repudiation:** stop work. Costs incurred after repudiation are not recoverable (*Rockingham County v. Luten Bridge Co.*, 35 F.2d 301 (4th Cir. 1929); *Clark v. Marsiglia*, 1 Denio 317 (N.Y. 1845)).
  - **Employment:** seek substantially similar employment in the same locality; need not accept different or inferior work (*Parker*).
  - **Goods:** the seller resells (§ 2-706) and may complete or salvage unfinished goods in reasonable commercial judgment (§ 2-704(2)). The buyer's failure to cover does not bar § 2-713 damages but can bar consequential losses that cover would have prevented (§ 2-715(2)(a)).
  - **Lost-volume sellers:** the resale is not mitigation (§ 2-708(2); *Neri v. Retail Marine Corp.*, 30 N.Y.2d 393 (1972)).
  - **Leases of real property:** majority and California landlords must mitigate (Real Property).
- **Essay tip.** Mitigation appears in the State Bar breach outline [S-088] [VERIFY V-024]. Every damages answer should include a short paragraph on it.

### V.E Rescission and reformation (R-CTR-105, R-CTR-106)
- **Rescission (unwinding).**
  - **Grounds:**
    - misrepresentation (fraudulent or material);
    - mutual mistake;
    - unilateral mistake under § 153;
    - duress; undue influence; incapacity;
    - material breach (the injured party may rescind and seek restitution instead of damages);
    - mutual agreement.
    - **CA** Civ. Code § 1689(b) adds failure of consideration and unlawfulness.
  - **Requirements:**
    - prompt action after discovery;
    - notice to the other party;
    - restoration, or an offer to restore, of everything received (**CA** Civ. Code § 1691);
    - a court then adjudges relief (§ 1692).
  - **Bars:**
    - affirmation or ratification after learning the facts;
    - unreasonable delay (laches), loss of the power of avoidance (R2d §§ 380–381 [VERIFY V-111]);
    - rights of bona fide purchasers;
    - unclean hands.
  - **Election.** Rescission and expectation damages are generally inconsistent. The UCC allows both for fraud (§ 2-721).
- **Reformation (rewriting the writing to match the deal).**
  - **Grounds:**
    - mutual mistake in expression (a scrivener's error) (R2d § 155);
    - fraud as to the writing's contents, or a unilateral mistake known to the other party (§ 166).
  - **Proof:** clear and convincing evidence of the prior agreement (most courts).
  - **Not barred** by the parol evidence rule, the SOF, or the plaintiff's negligence in failing to read (§ 157).
  - **Bars:** a bona fide purchaser for value; laches; unclean hands.
  - **CA:** Civ. Code § 3399.
- **How tested.**
  - Essays: rescission of a property purchase for misrepresentation (Feb 2018 Q1); rescission of an insurance policy and reformation of coverage (Feb 2016 Q4); omitted mineral-rights terms (Jul 2023 Q5) [VERIFY V-071].
  - Structure: ground → requirements → defenses → result. Cross-reference Remedies (REM) for equitable defenses.

### V.F Specific performance and injunction (R-CTR-104)
- **Elements:**
  1. A valid contract with **definite and certain** terms.
  2. The plaintiff's conditions are satisfied, or the plaintiff is ready, willing and able.
  3. An **inadequate legal remedy** — land (always unique), unique or rare goods (§ 2-716: "unique or in other proper circumstances," such as a requirements contract where cover is unavailable), or damages too speculative.
  4. **Feasibility** of enforcement. No personal services; courts avoid long, complex supervision.
  5. **No equitable defenses:**
     - laches;
     - unclean hands;
     - unconscionability or inadequate consideration (**CA** Civ. Code § 3391 [VERIFY V-108]);
     - mistake or misrepresentation;
     - sale to a bona fide purchaser;
     - the SOF, unless part performance applies.
- **Mutuality of remedy** is no longer required if the plaintiff's performance is assured (**CA** Civ. Code § 3386 [VERIFY V-108]).
- **CA:** breach of an agreement to transfer real property is presumed not adequately relieved by money; the presumption is conclusive for a single-family dwelling the buyer intends to occupy (Civ. Code § 3387). Obligations to render personal service, and contracts too uncertain, are not specifically enforceable (§ 3390) [VERIFY V-108].
- **UCC replevin** (§ 2-716(3)): for goods identified to the contract where the buyer cannot cover after reasonable effort.
- **Personal-service contracts.** No specific performance (involuntary servitude; supervision). A **negative injunction** may bar a performer with unique skills from working for a competitor during the contract term if reasonable, and if it does not leave the person unable to earn a living (*Lumley v. Wagner* (1852)).
- **How tested.** Essays: specific performance of a unique chattel (Jul 2017 Q3, chess piece; Remedies crossover); refusal of specific performance for an artist's personal services (Feb 2020 Q3); land deals (Jul 2019 Q5) [VERIFY V-071]. Pair it with TRO and preliminary-injunction analysis when the call asks (see the Remedies folder).

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: PRC 继续履行 is a routine remedy; in the US, specific performance is an exceptional equitable remedy that requires inadequate damages (approx.).

### V.G Reliance and restitution interests (R-CTR-107, R-CTR-108, R-CTR-018)
- **Reliance** (R2d §§ 344(b), 349):
  - expenditures made in preparation or performance;
  - the breaching party may deduct the loss the plaintiff would have suffered on a losing contract;
  - used when profits are uncertain (*Sullivan v. O'Connor*, 363 Mass. 579 (1973)) and for promissory estoppel.
- **Restitution** (R2d §§ 344(c), 370–377):
  - **measure** (§ 371): the reasonable value of the benefit (market cost to obtain it) or the increase in the defendant's wealth, whichever is just;
  - **available to the non-breaching party on total breach** (§ 373), except where it has fully performed and only a liquidated money debt remains;
  - **ceiling:** courts split on capping restitution at the contract price (the R2d does not cap it for the injured party; many courts cap it, especially for completed performance).
  - **Also available** where the contract is unenforceable under the SOF (§ 375), voidable (§ 376), or discharged by impracticability or frustration (§ 377).
- **Quasi-contract** where there is no contract at all (I.A.8).
- **Election.** The plaintiff chooses expectation, reliance or restitution, and cannot double-recover.
- **How tested.** Essays: restitution as an alternative remedy in a services dispute (Jul 2014 Q1) [VERIFY V-071]; recovery when no enforceable contract exists.

### V.H Remedial rights of breaching parties (R-CTR-109, R-CTR-065, R-CTR-067)
- **Substantial performance** lets a breaching party recover the contract price less damages (IV.C).
- **Divisibility** lets it recover for completed installments.
- **Restitution for a party in breach** (R2d § 374; *Britton v. Turner*, 6 N.H. 481 (1834)): the value of the benefit conferred, minus the injured party's damages, and never more than a pro rata share of the contract price.
  - Some courts deny recovery to a **willful** breacher.
  - **CA:** even a willfully defaulting land buyer recovers the excess of the deposit over the seller's actual damages (*Freedman v. Rector, Wardens & Vestrymen of St. Matthias Parish* (1951) 37 Cal.2d 16) [S-210]; relief from forfeiture under Civ. Code § 3275.
- **UCC § 2-718(2)–(3).** A buyer whose breach leads the seller to withhold delivery recovers its payments in excess of (a) a valid liquidated-damages amount, or (b) absent one, the lesser of 20% of the price or $500. This is offset by the seller's proven damages and the value of benefits the buyer received.
- **Liquidated damages and deposits** may limit recovery if valid (V.C).

## VI Third-party rights (essay: third-party beneficiaries; assignment and delegation)

### VI.A Third-party beneficiaries (R-CTR-110, R-CTR-111)
- **Intended vs. incidental** (R2d § 302). Recognizing a right in the beneficiary must be appropriate to effectuate the parties' intention, and either:
  - (a) performance satisfies the promisee's money obligation to the beneficiary (creditor beneficiary: *Lawrence v. Fox*, 20 N.Y. 268 (1859)); or
  - (b) the promisee intends to give the beneficiary the benefit (donee beneficiary: *Seaver v. Ransom*, 224 N.Y. 233 (1918)).
  - **Indicators:** the beneficiary is named; performance runs directly to it; the promisee's relationship and motive.
  - **Incidental beneficiaries** (neighbors, a supplier who would benefit, members of the public under government contracts) have **no rights** (*H.R. Moch Co. v. Rensselaer Water Co.*, 247 N.Y. 160 (1928)).
- **CA:** Civ. Code § 1559 ("expressly for the benefit of a third person"). *Goonewardene v. ADP, LLC* (2019) 6 Cal.5th 817 requires:
  - (1) the third party would in fact benefit;
  - (2) a **motivating purpose** of the contracting parties was to provide that benefit; and
  - (3) allowing the third party to sue is consistent with the contract's objectives and the parties' reasonable expectations [S-203].
- **Vesting** (R2d § 311). Before vesting, the parties may modify or rescind freely, unless the contract says otherwise. Rights vest when the beneficiary:
  - manifests assent at the request of a party;
  - sues; or
  - materially changes position in justifiable reliance.
  - A contract may reserve a power to change beneficiaries (insurance).
- **Defenses** (§ 309). The promisor may assert against the beneficiary any defense it has against the promisee arising from the contract: fraud, mistake, the promisee's non-performance, failure of a condition. It may **not** assert against the beneficiary its claims or defenses against the promisee that arise outside the contract (e.g., set-off of an unrelated debt).
- **Who sues whom:**
  - beneficiary v. promisor;
  - creditor beneficiary v. promisee (on the original debt);
  - promisee v. promisor (damages, or specific performance for a donee beneficiary).
  - A donee beneficiary generally cannot sue the promisee.

### VI.B Assignment of rights and delegation of duties (R-CTR-112 to R-CTR-116)
**Vocabulary.**
- **Assignment** transfers **rights**: assignor → assignee; the obligor owes performance.
- **Delegation** transfers performance of **duties**: delegator → delegate; the obligee receives performance.
- A transfer of "the contract" or "all my rights under the contract" is an assignment of rights and a delegation of duties. The assignee's acceptance is a promise to perform (R2d § 328; UCC § 2-210).

**Assignment.**
- **Form.** A present intent to transfer; no writing (unless the SOF or another statute requires one) and no consideration needed; partial assignments are OK (R2d § 324). Future rights under an existing contract are assignable.
- **Not assignable** (§ 317(2)):
  - if it would **materially change** the obligor's duty, increase its burden or risk, or impair its chance of return performance (personal services; a requirements buyer's rights, sometimes; insurance coverage);
  - if forbidden by law (e.g., some wage assignments);
  - if validly precluded by contract.
  - **CA:** Civ. Code § 954.
- **Anti-assignment clauses** (§ 322; UCC § 2-210):
  - "the contract" bars only **delegation**;
  - a prohibition of assignment of rights gives a damages claim but does not void the assignment, unless the clause says assignments are "void";
  - clauses restricting assignment of **rights to payment** are ineffective (UCC § 9-406(d)).
- **Assignee's position** (§ 336):
  - subject to all defenses arising from the contract;
  - subject to other defenses and claims accruing before the obligor got **notice**.
  - Payment to the assignor before notice discharges the obligor (§ 338). After notice, the obligor must pay the assignee.
- **Revocability.** An assignment for value is irrevocable. A gratuitous assignment is revocable (death, a later assignment, notice) unless written and signed, accompanied by a delivered token, or relied on (§ 332).
- **Successive assignments** (§ 342).
  - Majority (American): first in time wins, unless a later assignee for value without notice first obtains payment, a judgment, a novation, or a delivered token.
  - Minority (English): first to notify the obligor.
  - Article 9 filing rules govern sales of accounts.
- **Warranties of the assignor for value:** it will not defeat the right; the right exists and is not subject to undisclosed defenses; it has not made another assignment. It does **not** warrant the obligor's solvency.

**Delegation.**
- **Delegable** unless:
  - the obligee has a **substantial interest** in personal performance (artistic or professional skill, personal trust);
  - the contract prohibits it; or
  - a statute or policy forbids it.
- **Delegator remains liable** (as a surety, in effect) unless there is a **novation**.
- **Delegate's liability:** to the obligee only if it **assumes** the duty (promises the delegator, for consideration, to perform; the obligee is a third-party beneficiary). A mere delegate who does not assume is not liable to the obligee.
- **UCC:** the obligee may treat a delegation as reasonable grounds for insecurity and demand assurance from the delegate (§ 2-210).

**How tested.** MBE: an anti-assignment clause with or without "void"; the obligor paying the assignor before notice; delegation of personal services; the delegate's liability after assumption; successive assignments; vesting of beneficiary rights. Essays: third-party rights appear in the State Bar formation outline [S-088] [VERIFY V-024] and occasionally in fact patterns (an agent's commission, a buyer assigning a purchase contract).
