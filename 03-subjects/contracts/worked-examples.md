# Contracts — worked examples

All fact patterns are original. Each gives the **rule** (by ID), a short **application** that argues both sides where the facts allow, and a **conclusion**, in the shape a California grader expects.

---

### WE-01 — Mixed goods and services (R-CTR-001)
**Facts.** Hotel H hires Landscaper L to design and plant a garden for $60,000: design $10,000, plants and trees $35,000, labor $15,000. L abandons the job halfway.

**Analysis.** Article 2 governs goods; mixed contracts follow their predominant purpose.
- **For the UCC:** most of the price ($35,000) is for plants, which are movable goods.
- **For the common law:** H hired a landscaper, a service provider, for a design-and-build result. The design and labor ($25,000) are integral, and the contract is framed around services.

**Conclusion.** It is a close call. A court likely finds services predominate, because H bargained for a finished landscape, so the common law governs. A good answer states both tests and notes that the outcome (material breach, damages) would be similar either way.

### WE-02 — Revocation vs. mailbox (R-CTR-007, R-CTR-015)
**Facts.** On May 1, S emails B an offer to sell her used piano for $3,000, "open until May 10." On May 4, B mails a letter of acceptance. On May 5, S calls B and says, "I've sold the piano." B's letter arrives May 7.

**Analysis.**
- An offer is revocable despite a promise to hold it open, unless consideration or another doctrine makes it irrevocable. S is not a merchant in pianos, and B paid nothing.
- A revocation is effective on receipt (May 5). An acceptance is effective on dispatch (May 4), if mail is a reasonable medium. Responding by mail to an emailed offer for a used piano is likely reasonable.

**Conclusion.** A contract was formed on May 4, and S is in breach.

### WE-03 — Merchant's firm offer (R-CTR-009)
**Facts.** Lumber Co. emails Builder a signed quote: "We guarantee $600 per thousand board feet on 50,000 ft. through June 30." It is April 1. On May 15, prices jump, and Lumber Co. withdraws the quote. Builder accepts on May 20.

**Analysis.**
- Lumber Co. is a merchant, and the signed email gives an assurance that the offer will be held open, so § 2-205 applies.
- The stated period is about three months (April 1 to June 30), within the three-month cap.

**Conclusion.** The offer was irrevocable, the withdrawal was ineffective, and a contract was formed on May 20.

### WE-04 — Battle of the forms (R-CTR-013, R-CTR-014)
**Facts.** Retailer R sends a purchase order for 1,000 lamps. It says nothing about disputes. Manufacturer M's acknowledgment says "All disputes to binding arbitration" and "Seller warrants only against defects for 30 days; all other warranties disclaimed." Both are merchants. The lamps ship, and R pays.

**Analysis.**
- M's acknowledgment is a definite acceptance, not expressly conditional, so a contract is formed on the writings.
- Arbitration is an **additional** term. It becomes part of the contract unless it materially alters the deal; courts split, and many treat it as material (surprise).
- The warranty disclaimer is a material alteration (surprise or hardship), so it drops out, and the § 2-314 merchantability warranty applies.

**Conclusion.** The contract includes merchantability. The arbitration clause is likely out as a material alteration, though the courts split.

### WE-05 — Pre-existing duty and UCC modification (R-CTR-024, R-CTR-029)
**Facts.**
- (a) A contractor agreed to remodel a kitchen for $40,000. Midway, it says it underbid and needs $8,000 more. The owner agrees, then refuses to pay the extra.
- (b) A supplier of 10,000 steel brackets faces a 50% rise in steel costs after a mill shutdown. The buyer agrees to pay 15% more, then refuses.

**Analysis.**
- (a) This is common law. The contractor gave nothing new, and an underbid is not an unanticipated circumstance under R2d § 89. **CA:** under Civ. Code § 1698, an oral modification of a written contract also needs new consideration unless executed (only a written modification of an oral contract needs none, § 1697).
- (b) This is UCC § 2-209: no consideration needed, and the mill shutdown is a legitimate commercial reason, so the modification was made in good faith. Check the SOF: the modified contract for goods of $500 or more must satisfy § 2-201.

**Conclusion.** (a) The extra promise is unenforceable. (b) The modification is enforceable if a sufficient writing exists.

### WE-06 — Promissory estoppel (R-CTR-027)
**Facts.** Employer E tells long-time employee W, "If you retire now, we'll pay you $2,000 a month for life." W retires at 62. Three years later, E stops paying. W is now 65 and not re-employable.

**Analysis.**
- There is no bargain: E did not seek W's retirement in exchange.
- But E should reasonably have expected W to rely by retiring, W did rely, and W cannot now return to work. Injustice can be avoided only by enforcement.

**Conclusion.** The promise is enforceable under § 90, likely in full given the irreversible reliance.

### WE-07 — Statute of frauds: one year and suretyship (R-CTR-044, R-CTR-045)
**Facts.** On March 1, Manager M is orally hired "for 12 months starting April 1." On the same day, M's father tells the landlord renting M an apartment, "If my son doesn't pay rent, I will."

**Analysis.**
- The employment contract cannot be performed within one year of its making (March 1 to March 31 of the next year), so it is within the statute and unenforceable against the employer absent a writing or an exception.
- The father's promise is collateral, made to the creditor, to pay another's debt. It is within the suretyship provision, and the main purpose rule does not apply, because the father gains no economic benefit.

**Conclusion.** Both are unenforceable without a signed writing. M may recover the value of work actually performed in restitution.

### WE-08 — UCC statute of frauds: merchant confirmation and special manufacture (R-CTR-047)
**Facts.** Bakery B (a merchant) phones Supplier S (a merchant) and orders 400 custom-printed boxes bearing B's logo at $2 each. S begins printing. B emails a cancellation. S never sent a confirmation.

**Analysis.**
- The goods are $800, so § 2-201 applies, and B signed nothing.
- **Specially manufactured goods exception:** logo boxes are not suitable for sale to others in S's ordinary course, and S made a substantial beginning before B's repudiation.

**Conclusion.** The contract is enforceable despite the statute. S may sue for the price (§ 2-709), since the goods cannot be resold.

### WE-09 — Parol evidence and a merger clause (R-CTR-049, R-CTR-050, R-CTR-052)
**Facts.** A written contract to sell a vintage guitar for $12,000 has a merger clause. The buyer claims that, at signing, the seller orally (1) promised to include a hard case, and (2) said the guitar had all original parts, which is false.

**Analysis.**
- **(1) Case.** This is a consistent additional term, offered against what the merger clause shows to be a complete integration. Under § 2-202, it is excluded if it would certainly have been included in the writing. A case is the kind of item that would typically be listed, so it is likely excluded. **CA:** *Masterson* asks whether it would naturally have been included, with a similar likely result.
- **(2) Originality.** This is offered to prove fraud or misrepresentation, which the rule does not bar. **CA:** *Riverisland* admits fraud evidence even if it contradicts the writing.

**Conclusion.** The case promise is likely excluded. The originality statement is admissible to support rescission or damages for fraud.

### WE-10 — Mutual mistake and risk allocation (R-CTR-034)
**Facts.** An estate-sale seller and a buyer both believe a vase is a 1970s reproduction. They agree on $200, "sold as is." It is a Ming-era piece worth $90,000.

**Analysis.**
- Both parties were mistaken about a basic assumption (the vase's identity and age), with a material effect on the exchange.
- **Risk:** "as is" allocates the risk of defects in quality. Whether it covers the item's very identity is arguable. The seller also chose to sell without appraisal (conscious ignorance).

**Conclusion.** The seller likely bore the risk, under the "as is" clause and conscious ignorance, so rescission is likely denied. Argue both sides.

### WE-11 — Misrepresentation and rescission (R-CTR-037, R-CTR-105)
**Facts.** The seller of a restaurant building tells the buyer, "The kitchen passed its health inspection last month." In fact, it failed. The buyer closes, learns the truth two weeks later, promptly notifies the seller, and offers to reconvey.

**Analysis.**
- It is a false assertion of fact, and material: inspection status matters to a restaurant buyer.
- It induced the purchase, and the buyer's reliance was justified (no reason to doubt it).
- The rescission requirements (prompt notice, offer to restore) are met.

**Conclusion.** The buyer may rescind and recover the price. If the seller knew the statement was false, the buyer could instead sue for fraud damages, including punitive damages in California (Civ. Code § 3294).

### WE-12 — Express condition and excuse (R-CTR-062, R-CTR-064)
**Facts.** A home sale is "subject to Buyer obtaining financing at no more than 7% by June 1." The buyer never applies for a loan and on June 2 says the condition failed.

**Analysis.**
- Financing is an express condition for the buyer's benefit, but the buyer has an implied duty to make good-faith efforts to satisfy it.
- By not applying, the buyer hindered the condition, and the prevention doctrine excuses it.

**Conclusion.** The buyer cannot rely on the failed condition. Its refusal to close is a breach.

### WE-13 — Material breach, time of the essence (R-CTR-066)
**Facts.** Grader G contracts to finish site work for $900,000 "in about 4 months"; there is no time-of-the-essence clause. G finishes 85% of the work in 4 months and asks for 3 more weeks. The owner terminates and hires another firm.

**Analysis.**
- **For a minor breach:** 85% is done, the delay is short, G is acting in good faith, damages (delay costs) can compensate, and termination forfeits G's profit.
- **For a material breach:** if the owner's financing or tenant deadlines depended on timing, and G knew it, the owner lost a substantial benefit.

**Conclusion.** Without a time-of-the-essence clause or known deadline facts, the breach is likely minor. The owner's termination is then a material breach, and G recovers its expected profit, less the owner's delay damages.

### WE-14 — Anticipatory repudiation and mitigation (R-CTR-068, R-CTR-095)
**Facts.** In January, a distributor contracts to buy 2,000 bicycles at $300 each for April delivery. In February it writes, "We will not take the bicycles." The manufacturer finishes building them anyway and then sues for the full price.

**Analysis.**
- The February letter is an unequivocal repudiation.
- The manufacturer could cancel and sue. Under § 2-704(2) it may complete goods only in reasonable commercial judgment to minimize loss. Bicycles are readily resellable, so the price action (§ 2-709) is unavailable.

**Conclusion.** The manufacturer recovers the contract price minus the resale or market price, plus incidental damages. If it has unlimited capacity, it may claim lost profits as a lost-volume seller.

### WE-15 — Perfect tender, cure and risk of loss (R-CTR-072, R-CTR-073, R-CTR-079)
**Facts.** A contract for 500 blue office chairs, "F.O.B. Seller's warehouse," delivery by March 31. On March 10 the seller ships 500 gray chairs. They arrive March 15; the buyer rejects them the same day with notice. The seller promises blue chairs by March 25, but a warehouse fire on March 20 destroys them.

**Analysis.**
- **Rejection** was proper under perfect tender (§ 2-601), timely and with notice.
- **Cure:** time remained, so the seller could cure by March 31 (§ 2-508(1)).
- **Risk:** the gray chairs were nonconforming, so risk never passed to the buyer (§ 2-510(1)). The destroyed blue chairs were still in the seller's warehouse, never delivered to a carrier, so the risk was the seller's (§ 2-509(1)).

**Conclusion.** The seller bears the loss and must still deliver conforming chairs by March 31 or be in breach.

### WE-16 — Revocation of acceptance and limited remedy (R-CTR-075, R-CTR-097)
**Facts.** A landscaping company buys a $45,000 commercial mower with an exclusive "repair or replace parts" remedy. Over two months the mower stalls repeatedly, and four repair attempts fail.

**Analysis.**
- A recurring defect that defeats the mower's use substantially impairs its value.
- The defect was latent at acceptance, or acceptance rested on assurances of repair.
- Four failed repairs mean the exclusive remedy has failed of its essential purpose (§ 2-719(2)).

**Conclusion.** The buyer may revoke acceptance (§ 2-608), recover the price, cover, and claim incidental damages and, unless validly excluded, consequential damages.

### WE-17 — Warranty disclaimers (R-CTR-058 to R-CTR-060)
**Facts.** A used-equipment dealer sells a generator. The contract says, in small grey text at the bottom of page 3: "Seller makes no warranties beyond those stated." The dealer told the buyer, "This unit runs 10 hours on a tank."

**Analysis.**
- **Express warranty:** "runs 10 hours" is an affirmation of fact, and the boilerplate cannot negate it (§ 2-316(1)).
- **Merchantability:** the dealer is a merchant. The disclaimer does not mention merchantability and is not conspicuous.
- **Fitness:** there are no facts that the dealer knew of a particular purpose.

**Conclusion.** The express warranty and merchantability are both in force.

### WE-18 — Impracticability vs. price increase (R-CTR-083)
**Facts.**
- (a) A grain dealer contracts to supply 10,000 bushels of wheat; drought doubles the market price.
- (b) A farmer contracts to sell "all wheat grown on Smith Farm, est. 10,000 bushels"; drought destroys the Smith Farm crop.

**Analysis.**
- (a) A price increase is a risk a fixed-price seller assumes, and wheat can be bought elsewhere.
- (b) The specific source contemplated by both parties failed, and its continued existence was a basic assumption (§ 2-615; § 2-613 analogy).

**Conclusion.** (a) No excuse. (b) Excused, with any partial crop allocated to the buyer.

### WE-19 — Accord and satisfaction (R-CTR-087)
**Facts.** A consultant bills $15,000. The client, honestly claiming the work was late, sends a check for $9,000 conspicuously marked "Full and final payment of disputed invoice #42." The consultant crosses out the notation and deposits the check.

**Analysis.**
- There is a bona fide dispute, and a good-faith tender with a conspicuous full-satisfaction statement.
- Under UCC § 3-311, obtaining payment discharges the claim, and crossing out the notation does not prevent the accord.

**Conclusion.** The claim is discharged, unless a statutory exception applies (for example, timely repayment within the statutory window).

### WE-20 — Expectation damages with consequential and liquidated damages (R-CTR-089, R-CTR-092, R-CTR-096)
**Facts.** A caterer orders a $30,000 walk-in freezer for delivery by May 1. The contract says: "Seller pays $5,000 if delivery is late." The caterer told the seller about a June wedding contract worth $20,000 in profit. Delivery comes June 15. The caterer had to cancel the wedding job and rented a temporary freezer for $2,000.

**Analysis.**
- **Liquidated damages.** Is $5,000 a reasonable forecast of hard-to-estimate delay losses? Arguably yes for ordinary delays. **CA:** it is presumed valid under Civ. Code § 1671(b) unless the seller proves it unreasonable.
- **Effect of a valid clause.** If valid, it caps the delay damages at $5,000, and the caterer cannot also recover the lost wedding profit.
- **If the clause is invalid (a penalty or not exclusive).** The lost $20,000 profit was foreseeable, because the seller knew of the wedding (*Hadley*'s second limb). The $2,000 rental was a reasonable mitigation cost. Certainty: the wedding profit is specific and provable.

**Conclusion.** The likely result is the $5,000 liquidated sum. If the clause is struck, the caterer recovers $22,000.

### WE-21 — Specific performance of a unique chattel (R-CTR-104)
**Facts.** A collector agrees to buy a signed 1952 first-edition novel for $40,000. Before delivery, the seller gets a better offer and refuses to deliver.

**Analysis.**
- There is a valid contract, and the buyer is ready to pay.
- A rare signed edition is unique, so cover is impossible and damages are inadequate (§ 2-716).
- A delivery order is easy to supervise. There are no equitable defenses (the price is fair, no laches).

**Conclusion.** Specific performance is likely granted. A TRO or preliminary injunction could preserve the book pending suit (see Remedies).

### WE-22 — Breaching buyer's deposit (R-CTR-109)
**Facts.** A buyer pays a $6,000 deposit on a $40,000 boat, then backs out. The contract has no liquidated-damages clause. The seller resells for $40,000, with $500 of extra advertising costs.

**Analysis.**
- Under § 2-718(2)(b), the seller keeps the lesser of 20% ($8,000) or $500, so $500. The buyer's restitution claim is $5,500.
- Under § 2-718(3), that restitution is subject to offset for damages the seller proves: here the $500 of incidental (advertising) costs. If the seller is a lost-volume dealer, it may also offset its lost profit.

**Conclusion.** The buyer recovers about $5,000 ($6,000 − $500 statutory retention − $500 proven incidental damages), less any lost profit a lost-volume seller proves.

### WE-23 — Third-party beneficiary and vesting (R-CTR-110, R-CTR-111)
**Facts.** Dana sells her bakery to Eli. Eli promises Dana to pay Dana's $25,000 equipment loan to Bank. Before Bank learns of the deal, Dana and Eli agree that Eli will pay Dana instead. Bank then sues Eli.

**Analysis.**
- Bank is an intended (creditor) beneficiary, because performance satisfies Dana's debt to Bank.
- Its rights had not vested: no assent, suit or reliance before the modification.

**Conclusion.** The modification binds Bank, which cannot sue Eli. It may still sue Dana on the original loan.

### WE-24 — Assignment, notice and delegation (R-CTR-113, R-CTR-114, R-CTR-116)
**Facts.** Painter P contracts to paint Owner O's office for $20,000. The contract says, "Rights under this contract may not be assigned." P assigns the right to payment to Lender L and delegates the work to Crew C. C paints poorly. O, not yet notified of the assignment, pays P.

**Analysis.**
- The anti-assignment clause lacks "void" language, and rights to payment are freely assignable, so the assignment is effective. But O paid P **before notice**, so O is discharged as against L.
- The delegation is likely permissible (routine painting, not personal artistry). P remains liable for C's poor work. C is liable to O only if it assumed P's duty.

**Conclusion.** L must look to P. O may sue P (and C, if C assumed the duty) for defective performance.
