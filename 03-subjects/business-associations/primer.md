# Business Associations — primer

Read this first. It explains what the subject is about, how the pieces fit together, and the ideas that decide most essays. Detail is in [`outline.md`](outline.md); exam wording is in [`rule-statements.md`](rule-statements.md).

## 1. What the subject is
Business Associations answers three questions about any business:

1. **What is it?** A sole proprietorship, a general partnership, a limited partnership, an LLP, an LLC, or a corporation? Many essays begin here, because the answer decides who pays.
2. **Who can bind it, and who pays when it owes money?** Agency law decides whether a person's contract or tort binds the business. Entity law decides whether the owners must pay the business's debts personally.
3. **Who runs it, and what do the managers owe the owners?** Directors, officers, partners and LLC managers are fiduciaries. Shareholders can sue them, usually on the corporation's behalf.

Federal law adds a fourth layer for corporations: **securities fraud and insider trading**.

## 2. Why the entity matters: the liability ladder
Think of entities as a ladder of liability protection:

| Entity | Formed by | Owners personally liable for business debts? |
|---|---|---|
| Sole proprietorship | Nothing (one owner) | Yes, fully |
| General partnership | Nothing (two or more co-owners in business for profit) | Yes, jointly and severally |
| Limited partnership | Filing a certificate | General partners yes; limited partners no |
| LLP | Partnership + registration | No, except for own wrongs |
| LLC | Filing articles of organization | No (unless veil pierced) |
| Corporation | Filing articles of incorporation | No (unless veil pierced) |

**The key idea:** limited liability is a privilege the state gives in exchange for a **filing**. If no one filed anything, there is no shield: two people running a business together are a **general partnership by default**, whatever they call it. That single rule drives several past essays (Feb 2020, Feb 2022, Jul 2025).

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: PRC entities generally exist only after registration (登记). In the US, a general partnership exists the moment two people run a business together for profit, with no registration — a frequent trap for civil-law readers (approx.).

## 3. Agency: the wiring that connects people to entities
An entity can act only through people. **Agency** decides when a person's act counts as the entity's act.
- **Contracts.** The principal is bound if the agent had **actual authority** (the principal told the agent, expressly or by implication), **apparent authority** (the principal's conduct led the third party to reasonably believe the agent had authority), or the principal **ratified** the act afterward.
- **Torts.** An employer is liable for an employee's torts committed **within the scope of employment** (respondeat superior), even if the employer was careful. Independent contractors are different: generally no vicarious liability.

Partners are agents of their partnership by statute; officers are agents of their corporation; LLC members or managers are agents of their LLC (in California, by statute).

## 4. Corporations: the separation of ownership and control
A corporation separates **owners** (shareholders) from **managers** (directors, who appoint officers). That separation creates the subject's central tension: managers control other people's money.
- **Shareholders** elect directors, vote on fundamental changes (mergers, sales of all assets, amending the articles), inspect records, and sue.
- **Directors** manage, owing a **duty of care** (be informed, act in good faith) and a **duty of loyalty** (do not put yourself ahead of the corporation).
- **The business judgment rule** keeps courts out of honest, informed, disinterested business decisions, even bad ones. It falls away when a director has a conflict, acts in bad faith, or decides without information.

## 5. Key vocabulary
| Term | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| Actual authority | Permission the principal gave the agent (express or implied) |
| Apparent (ostensible) authority | Authority a third party reasonably believes the agent has, because of the principal's conduct |
| Ratification | The principal's later approval of an unauthorized act |
| Respondeat superior | Employer's strict liability for employees' torts within the scope of employment |
| Promoter | Person who arranges a corporation's formation and signs contracts for it before it exists |
| De facto corporation | A failed but good-faith attempt to incorporate, treated as a corporation |
| Piercing the veil (alter ego) | Holding owners personally liable because they abused the corporate form |
| Business judgment rule (BJR) | Presumption that directors acted on an informed basis, in good faith, without conflict |
| Self-dealing / interested-director transaction | A deal between the corporation and a director (or the director's company) |
| Corporate opportunity | A business chance that belongs to the corporation, which a director may not take without offering it first |
| Derivative suit | A shareholder's suit on the corporation's behalf; any recovery goes to the corporation |
| Demand futility | Excuse for not first asking the board to sue, because the board is conflicted |
| Insider trading | Trading on material nonpublic information in breach of a duty |
| Section 16(b) | Strict rule forcing insiders of public companies to return profits from trades within six months |
| Dissociation / dissolution | A partner leaving / the partnership ending and winding up |
| Equitable subordination | A bankruptcy court putting an insider's claim behind other creditors because of inequitable conduct |

## 6. How the pieces connect on an essay
Most BA essays are **liability chains**. For each debt or injury:

```
Who acted? → Was that person an agent/employee/partner/officer of the business? (agency)
          → Is the business bound or liable? (authority; respondeat superior; § 16305)
          → What kind of business is it? (formation; filing; defective incorporation)
          → Are the owners personally liable? (partnership liability; promoter liability; veil piercing; own tort)
```

Shareholder-suit essays are **director-by-director**:

```
Direct or derivative? → Standing, demand, bond (procedure)
→ For each director: duty of care → BJR → duty of loyalty → safe harbors / fairness → defenses (exculpation, reliance)
→ Remedy
```

## 7. The ideas that matter most
1. **No filing, no shield.** Co-owners of an unfiled business are general partners, jointly and severally liable (Corp. Code §§ 16202, 16306).
2. **Authority runs from the principal, not the agent.** Apparent authority must be traceable to the principal's manifestations; an agent cannot bootstrap its own authority.
3. **Partners bind the partnership in the ordinary course** unless the third party knew of the limit (§ 16301). Secret internal limits do not protect the partnership.
4. **Promoters stay liable** on pre-incorporation contracts even after the corporation adopts them, unless there is a novation.
5. **Veil piercing needs two things**: unity of interest (undercapitalization, commingling, no formalities) **and** an injustice. Undercapitalization plus siphoning money out is the classic winner.
6. **The BJR protects process, not results.** Ask: Were the directors informed? Disinterested? In good faith? If yes, the decision stands. If a director had a conflict, move to loyalty and the safe harbors.
7. **Derivative suits have hurdles**: contemporaneous ownership, demand or futility, and (in California) a bond of up to $50,000.
8. **Insider trading requires a duty**: insiders (classical), tippees of insiders who tip for a personal benefit, and misappropriators who deceive their source. Section 16(b) is different: strict liability, public companies only, six-month window.

## 8. How California fits in
Essays default to general principles [S-077]. For Business Associations that means the Restatement of Agency, RUPA, the MBCA and Delaware case law. California's Corporations Code mostly agrees, and its partnership statute copies RUPA. The differences worth one sentence each (cumulative voting, distribution tests, interested-director approvals, the derivative-suit bond, the limited-partner control rule, LLC agency, LLPs limited to professions, § 2115) are in [`ca-distinctions.md`](ca-distinctions.md).

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: the duty of loyalty and duty of care correspond to 忠实义务 and 勤勉义务 in the PRC Company Law; what is new is the procedural machinery — demand, derivative standing and the business judgment rule — that decides whether a court will look at the merits at all (approx.).
