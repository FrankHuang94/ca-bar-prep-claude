## I. Agency

### R-BUS-001 — Agency: definition and formation
**Rule (write this):** Agency is the fiduciary relationship that arises when one person (the principal) manifests assent to another (the agent) that the agent shall act on the principal's behalf and subject to the principal's control, and the agent manifests assent or otherwise consents to so act. No writing, consideration or intent to create "an agency" is required; the test is objective.

**Elements:** 1. Assent (manifestation by the principal; consent by the agent). 2. The agent acts **on behalf of** (for the benefit of) the principal. 3. The principal has the **right to control** the agent (the ends, not necessarily the details).

**Exceptions / defenses:** The principal must have capacity to do the act itself (R3d Agency § 3.04); the agent needs only minimal capacity (a minor can be an agent, § 3.05). A gratuitous agent is still an agent and owes fiduciary duties. If the act itself requires a writing (e.g., a land sale), the agent's authority must also be in writing (equal dignities rule; R-CTR-048).

**Authority:** R3d Agency § 1.01 [VERIFY V-701]; Cal. Civ. Code §§ 2295, 2307, 2309 [VERIFY V-702].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. California's Civil Code states the same concept: an agent "represents another, called the principal, in dealings with third persons."

**Example:** Ann asks her neighbor Bo to pick up a lawn mower she ordered and pay for it with her card; Bo agrees. Bo is Ann's agent for that errand even though he is unpaid and nothing is in writing.

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: close to 委托代理 under the Civil Code, but US agency is a fiduciary status defined by consent plus control, and it also drives tort liability (respondeat superior) (approx.).

### R-BUS-002 — Employee vs. independent contractor
**Rule (write this):** Whether an agent is an employee (servant) or an independent contractor turns mainly on the principal's right to control the manner and means of the work. An employer is vicariously liable for an employee's torts within the scope of employment, but a hirer is generally not liable for the torts of an independent contractor.

**Elements:** Factors (R2d Agency § 220): extent of control over details; whether the worker runs a distinct business; whether the work is usually done under supervision; skill required; who supplies tools and the place of work; length of the relationship; payment by time or by the job; whether the work is part of the principal's regular business; the parties' belief.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Labels in a contract do not control. Even for independent contractors, the hirer remains liable for nondelegable duties, inherently dangerous activities and its own negligence in selecting or supervising (R-BUS-018).

**Authority:** R2d Agency § 220; R3d Agency § 7.07(3) [S-810]; *S.G. Borello & Sons, Inc. v. Department of Industrial Relations* (1989) 48 Cal.3d 341.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** general control test on the essay. California's ABC test (*Dynamex Operations West, Inc. v. Superior Court* (2018) 4 Cal.5th 903; Lab. Code § 2775) governs wage-and-hour and Labor Code status, not common-law respondeat superior; mention it only if the facts raise employment-law status.

**Example:** A courier company sets drivers' routes, hours and uniforms and pays by the hour. The drivers are employees, so the company answers for a driver's negligent delivery-route crash.

### R-BUS-003 — Agent's duty of loyalty
**Rule (write this):** An agent owes the principal a fiduciary duty of loyalty: to act solely for the principal's benefit in matters connected with the agency. The agent may not acquire a material benefit from third parties, act as or for an adverse party, compete with the principal, or use the principal's property or confidential information for the agent's own purposes, unless the principal consents after full disclosure.

**Elements:** 1. No secret profits or kickbacks. 2. No self-dealing or acting for an adverse party. 3. No competition during the agency (preparing to compete is allowed). 4. No misuse of the principal's property or confidential information, during or after the agency.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Informed consent by the principal, given in good faith after disclosure of all material facts, in a fair transaction. After termination, the agent may compete but may not use trade secrets or solicit using confidential lists.

**Authority:** R3d Agency §§ 8.01–8.05 [VERIFY V-701]; *Bancroft-Whitney Co. v. Glen* (1966) 64 Cal.2d 327.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A purchasing agent takes a 2% "thank-you" payment from a supplier she selects. She must account to her principal for the payment even if the price was fair.

**Remedies:** disgorgement of profits, constructive trust (R-REM-044), damages, forfeiture of compensation, rescission of the tainted contract.

### R-BUS-004 — Agent's duties of care, obedience, disclosure and accounting
**Rule (write this):** An agent must act with the care, competence and diligence normally exercised by agents in similar circumstances, act within actual authority and comply with the principal's lawful instructions, give the principal material information the agent knows or should know the principal would want, and keep and account for the principal's property separately.

**Elements:** Care and competence; obedience (actual authority, reasonable instructions); notice/disclosure of material facts; accounting and no commingling.

**Exceptions / defenses:** No duty to obey illegal or unethical instructions. A gratuitous agent's duty of performance is limited, but once it acts it must act with care.

**Authority:** R3d Agency §§ 8.08–8.12 [VERIFY V-701].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** An agent told to sell stock "only at $40 or more" sells at $35. He breached the duty of obedience and is liable for the $5 difference, even if the principal is bound to the buyer by apparent authority.

### R-BUS-005 — Principal's duties to the agent
**Rule (write this):** A principal must perform its contract with the agent (including compensation), deal fairly and in good faith, and indemnify the agent for payments and losses the agent incurs while acting within actual authority.

**Elements:** Compensation as agreed (or reasonable value if implied); reimbursement of authorized expenses; indemnification for liabilities incurred within actual authority; cooperation.

**Exceptions / defenses:** No indemnity for losses from the agent's own unauthorized or wrongful acts.

**Authority:** R3d Agency §§ 8.13–8.15 [VERIFY V-701]; Lab. Code § 2802 (employer must indemnify employee's necessary expenditures).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A sales agent pays a $500 shipping fee to close a sale the principal authorized. The principal must reimburse her.

### R-BUS-006 — Termination of the agent's actual authority
**Rule (write this):** Actual authority ends by the act of either party (the principal's revocation or the agent's renunciation, even if wrongful), by the agreed term or event, by changed circumstances the agent should realize the principal would not want the agent to act, or by operation of law (death, loss of capacity, bankruptcy in some cases, impossibility). The principal's death ends actual authority when the agent has notice of it.

**Elements:** 1. Acts of the parties: power to terminate exists even when there is no right to do so (wrongful termination gives damages, not continued authority). 2. Lapse of time or purpose achieved. 3. Operation of law.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A **power given as security** (a power coupled with an interest) is irrevocable and survives the principal's death or incapacity. Apparent authority may linger after termination (R-BUS-007).

**Authority:** R3d Agency § 3.07(2) (death: effective when the agent has notice) [S-1807]; §§ 3.06, 3.08–3.10, 3.12–3.13 [VERIFY V-701]; Cal. Civ. Code §§ 2355–2356 [VERIFY V-702].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A principal fires her agent on Monday though the contract runs to year-end. The agent's authority ended Monday; the principal owes contract damages for the early termination.

### R-BUS-007 — Lingering apparent authority after termination
**Rule (write this):** When actual authority ends, apparent authority continues as to a third party who reasonably believes the agent is still authorized, based on the principal's earlier manifestations, until the third party has notice of the termination.

**Elements:** 1. Prior manifestation by the principal to the third party (or a course of dealing). 2. The third party's reasonable belief, traceable to that manifestation. 3. No notice of termination.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Notice ends it: actual notice to those who dealt with the agent; reasonable general notice (publication) for others. Under R3d Agency, termination of actual authority does not by itself end apparent authority (§ 3.11), and the principal's death is effective against a third party only when that third party has notice of it (§ 3.07(2)) [S-1807].

**Authority:** R3d Agency § 3.11 [S-1158]; Cal. Civ. Code § 2356 [VERIFY V-702].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A distributor fires its sales rep but does not tell a long-standing customer. The rep takes the customer's $2,000 deposit and disappears. The distributor is bound and must honor the order.

### R-BUS-008 — Actual authority: express and implied
**Rule (write this):** An agent has actual authority to take action that the agent reasonably believes, in light of the principal's manifestations to the agent, the principal wishes the agent to take. Express authority is stated in words; implied authority covers acts reasonably necessary to accomplish the express authority, acts customary for such an agent, and acts suggested by prior acquiescence.

**Elements:** 1. Manifestation from **principal to agent**. 2. The agent's **reasonable belief** that it is authorized. 3. Implied authority: necessary or incidental acts, custom, past course of dealing, emergency.

**Exceptions / defenses:** No actual authority if the agent knows or should know the principal would not want the act (changed circumstances), or acts contrary to instructions.

**Authority:** R3d Agency §§ 2.01–2.02 [S-810] [VERIFY V-701]; Cal. Civ. Code §§ 2299, 2330 [VERIFY V-702].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. Write "actual (express or implied)" first in any contract-liability analysis, then apparent authority.

**Example:** A store manager hired "to run the shop" orders routine inventory and hires a cashier. Both acts are within implied actual authority even though no one listed them.

### R-BUS-009 — Apparent authority
**Rule (write this):** Apparent authority exists when a third party reasonably believes the agent has authority to act on behalf of the principal and that belief is traceable to the principal's manifestations to the third party. The principal is bound even though the agent lacked actual authority.

**Elements:** 1. A manifestation by the **principal to the third party** (words, conduct, title or position given to the agent, prior course of dealing, failure to correct a known misimpression). 2. The third party actually and **reasonably** believes the agent is authorized. 3. The belief is **traceable** to the principal, not just to the agent's own statements.

**Exceptions / defenses:** An agent cannot create its own apparent authority. A third party who knows of a limitation, or a transaction that is unusual enough to put a reasonable person on inquiry, defeats reasonableness. Apparent authority binds the principal; the agent who exceeded actual authority is liable to the principal (R-BUS-004).

**Authority:** R3d Agency §§ 2.03, 3.03 [S-810] [VERIFY V-701]; Cal. Civ. Code §§ 2300, 2317, 2334 (ostensible authority) [VERIFY V-702].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same core rule; California calls it **ostensible** authority, and its Civil Code binds the principal only to third parties who, in good faith and without want of ordinary care, incurred a liability or parted with value in reliance on it (Civ. Code § 2334) [VERIFY V-934]. One sentence: "California's Civil Code calls this ostensible authority (§ 2317) and requires the third party's good-faith, reasonable reliance (§ 2334)."

**Example:** A company names Lee "purchasing director" and lets him place orders for two years. A secret cap of $10,000 does not stop a supplier, unaware of the cap, from enforcing his $25,000 order.

### R-BUS-010 — Ratification
**Rule (write this):** A principal ratifies an act done without authority by manifesting assent to be bound or by conduct that justifies a reasonable assumption that it so consents (including knowingly accepting the benefits), with knowledge of the material facts. Ratification relates back and binds the principal as if the act had been authorized from the start.

**Elements:** 1. The act was done (or purported to be done) on the principal's behalf. 2. The principal **existed** when the act was done and has **capacity** when it ratifies (R3d § 4.04; older law also required capacity at the time of the act) [VERIFY V-701]. 3. The principal has **knowledge of material facts**. 4. **Affirmance** of the entire transaction (express, implied by conduct, or by retaining benefits). 5. Timeliness: before the third party withdraws or circumstances materially change.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Partial ratification is not allowed (all or nothing). A principal that did not exist when the act was done (a corporation not yet formed) cannot ratify in the strict sense; it **adopts** instead, effective from adoption (R-BUS-055).

**Authority:** R3d Agency §§ 4.01–4.08 [VERIFY V-701]; Cal. Civ. Code §§ 2307, 2310 [VERIFY V-702].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** Without authority, an employee signs a contract to buy a delivery van for her employer. The owner learns the terms, and the company uses the van for a month. The company has ratified and is bound to pay.

### R-BUS-011 — Estoppel, undisclosed principals and "inherent" authority
**Rule (write this):** A person who intentionally or carelessly causes a third party to believe an actor is its agent, or who knows of the belief and fails to correct it, is estopped to deny the agency to a third party who justifiably and detrimentally relies. An undisclosed principal may not rely on secret instructions that cut the agent's authority below what a third party would reasonably believe such an agent to have if the principal were disclosed, and is liable for an unauthorized act on which a third party justifiably and detrimentally relies if the principal, knowing of the agent's conduct, did not take reasonable steps to notify the third party.

**Elements:** Estoppel: 1. intentional or careless conduct (or failure to correct); 2. the third party's justifiable belief; 3. detrimental change of position. Undisclosed principal: 1. agent acts on the principal's behalf while the third party believes it deals with the agent alone; 2. the act is within the authority an agent in that position usually has, and the principal's restrictive instructions were secret; or 3. the principal knew of the agent's conduct and failed to notify a third party who relied.

**Exceptions / defenses:** R2d Agency used "inherent agency power" (§§ 8A, 161, 194) for these cases (*Watteau v. Fenwick* [1893] 1 Q.B. 346); R3d Agency drops that label and uses estoppel (§ 2.05) and the undisclosed-principal rule (§ 2.06) instead [VERIFY V-701].

**Authority:** R3d Agency §§ 2.05–2.06 [VERIFY V-701]; R2d Agency §§ 8A, 194.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. Use apparent authority first; turn to estoppel or inherent power only if apparent authority fails.

**Example:** A tavern owner lets its manager run the bar under the manager's own name and forbids buying cigars on credit. The manager buys cigars on credit from a supplier who thinks the manager owns the bar. The owner is liable as an undisclosed principal.

### R-BUS-012 — Agent's own liability on the contract
**Rule (write this):** An agent who makes an authorized contract for a **disclosed** principal (the third party knows both the agency and the principal's identity) is not a party to it. If the principal is **unidentified** (partially disclosed) or **undisclosed**, the agent is also a party and is personally liable, though the agent may seek indemnity from the principal.

**Elements:** 1. Classify the disclosure at the time of contracting. 2. Disclosed → principal alone liable (unless the agent agreed to be bound). 3. Unidentified → principal and agent both liable. 4. Undisclosed → agent liable; principal also liable once discovered; the third party must elect whom to hold to judgment in some jurisdictions.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Signing "Ann Lee, as agent for Blue Corp." discloses the principal. Signing "Ann Lee" alone, or "Ann Lee, agent," without naming the principal, risks personal liability.

**Authority:** R3d Agency §§ 6.01–6.03 [VERIFY V-701]; Cal. Civ. Code § 2343 [VERIFY V-702].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** An agent signs a lease "Raj, for a client" without naming Tech Inc. The landlord can hold Raj personally, and Tech Inc. as well once identified.

### R-BUS-013 — Implied warranty of authority
**Rule (write this):** A person who purports to make a contract on behalf of a principal impliedly warrants that he has authority to do so. If he lacks actual authority and the principal is not bound (no apparent authority or ratification), the purported agent is liable to the third party for the loss caused, unless the agent disclaimed the warranty or the third party knew of the lack of authority.

**Elements:** 1. Purported agent acts for a principal. 2. No actual authority. 3. Principal not bound. 4. Third party's loss (benefit of the bargain with the principal).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Disclaimer; third party's knowledge; ratification by the principal ends the claim.

**Authority:** R3d Agency § 6.10 [VERIFY V-701]; Cal. Civ. Code § 2342 (one who assumes to act as an agent warrants the authority assumed) [VERIFY V-934]; § 2343 [VERIFY V-702].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A son signs a sale of his father's car "for Dad" without permission; the father refuses to deliver. The buyer can recover from the son the extra cost of a comparable car.

### R-BUS-014 — Respondeat superior: scope of employment
**Rule (write this):** An employer is vicariously liable for torts committed by its employee acting within the scope of employment. An employee acts within the scope of employment when performing work assigned by the employer or engaging in a course of conduct subject to the employer's control; conduct is outside the scope when it occurs within an independent course of conduct not intended by the employee to serve any purpose of the employer.

**Elements:** 1. **Employee** (right to control; R-BUS-002). 2. **Tort** by the employee. 3. **Within scope**: the kind of work the employee was hired to do; substantially within authorized time and space limits; motivated at least in part to serve the employer (R2d Agency § 228 factors).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Liability is strict and vicarious; the employer's own care is no defense. Forbidden acts can still be within scope if they are the employee's improper way of doing the job.

**Authority:** R3d Agency §§ 2.04, 7.07 [S-810]; R2d Agency §§ 219, 228; Cal. Civ. Code § 2338; *Perez v. Van Groningen & Sons, Inc.* (1986) 41 Cal.3d 962.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same core rule. California asks whether the risk is "typical of or broadly incidental to" the enterprise, i.e., foreseeable from the nature of the work (*Lisa M. v. Henry Mayo Newhall Memorial Hospital* (1995) 12 Cal.4th 291).

**Example:** A farm worker, against his employer's rule, lets a child ride on the tractor while disking a field, and the child is hurt. The worker was still doing his assigned job, so the employer is liable (*Perez*).

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: similar in result to the employer's liability for staff causing harm "in performing work tasks" (用人单位责任) in the PRC Civil Code, but US law asks about scope of employment and is strict, with no fault-based defense for the employer (approx.).

### R-BUS-015 — Frolic, detour, and going and coming
**Rule (write this):** A minor deviation from the employer's business (a **detour**) remains within the scope of employment, but a substantial departure for the employee's own purposes (a **frolic**) is outside it until the employee returns to the employer's business. Under the going-and-coming rule, ordinary commuting is outside the scope of employment, subject to exceptions where the trip benefits the employer.

**Elements:** Detour vs. frolic factors: the employee's intent, the time and distance of the deviation, and whether it was foreseeable. Going-and-coming exceptions: travel the employer pays for or requires; a vehicle the employer requires for work; special errands; trips that serve a business purpose.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A combined personal-and-business trip is within scope if the business purpose was a concurrent cause.

**Authority:** R2d Agency § 228; R3d Agency § 7.07 cmt. [VERIFY V-701]; *Hinman v. Westinghouse Electric Co.* (1970) 2 Cal.3d 956 (travel time paid by employer); *Moradi v. Marsh USA, Inc.* (2013) 219 Cal.App.4th 886 (required-vehicle exception) [VERIFY V-727].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. On the essay, a firm policy requiring employees to carry and answer work phones at all times (Jul 2023 Q1) invites a two-sided argument on whether a call taken while driving was work [VERIFY V-071].

**Example:** A delivery driver stops two blocks off-route for coffee and hits a pedestrian — detour, within scope. The same driver drives 30 miles to visit a friend on a day off — frolic, outside scope.

### R-BUS-016 — Intentional torts of employees
**Rule (write this):** An employer is liable for an employee's intentional tort only if it was within the scope of employment: the tort was committed at least partly to serve the employer, force was a natural incident of the job, or (in California) the risk was engendered by or arose from the employment so that the tort is a generally foreseeable consequence of the enterprise.

**Elements:** 1. Motive to serve the employer, or 2. the job naturally involves force or confrontation (bouncers, security, repossession), or 3. California: a causal nexus between the work and the tort, and the tort is not so unusual that charging the loss to the enterprise would be unfair.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Purely personal malice (a sexual assault unrelated to job duties, a fight over a personal grudge) is outside the scope. The employer may still be directly liable for negligent hiring or supervision (R-BUS-018).

**Authority:** R2d Agency §§ 228(1)(d), 245; *Lisa M. v. Henry Mayo Newhall Memorial Hospital* (1995) 12 Cal.4th 291; *Mary M. v. City of Los Angeles* (1991) 54 Cal.3d 202 (police officer's abuse of authority).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** general rule on the essay; add the California "engendered by the employment" test where facts are close.

**Example:** A nightclub bouncer punches a patron while ejecting him. The force was incident to his job, so the club is liable even though the punch was excessive.

### R-BUS-017 — Independent contractors: hirer's liability
**Rule (write this):** A person who hires an independent contractor is generally not vicariously liable for the contractor's torts. Exceptions: the hirer is liable for inherently or abnormally dangerous activities, for breach of a nondelegable duty (e.g., duties owed to invitees or imposed by statute), and for its own negligence in selecting, instructing or supervising the contractor.

**Elements:** 1. Independent-contractor status (R-BUS-002). 2. If so, test each exception.

**Exceptions / defenses:** California limits the "peculiar risk" doctrine as to the contractor's own employees (*Privette v. Superior Court* (1993) 5 Cal.4th 689).

**Authority:** Restatement (Second) of Torts §§ 409–429; *Privette v. Superior Court* (1993) 5 Cal.4th 689.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same general rule.

**Example:** A store hires a licensed blasting contractor to clear land, and flying rock damages a neighbor's house. The store is liable because blasting is abnormally dangerous.

### R-BUS-018 — Principal's direct liability; apparent agency in tort
**Rule (write this):** Apart from vicarious liability, a principal is directly liable for its own negligence in hiring, training, supervising or retaining an agent, and for torts it authorized or ratified. A principal is also liable when an agent with apparent authority commits a tort (such as fraud) in the course of dealing with a third party who relies on that appearance.

**Elements:** Negligent hiring/supervision: 1. the principal knew or should have known of the agent's unfitness; 2. the unfitness caused the harm. Apparent authority torts: 1. agent acts with apparent authority; 2. tort is accomplished through that apparent authority.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Standard negligence defenses (comparative fault) apply to the direct-liability theory.

**Authority:** R3d Agency §§ 7.03, 7.05, 7.08 [VERIFY V-701]; Cal. Civ. Code § 2338.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A bank's branch manager, held out as authorized to accept deposits, pockets a customer's cash "for a special CD." The bank is liable because the fraud was carried out through apparent authority.

### R-BUS-019 — Agent's own tort liability
**Rule (write this):** An agent is personally liable for his own torts even when acting within the scope of the agency and for the principal's benefit; the principal's vicarious liability is additional, not substitutive. The injured party may sue both, and an employer who pays may seek indemnity from the employee.

**Elements:** 1. The agent committed the tort. 2. Agency is no defense.

**Exceptions / defenses:** An agent is not liable merely for the principal's torts in which the agent did not participate.

**Authority:** R3d Agency § 7.01 [VERIFY V-701].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. This is why corporate officers who personally commit or direct torts are liable (R-BUS-050).

**Example:** A partner's associate rear-ends a car while driving to a client meeting. The associate is personally liable; the firm is vicariously liable too.

### R-BUS-020 — Notice and knowledge imputed to the principal
**Rule (write this):** Notice of a fact that an agent knows or has reason to know is imputed to the principal if the knowledge is material to the agent's duties to the principal, unless the agent acts adversely to the principal (for the agent's own purposes or another's) or is subject to a duty not to disclose. Notice given to an agent is effective as notice to the principal if the agent has actual or apparent authority to receive it.

**Elements:** 1. Agent's knowledge or notice. 2. Material to duties. 3. No adverse-interest exception.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Adverse-interest exception (the agent is defrauding the principal), except where needed to protect an innocent third party who dealt with the principal in good faith.

**Authority:** R3d Agency §§ 5.01–5.04 [VERIFY V-701]; Cal. Civ. Code § 2332.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A property manager learns the building's roof leaks but does not tell the owner. The owner is treated as knowing of the leak in a later tenant's suit.

## II. General partnerships

### R-BUS-021 — Formation of a general partnership
**Rule (write this):** A general partnership is formed by the association of two or more persons to carry on as co-owners a business for profit, whether or not they intend to form a partnership. No filing, writing or formal agreement is required; the parties' conduct controls, and the label they use is not decisive.

**Elements:** 1. Two or more persons (individuals or entities). 2. **Carrying on** a business (an ongoing activity, not mere co-ownership of property). 3. As **co-owners** (sharing control and profits). 4. **For profit** (the aim, not success).

**Exceptions / defenses:** An entity formed by filing under another statute (corporation, LLC, LP) is not a general partnership. Co-ownership of property, even with shared rents, does not by itself create a partnership. An oral partnership agreement need not settle every term (*Holmes v. Lerner* (1999) 74 Cal.App.4th 442).

**Authority:** Corp. Code § 16202(a)–(b); RUPA § 202.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. This is the classic threshold issue: when owners "start a business" without filing anything, write "General partnership by default" first (Feb 2020 Q5; Feb 2022 Q5; Jul 2025 Q3) [VERIFY V-071].

**Example:** Ann, Bob and Claire pool savings, open a shoe store, share management, and agree to split profits equally, but file nothing. They are general partners, whatever they call the venture.

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: like a 普通合伙企业 in liability terms, but a US general partnership arises by conduct alone, with no registration, so owners can be partners without knowing it (approx.).

### R-BUS-022 — Profit sharing: presumption and exceptions
**Rule (write this):** A person who receives a share of the profits of a business is presumed to be a partner, unless the profits were received in payment of a debt, as wages or other compensation to an employee or independent contractor, as rent, as an annuity or retirement benefit, as interest or other charge on a loan (even if it varies with profits), or for the sale of the goodwill of a business.

**Elements:** 1. Share of **profits** (not gross revenue). 2. No listed exception. 3. Weigh other indicia: control/management rights, contribution of capital, sharing of losses, how the parties held themselves out, joint title to business property.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Sharing **gross returns** does not by itself establish a partnership. The presumption is rebuttable.

**Authority:** Corp. Code § 16202(c)(3); RUPA § 202(c)(3).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A lender advances $100,000 for "10% of annual profits until repaid" and has no say in management. The profit share is interest on a loan; the lender is not a partner.

### R-BUS-023 — Partnership by estoppel (purported partner)
**Rule (write this):** A person who represents himself, or consents to being represented by another, as a partner in an existing partnership or with others not actually partners, is liable to a third party who extends credit or enters a transaction in reliance on the representation. Where the representation is made publicly, liability extends to anyone who relies on it, whether or not the purported partner knew of that third party.

**Elements:** 1. Representation of partnership status by the defendant, or **consent** to another's representation. 2. Third party's **reliance** (credit extended or transaction entered). 3. Resulting obligation.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Mere silence or failure to deny a representation the defendant did not make or authorize generally is not consent. If all partners consent to the representation, the purported partner binds them as if a partner; otherwise only those consenting are bound.

**Authority:** Corp. Code § 16308; RUPA § 308.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A retired dentist lets her former practice keep her name on its letterhead as "partner." A supplier extends credit after reading the letterhead. She is liable as a purported partner.

### R-BUS-024 — Partnership as an entity; partnership property
**Rule (write this):** A partnership is an entity distinct from its partners. Property acquired by the partnership, or in the partnership's name, or by a partner with partnership funds, is partnership property, not property of the individual partners; a partner cannot transfer it individually.

**Elements:** Property is partnership property if acquired 1. in the partnership's name, or 2. by a partner with an instrument indicating the partner's capacity as partner. Property bought with partnership assets is **presumed** partnership property even if titled in a partner's name.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Property acquired in a partner's name without indication of partnership capacity and without partnership funds is presumed separate property, even if used for the business.

**Authority:** Corp. Code §§ 16201, 16203, 16204, 16501; RUPA §§ 201, 203–204, 501.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** Partners buy a delivery truck with partnership funds and title it in one partner's name. The truck is presumed partnership property.

### R-BUS-025 — Partner's authority to bind the partnership
**Rule (write this):** Each partner is an agent of the partnership for the purpose of its business. An act of a partner for apparently carrying on in the ordinary course the partnership business, or business of the kind carried on by the partnership, binds the partnership, unless the partner had no authority to act for the partnership in the particular matter and the third party knew or had received notification that the partner lacked authority.

**Elements:** 1. Partner's act. 2. Apparently in the **ordinary course** of the partnership's business or of business of that kind. 3. Third party **lacked knowledge or notification** of the restriction.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A secret limit in the partnership agreement does not bind a third party without knowledge. A filed statement of partnership authority can limit authority to transfer real property (R-BUS-026). Actual authority is analyzed under agency principles (R-BUS-008).

**Authority:** Corp. Code § 16301(1); RUPA § 301(1).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. Analyze actual authority first (agreement, majority vote), then apparent authority under § 16301(1).

**Example:** The partners of a shoe store agree that no partner may buy more than $10,000 of inventory alone. Ann orders $50,000 of shoes from Big Shoe Co., which knows nothing of the limit. The partnership is bound (Jul 2025 Q3 pattern).

### R-BUS-026 — Acts outside the ordinary course; statements of authority
**Rule (write this):** An act of a partner that is not apparently for carrying on the partnership business in the ordinary course binds the partnership only if the other partners actually authorized it. A statement of partnership authority filed with the Secretary of State may grant or limit partners' authority; a filed limitation on a partner's authority to transfer partnership real property is constructive notice once a certified copy of the filed statement is recorded in the office for recording transfers of that property.

**Elements:** 1. Extraordinary act (selling the business's main asset, admitting a partner, guaranteeing a stranger's debt, confessing judgment). 2. Requires **all** partners' consent (or actual authority under the agreement).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Ratification by the partnership. Other filed limits (non-real-property) bind only those with actual knowledge.

**Authority:** Corp. Code §§ 16301(2), 16303, 16401(j); RUPA §§ 301(2), 303(e)–(f), 401(j).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** One partner of a bakery sells the bakery's building without the others' consent. The sale is outside the ordinary course and does not bind the partnership unless the others authorized or ratify it.

### R-BUS-027 — Partnership liability for partners' torts and breaches
**Rule (write this):** A partnership is liable for loss or injury caused to a person, or for a penalty incurred, as a result of a wrongful act or omission, or other actionable conduct, of a partner acting in the ordinary course of business of the partnership or with the authority of the partnership. It is also liable when a partner, acting within apparent authority, misapplies money or property received from a third person.

**Elements:** 1. Partner's wrongful act. 2. In the **ordinary course** of partnership business (scope-of-business analogue of respondeat superior) **or** with authority.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A partner's purely personal frolic is outside the ordinary course. For employees (non-partners) of the firm, use respondeat superior (R-BUS-014).

**Authority:** Corp. Code § 16305; RUPA § 305.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A law-firm partner, driving to court for a firm client, negligently injures a pedestrian. The firm is liable under § 16305, and the other partners are personally liable under § 16306 unless the firm is an LLP (Jul 2023 Q1 pattern).

### R-BUS-028 — Partners' joint and several personal liability
**Rule (write this):** All partners are liable jointly and severally for all obligations of the partnership, whether arising in contract or tort, unless otherwise agreed by the claimant or provided by law (for example, registration as an LLP).

**Elements:** 1. A partnership obligation. 2. The defendant was a partner when it was incurred. 3. No LLP shield or creditor agreement.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A person admitted as a partner is not personally liable for obligations incurred before admission (R-BUS-030). LLP partners are shielded (R-BUS-045). The creditor generally must exhaust partnership assets first (R-BUS-029).

**Authority:** Corp. Code § 16306(a)–(c); RUPA § 306.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A partnership defaults on a $30,000 bank loan. Each partner is personally liable for the full $30,000, subject to the exhaustion rule and contribution among partners.

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: PRC general partners also bear unlimited joint liability (无限连带责任) for partnership debts, so this rule should feel familiar; the difference is that a US partnership can arise without any registration (approx.).

### R-BUS-029 — Exhaustion rule
**Rule (write this):** A judgment against a partner based on a partnership obligation may not be satisfied from the partner's personal assets unless a judgment on the same claim has been obtained against the partnership and a writ of execution on it has been returned unsatisfied, the partnership is a debtor in bankruptcy, the partner agreed that the creditor need not exhaust, a court permits it because partnership assets are clearly insufficient or exhaustion would be excessively burdensome, or liability is imposed on the partner independently of the partnership obligation.

**Elements:** 1. Sue the partnership and the partners (may be in the same action). 2. Levy on partnership assets first. 3. Then reach personal assets, unless an exception applies.

**Exceptions / defenses:** The partner's own tort is an independent basis of liability, so no exhaustion is needed against the tortfeasor-partner.

**Authority:** Corp. Code § 16307(c)–(d); RUPA § 307(c)–(d).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. One line on the essay: "Creditors must first exhaust partnership assets (§ 16307(d)), except against the partner who committed the tort."

**Example:** A tort victim wins a judgment against the firm and all partners. She may levy immediately on the negligent partner's house, but on the other partners' personal assets only after the firm's assets prove insufficient.

### R-BUS-030 — Incoming and dissociated partners' liability
**Rule (write this):** A person admitted as a partner into an existing partnership is not personally liable for partnership obligations incurred before admission, though the incoming partner's capital contribution is at risk for them. A dissociated partner remains liable for obligations incurred before dissociation and, for a limited time, for transactions with third parties who reasonably believed the person was still a partner and had no notice of the dissociation.

**Elements:** 1. When was the obligation incurred relative to admission or dissociation? 2. For post-dissociation transactions: the other party's reasonable belief and lack of notice, within the statutory window (two years under RUPA), cut off earlier by a filed statement of dissociation (effective notice after 90 days).

**Exceptions / defenses:** A creditor may release a dissociated partner by agreement or novation; material alteration of the obligation without the dissociated partner's consent can discharge him.

**Authority:** Corp. Code §§ 16306(b), 16702–16704; RUPA §§ 306(b), 702–704 [VERIFY V-723].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** Dee joins a partnership in March; a supplier's December invoice predates her admission. Dee's personal assets are not reachable for it, but her capital in the firm is.

### R-BUS-031 — Partners' management rights and voting
**Rule (write this):** Absent agreement, each partner has equal rights in the management and conduct of the partnership business. A difference arising in a matter in the ordinary course of business may be decided by a majority of the partners, but an act outside the ordinary course, and any amendment of the partnership agreement, requires the consent of all partners.

**Elements:** 1. Equal (per capita, not per capital) management rights. 2. Ordinary matters → majority. 3. Extraordinary matters and amendments → unanimity. 4. Admission of a new partner → unanimity.

**Exceptions / defenses:** The partnership agreement may vary these defaults (e.g., weighted votes, a managing partner).

**Authority:** Corp. Code § 16401(f), (i), (j); RUPA § 401(f), (i), (j).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** Two of three partners vote to open a second store in another city, a step outside the ordinary course. Absent agreement, the third partner's objection blocks it.

### R-BUS-032 — Profits, losses, compensation and capital
**Rule (write this):** Absent agreement, each partner is entitled to an equal share of the partnership profits and is chargeable with a share of the losses in proportion to the partner's share of the profits. A partner is not entitled to remuneration for services, except reasonable compensation for winding up, and is entitled to reimbursement for payments made and indemnity for liabilities incurred in the ordinary course.

**Elements:** 1. Profits: equal, regardless of capital contributed. 2. Losses: follow the profit-sharing ratio. 3. No salary by default. 4. Advances beyond agreed capital are loans earning interest.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Service-only partner: in *Kovacik v. Reed* (1957) 49 Cal.2d 166 the California Supreme Court held that where one party contributes only money and the other only services, and they did not agree to share losses, the service partner need not contribute to the capital loss [S-818]. Whether *Kovacik* survives § 16401(b) is unsettled; the uniform act's official comment cites *Kovacik* as contrary case law and applies the default loss-sharing rule even to a partner who contributed no capital [VERIFY V-706].

**Authority:** Corp. Code § 16401(a)–(e), (h); RUPA § 401.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** general rule: losses follow profits. On a California essay, cite *Kovacik* as the counter-argument when one partner gave only labor.

**Example:** Kim contributes $100,000; Lu contributes skills. They agree to split profits 50/50 and say nothing about losses. The business loses all $100,000. Under § 16401(b) Lu owes Kim $50,000; under *Kovacik* Lu owes nothing.

### R-BUS-033 — Information, accounting, transferable interest and charging orders
**Rule (write this):** Each partner has access to the partnership's books and records and a right to information about the business reasonably needed to exercise partner rights. A partner may sue the partnership or another partner for an accounting or other legal or equitable relief. A partner's only transferable interest is the partner's share of profits and losses and right to receive distributions; a transferee does not become a partner, and a judgment creditor of a partner reaches that interest only through a charging order.

**Elements:** Transfer: 1. assignment of economic rights only; 2. no management or information rights; 3. assignor remains a partner (until expelled or dissociated).

**Exceptions / defenses:** The other partners may consent to admit the transferee as a partner (unanimously, absent agreement).

**Authority:** Corp. Code §§ 16403, 16405, 16502–16504; RUPA §§ 403, 405, 502–504.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A partner's creditor obtains a charging order. Distributions due to the partner go to the creditor, but the creditor cannot vote or inspect books.

### R-BUS-034 — Partner's duty of loyalty
**Rule (write this):** A partner owes the partnership and the other partners a duty of loyalty, limited to: (1) accounting for and holding as trustee any property, profit or benefit derived from the conduct or winding up of the partnership business or the use of partnership property, including appropriation of a partnership opportunity; (2) refraining from dealing with the partnership as or for a party with an adverse interest; and (3) refraining from competing with the partnership before dissolution.

**Elements:** 1. Secret profits / partnership opportunity. 2. Self-dealing / adverse interest. 3. Competition. Disclosure plus consent by the other partners (or as the agreement permits) cures.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A partner does not violate a duty merely because the conduct furthers the partner's own interest. The agreement may identify specific activities that do not violate loyalty if not manifestly unreasonable, and all partners may authorize or ratify a specific act after full disclosure; the agreement may not eliminate the duty.

**Authority:** Corp. Code §§ 16103(b), 16404(b), (e); RUPA §§ 103, 404; *Meinhard v. Salmon*, 249 N.Y. 458, 164 N.E. 545 (1928) ("the punctilio of an honor the most sensitive").

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. Cite *Meinhard* for the classic standard, then the statutory list.

**Example:** A co-venturer managing a hotel lease is offered a larger redevelopment of the same site by the landlord and takes it alone without telling his partner. He usurped a partnership opportunity (*Meinhard*).

### R-BUS-035 — Partner's duty of care; good faith and fair dealing
**Rule (write this):** A partner's duty of care in conducting partnership business is limited to refraining from grossly negligent or reckless conduct, intentional misconduct, or a knowing violation of law. A partner must also discharge partnership duties and exercise rights consistently with the obligation of good faith and fair dealing.

**Elements:** 1. Gross negligence or recklessness (ordinary negligence is not enough). 2. Intentional misconduct. 3. Knowing violation of law.

**Exceptions / defenses:** The agreement may not unreasonably reduce the duty of care or eliminate good faith; it may set reasonable standards for measuring it.

**Authority:** Corp. Code §§ 16103(b), 16404(c)–(d); RUPA §§ 103, 404.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A partner forgets to renew the firm's insurance once through ordinary carelessness. That is ordinary negligence, not a breach of the § 16404(c) duty of care.

### R-BUS-036 — Dissociation
**Rule (write this):** A partner is dissociated on the partnership's notice of the partner's express will to withdraw, an agreed event, expulsion (under the agreement, by unanimous vote in specified cases, or by court order), bankruptcy, death, appointment of a guardian, or similar events. A partner always has the **power** to dissociate, but dissociation is **wrongful** if it breaches an express provision of the agreement or, in a partnership for a definite term or undertaking, occurs before the term ends (with limited exceptions).

**Elements:** 1. Dissociation event. 2. Wrongful? (breach of an express term; or, in a term partnership, early withdrawal, judicial expulsion or bankruptcy). 3. Wrongful dissociator is liable for damages caused.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Withdrawal from a term partnership within 90 days after another partner's death or wrongful dissociation is not wrongful.

**Authority:** Corp. Code §§ 16601–16602; RUPA §§ 601–602.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** In a five-year partnership, a partner quits in year two to take a job. He had the power to leave, but the dissociation is wrongful and he owes damages.

### R-BUS-037 — Effect of dissociation: buyout or dissolution
**Rule (write this):** Dissociation ends the partner's right to manage and the duty not to compete, but does not automatically dissolve the partnership. If the partnership is not dissolved and wound up, it must buy out the dissociated partner's interest at the greater of liquidation value or going-concern value without the dissociated partner, offset by damages for wrongful dissociation; a wrongful dissociator in a term partnership can be made to wait for payment until the term ends unless a court finds no undue hardship.

**Elements:** 1. Did dissociation trigger dissolution (R-BUS-038)? 2. If not → buyout under Article 7. 3. Buyout price and offsets.

**Exceptions / defenses:** The dissociated partner may bind the partnership for a limited period by apparent authority (R-BUS-030).

**Authority:** Corp. Code §§ 16603, 16701–16704; RUPA §§ 603, 701–704.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. Under the old UPA (1914), any departure caused dissolution; RUPA changed that — say so if the facts invite it.

**Example:** One of four partners in a term partnership retires early. The other three continue the business and must pay her the buyout price less the damages her early exit caused, possibly at the end of the term.

### R-BUS-038 — Dissolution events
**Rule (write this):** A partnership is dissolved, and its business must be wound up, on: (1) in an at-will partnership, notice of a partner's express will to withdraw; (2) in a term partnership, the expiration of the term, the express will of all partners, or a partner's death or wrongful dissociation followed within 90 days by the express will of at least half of the remaining partners to wind up; (3) an event agreed to cause dissolution; (4) an event making the business unlawful; or (5) a judicial decree (e.g., economic purpose unreasonably frustrated, a partner's conduct makes it not reasonably practicable to carry on, or it is otherwise not reasonably practicable to carry on in conformity with the agreement).

**Elements:** Identify the type (at will vs. term), then the event.

**Exceptions / defenses:** After dissolution, all partners (other than a wrongful dissociator) may waive the right to wind up and continue the business as if no dissolution occurred.

**Authority:** Corp. Code §§ 16801–16802; RUPA §§ 801–802.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** In an at-will partnership, a partner emails the others, "I'm out, effective now." The partnership is dissolved and must wind up unless all the partners agree to continue.

### R-BUS-039 — Winding up and settling accounts
**Rule (write this):** On winding up, partnership assets are applied first to discharge obligations to creditors, including partners who are creditors; any surplus is paid to partners in cash according to their capital accounts (contributions plus share of profits, less distributions and share of losses). A partner with a negative account must contribute the deficit; if a partner fails to contribute, the others contribute in the proportion they share losses.

**Elements:** 1. Pay outside and partner creditors. 2. Compute each capital account. 3. Distribute positive balances; collect negative balances. 4. Partners may bind the partnership for acts appropriate to winding up.

**Exceptions / defenses:** The agreement may set a different order or method.

**Authority:** Corp. Code §§ 16803–16804, 16807; RUPA §§ 803–804, 807.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A and B each contributed $20,000; losses total $30,000 shared equally. Each account is $5,000 after losses; once creditors are paid, each receives $5,000.

## III. Limited partnerships

### R-BUS-040 — Limited partnership: formation and structure
**Rule (write this):** A limited partnership is formed only by filing a certificate of limited partnership with the Secretary of State and has at least one general partner and at least one limited partner. General partners manage the business and are personally liable for its obligations; limited partners invest capital and are generally shielded from personal liability.

**Elements:** 1. Filed certificate (name, address, agent, general partners). 2. At least one GP and one LP. 3. Partnership agreement (may be oral) governs internal affairs, subject to nonwaivable statutory limits.

**Exceptions / defenses:** If the certificate is never filed, the venture is a general partnership and all owners face unlimited liability, subject to rules protecting a person who erroneously believed he was a limited partner and promptly withdrew or corrected the filing.

**Authority:** Uniform Limited Partnership Act of 2008, Corp. Code § 15900 et seq., incl. §§ 15902.01, 15901.10 [VERIFY V-930]; ULPA (2001) §§ 201, 110.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** Two developers file a certificate naming DevCo as general partner and admit twenty investors as limited partners. The investors' risk is limited to their contributions.

### R-BUS-041 — General partner in a limited partnership: liability and duties
**Rule (write this):** A general partner of a limited partnership has the rights and powers to manage the business, is an agent of the limited partnership, and is personally liable, jointly and severally, for all obligations of the limited partnership. A general partner owes the limited partnership and its partners duties of loyalty and care and the obligation of good faith and fair dealing, defined as in a general partnership.

**Elements:** Management authority; agency power; unlimited personal liability; fiduciary duties (loyalty: no secret profits, no adverse dealing, no competition; care: no gross negligence, recklessness, intentional misconduct or knowing violation of law).

**Exceptions / defenses:** A corporate or LLC general partner limits the human principals' exposure (they risk only veil piercing). Some states allow a limited liability limited partnership (LLLP) that shields general partners. California's Act registers and recognizes foreign LLLPs [S-1161], but whether a domestic California LP can elect LLLP status is not confirmed [VERIFY V-930].

**Authority:** Corp. Code §§ 15904.02, 15904.04, 15904.08 [VERIFY V-930]; ULPA (2001) §§ 402, 404, 408.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A general partner diverts a profitable property deal to his own company. He breached the duty of loyalty and must account to the LP.

### R-BUS-042 — Limited partner's liability shield; California's control rule
**Rule (write this):** A limited partner is not personally liable for the obligations of the limited partnership solely by reason of being a limited partner. Under the 2001 Uniform Act, the shield holds even if the limited partner participates in management and control. California's 2008 Act keeps a narrow control rule: a limited partner who participates in control may be liable as a general partner, but only to persons who transact business with the partnership with actual knowledge of that participation and a reasonable belief, based on the limited partner's conduct, that the limited partner is a general partner.

**Elements:** California exposure: 1. Participation in control. 2. Third party's **actual knowledge** of that participation. 3. Third party's **reasonable belief**, based on the LP's conduct, that the LP is a general partner. 4. Transaction with the partnership.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Activities listed in § 15903.03(b) are not participation in control — for example, consulting with or advising a general partner, serving as a fiduciary of a corporate or trust general partner, approving amendments to the partnership agreement, and calling or voting at partner meetings — and the list is not exclusive (§ 15903.03(c)) [S-1150]. The section also preserves a limited partner's liability for participating in tortious conduct, and a limited partner remains liable for any unpaid promised contribution.

**Authority:** Corp. Code § 15903.03 [S-808] [S-1150]; ULPA (2001) § 303; Revised ULPA (1976/1985) § 303 (control rule).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** **differs.** General law (ULPA 2001): full shield regardless of control. California: shield unless the narrow control-and-reliance test is met. Write both.

**Example:** A limited partner tells a lender, "I'm running this place now," and negotiates the loan herself. A lender who relied on that conduct may hold her liable as a general partner in California; under ULPA (2001) she would remain shielded.

### R-BUS-043 — Limited partners' rights; dissociation and dissolution of an LP
**Rule (write this):** Limited partners have information rights, voting rights on matters the agreement or statute reserves (such as amendments and dissolution), and the right to bring direct and derivative actions; they owe no fiduciary duty solely by reason of being limited partners. A limited partner has no right to withdraw and be paid out before the partnership ends (one who dissociates becomes a mere transferee, with no buyout); the LP is dissolved by agreed events, consent of all general partners and a majority of limited partners in interest, the loss of the last general partner without replacement, or judicial decree.

**Elements:** Derivative standing requires a demand on the general partners or a showing that demand would be futile.

**Exceptions / defenses:** The agreement may expand voting or exit rights within statutory limits.

**Authority:** Corp. Code §§ 15903.04–15903.06, 15906.01, 15908.01, 15910.02–15910.03 [VERIFY V-930]; ULPA (2001) §§ 304–306, 601, 801, 1002–1003.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule in outline.

**Example:** Limited partners suspect the general partner is overpaying an affiliate. After an unanswered demand, they may sue derivatively on behalf of the LP.

## IV. Limited liability partnerships

### R-BUS-044 — LLP: formation and California's profession limit
**Rule (write this):** A general partnership becomes a limited liability partnership by filing a registration (statement of qualification) with the Secretary of State, after approval by the partners as required for amending the agreement. In California, a domestic registered LLP may be formed only to practice law, public accountancy, architecture, engineering or land surveying, and it must maintain specified security for malpractice claims.

**Elements:** 1. Existing general partnership. 2. Partner approval. 3. Filed registration. 4. California: an eligible licensed profession and the required insurance or other security.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Without registration the firm is an ordinary general partnership with unlimited partner liability. The architecture, engineering and land-surveying categories have been subject to legislative sunset dates [VERIFY V-704].

**Authority:** Corp. Code §§ 16101, 16953, 16956 [S-809] [VERIFY V-704] [VERIFY V-933]; RUPA §§ 1001–1003.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** **differs.** General law: any partnership may register. California: professional firms only. A California law firm's limited-liability choices are an LLP or a professional law corporation, not an LLC (R-BUS-108).

**Example:** A three-lawyer firm that never filed anything is a general partnership; its partners face unlimited liability for one partner's malpractice (Feb 2020 Q5 pattern).

### R-BUS-045 — LLP liability shield and its limits
**Rule (write this):** A partner in a registered LLP is not personally liable, directly or by contribution, for debts, obligations or liabilities of the partnership, whether in contract, tort or otherwise, incurred while the partnership is an LLP, solely by reason of being a partner. The partnership itself remains fully liable, and each partner remains personally liable for the partner's own wrongful conduct.

**Elements:** 1. Obligation incurred **while registered**. 2. Liability asserted only because of partner status. 3. Partner's own torts and malpractice remain personal (California's § 16306 preserves liability for the partner's own tortious conduct; some other states' statutes also reach those under the partner's direct supervision and control).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Obligations incurred before registration remain under ordinary partnership rules. Specified partners may agree in writing to be liable for specified partnership obligations, and a partner may act as guarantor or surety or provide collateral (§ 16306) [S-1156].

**Authority:** Corp. Code § 16306 [S-809] [S-1156]; RUPA § 306(c) (full shield).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same full-shield rule; California adds the security requirement (R-BUS-044).

**Example:** A partner of AB Law LLP trades on a client's confidential plans and harms the client. The LLP and the trading partner are liable; the other partner is not personally liable merely for being a partner, unless he participated in the wrong or was himself negligent, for example in supervising (Jul 2014 Q3 pattern) [VERIFY V-071].

## V. Corporations — entity, veil piercing and formation

### R-BUS-046 — Corporation as a separate entity; shareholder limited liability
**Rule (write this):** A corporation is a legal person separate from its shareholders, directors and officers. Shareholders are not personally liable for the corporation's debts; their risk is limited to the amount they invested (plus any unpaid subscription for their shares). Directors and officers are not personally liable for corporate obligations merely because of their positions.

**Elements:** 1. Validly formed corporation (R-BUS-051). 2. Obligation belongs to the corporation. 3. No independent ground of personal liability (guarantee, own tort, veil piercing, illegal distribution, promoter liability).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Veil piercing (R-BUS-047); personal guarantees; promoter liability (R-BUS-054); personal participation in torts (R-BUS-050); receipt of unlawful distributions (R-BUS-080).

**Authority:** MBCA § 6.22; Corp. Code §§ 200(c) (existence begins on filing), 207 (powers of a natural person).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. Open every "is the owner personally liable?" call with this rule, then work through the exceptions.

**Example:** Toy Shop Co. owes a supplier $55,000. Its two shareholders are not personally liable unless the supplier can pierce the veil or another exception applies (Jul 2026 Q4 pattern).

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: the same as 有限责任 under the PRC Company Law, and US veil piercing works like the PRC's 法人人格否认 doctrine for shareholders who abuse the corporate form (approx.).

### R-BUS-047 — Piercing the corporate veil (alter ego)
**Rule (write this):** Courts disregard the corporate entity and hold shareholders personally liable when (1) there is such a unity of interest and ownership that the separate personalities of the corporation and the shareholder no longer exist, and (2) adherence to the fiction of separate existence would sanction a fraud or promote injustice. Courts pierce reluctantly, case by case, and generally only against shareholders who actively participated in the abuse.

**Elements:** 1. **Unity of interest** (domination, commingling, undercapitalization, disregard of formalities, R-BUS-048). 2. **Inequitable result** if the entity is respected (fraud, evasion of an existing obligation, injustice beyond the creditor's simple non-payment).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Piercing is more readily allowed for **tort** victims (involuntary creditors) than for **contract** creditors, who could have investigated or demanded a guarantee. Inability to collect alone is not an inequitable result. Passive minority shareholders are not liable. Shareholders of a public corporation are virtually never pierced.

**Authority:** *Associated Vendors, Inc. v. Oakland Meat Co.* (1962) 210 Cal.App.2d 825; *Mesler v. Bragg Management Co.* (1985) 39 Cal.3d 290; *Sonora Diamond Corp. v. Superior Court* (2000) 83 Cal.App.4th 523; *Walkovszky v. Carlton*, 18 N.Y.2d 414 (1966).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same two-prong test; California calls it the **alter ego** doctrine. Cite *Associated Vendors* for the factor list.

**Example:** Anne and Beth form Toy Shop Co. with $1,000, pay personal bills from its account, never hold meetings and keep taking cash while unpaid debts mount. Unity of interest is strong, and letting them keep the cash while the supplier goes unpaid is the injustice: piercing is likely.

### R-BUS-048 — Alter-ego factors: undercapitalization, commingling, domination, formalities
**Rule (write this):** Factors showing unity of interest include inadequate capitalization in light of foreseeable risks; commingling of funds and assets; use of corporate assets for personal purposes; treating corporate debts as one's own or diverting assets to shareholders to the creditors' detriment; failure to observe corporate formalities and keep records; identical ownership, officers and offices among related entities; use of the corporation as a mere shell or instrumentality; and misrepresentation about who is responsible for debts. No single factor is decisive.

**Elements:** Weigh all factors; undercapitalization and commingling plus a fraud or injustice are the strongest combination.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Undercapitalization alone is usually insufficient, but in California it is a weighty factor (*Automotriz del Golfo de California S.A. de C.V. v. Resnick* (1957) 47 Cal.2d 792; *Minton v. Cavaney* (1961) 56 Cal.2d 576). In a California **statutory close corporation**, failure to observe formalities under a shareholders' agreement is not a factor (Corp. Code § 300(e)); for an LLC, failure to hold meetings is not a factor unless the governing documents require meetings (Corp. Code § 17703.04) [S-805].

**Authority:** *Associated Vendors, Inc. v. Oakland Meat Co.* (1962) 210 Cal.App.2d 825; *Automotriz* (1957) 47 Cal.2d 792; *Minton v. Cavaney* (1961) 56 Cal.2d 576; *Kinney Shoe Corp. v. Polan*, 939 F.2d 209 (4th Cir. 1991).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same factors. California adds the statutory "formalities are not a factor" rules for close corporations and LLCs.

**Example:** Art capitalizes Retail, Inc. with $5,000 to run a store needing $200,000 of inventory, lends it $50,000 instead of investing equity, and uses its card for family vacations. Supplier argues undercapitalization and commingling (Feb 2017 Q4 pattern).

### R-BUS-049 — Enterprise liability, parent-subsidiary and reverse piercing
**Rule (write this):** A parent corporation is not liable for its subsidiary's debts merely because of ownership and shared officers; the plaintiff must show the alter-ego elements, such as the parent's pervasive control making the subsidiary a mere instrumentality, plus injustice. Under the single-enterprise theory, commonly owned sister corporations operated as one business may be treated as one. Reverse piercing reaches the entity's assets to pay an owner's personal debt.

**Elements:** 1. Domination beyond normal parent oversight (commingled operations, no separate books, sub has no independent business). 2. Injustice.

**Exceptions / defenses:** California courts rejected outside reverse piercing for corporations (*Postal Instant Press, Inc. v. Kaswa Corp.* (2008) 162 Cal.App.4th 1510) but allowed it for an LLC (*Curci Investments, LLC v. Baldwin* (2017) 14 Cal.App.5th 214) [VERIFY V-724].

**Authority:** *Sonora Diamond Corp. v. Superior Court* (2000) 83 Cal.App.4th 523; *Sea-Land Services, Inc. v. Pepper Source*, 941 F.2d 519 (7th Cir. 1991).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule; reverse piercing is a California nuance.

**Example:** A franchisor's subsidiary has its own board, books, bank account and business. Shared directors alone do not make the parent liable for the subsidiary's lease.

### R-BUS-050 — Personal tort liability of directors, officers and shareholders
**Rule (write this):** A director, officer or shareholder who personally commits, participates in, authorizes or directs a tort is personally liable to the injured party, even though acting for the corporation. Corporate status shields a person from the corporation's liabilities, not from the person's own wrongdoing.

**Elements:** 1. Personal participation (commission, direction, authorization, or knowing failure to act where there was a duty to act). 2. Tort elements.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A director who did not participate, and merely held the office, is not liable. Contract liability of the corporation does not become personal this way.

**Authority:** *Frances T. v. Village Green Owners Assn.* (1986) 42 Cal.3d 490; R3d Agency § 7.01 [VERIFY V-701].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** Andy, a shareholder-officer of a newsletter corporation, writes and publishes a defamatory story about a ballplayer. Andy is personally liable for libel regardless of whether the veil is pierced (Feb 2015 Q5 pattern) [VERIFY V-071].

### R-BUS-051 — Formation: de jure incorporation
**Rule (write this):** A corporation is formed by one or more incorporators executing and filing articles of incorporation with the Secretary of State; corporate existence begins on filing. In California the articles must state the corporation's name, a statement of purpose (the statutory general-purpose clause is enough), the agent for service of process, and the number of shares it is authorized to issue; the MBCA requires no purpose clause.

**Elements:** 1. Articles with mandatory contents. 2. Filing. 3. Organizational steps (bylaws, directors, issuing shares) follow but do not condition existence.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Failure to complete post-filing steps (no bylaws, no meetings) does not defeat existence but feeds veil-piercing arguments.

**Authority:** Corp. Code §§ 200, 202; MBCA §§ 2.01–2.03, 2.06.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. MBCA § 2.03(b): the filed articles are conclusive proof of incorporation except in a proceeding by the state.

**Example:** Ann signs a lease on Feb 1 and files articles on Feb 15. On Feb 1 the corporation did not exist, so the lease raises promoter liability (R-BUS-054).

### R-BUS-052 — Defective incorporation: de facto corporation
**Rule (write this):** At common law, a business that tried but failed to incorporate is treated as a de facto corporation, shielding its owners from personal liability, if (1) a statute existed under which it could have incorporated, (2) the owners made a good-faith, colorable attempt to comply with it, and (3) the business actually used or exercised corporate powers. Only the state can challenge a de facto corporation's existence.

**Elements:** 1. Incorporation statute. 2. Good-faith attempt (e.g., articles sent for filing but rejected or lost). 3. Exercise of corporate privileges (operating as a corporation).

**Exceptions / defenses:** The MBCA rejects the doctrine for those who knew there was no incorporation: persons purporting to act as a corporation **knowing** there was no incorporation are jointly and severally liable (MBCA § 2.04). Many courts also refuse protection where the owners made no real attempt. California's current treatment is not confirmed [VERIFY V-713].

**Authority:** MBCA § 2.04; California three-element formulation reflected in State Board of Equalization decision, Appeal of [taxpayer] (1966) [S-823] [VERIFY V-713].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** state the common-law test and the MBCA § 2.04 rule; argue both sides.

**Example:** The owners' lawyer mailed signed articles that were rejected for a name conflict, and the owners, unaware, ran the business as "Paint Co., Inc." They argue de facto status; the landlord argues they never attempted to correct the filing.

### R-BUS-053 — Corporation by estoppel
**Rule (write this):** A third party who dealt with a business as a corporation, and relied on its corporate status rather than on the owners' personal credit, may be estopped from denying its corporate existence to hold the owners personally liable. Conversely, a person who held a business out as a corporation is estopped from denying corporate status to escape the business's obligations.

**Elements:** 1. The third party treated the business as a corporation (contracted in the corporate name, looked to corporate credit). 2. It would be inequitable to allow a windfall of personal liability. 3. Generally limited to contract claims.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Does not apply to tort victims, who did not choose to deal with a corporation. Under MBCA § 2.04, owners who knew there was no corporation remain liable; estoppel is weaker in MBCA states.

**Authority:** Common law; MBCA § 2.04 [VERIFY V-713].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** state the doctrine and its limits.

**Example:** A computer seller sold on credit to "Batting Average, Inc." and never asked for a personal guarantee. If the corporation was not validly formed, the owners argue the seller is estopped from suing them personally; the seller replies the owners knew no articles were filed (Feb 2015 Q5 pattern) [VERIFY V-071].

### R-BUS-054 — Promoter liability on pre-incorporation contracts
**Rule (write this):** A promoter who makes a contract on behalf of a corporation not yet formed is personally liable on it, and remains liable even after the corporation is formed and adopts the contract, unless the contract shows the parties intended otherwise (e.g., the other party agreed to look only to the future corporation) or there is a novation.

**Elements:** 1. Promoter acted for a not-yet-formed corporation. 2. Knowledge of non-existence by both parties does not by itself release the promoter. 3. Release only by: express agreement that the promoter is not bound, or novation substituting the corporation.

**Exceptions / defenses:** If the contract is really a revocable offer to the future corporation, the promoter is not bound. Adoption alone does not release the promoter; the promoter may seek indemnity from the corporation after adoption.

**Authority:** R3d Agency § 6.04 [VERIFY V-714]; MBCA § 2.04; common law.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** Arnold signs a five-year lease "on behalf of Paint Co., a corporation to be formed." Paint Co. later forms and votes to assume the lease, but the landlord never releases Arnold. Arnold remains personally liable alongside the corporation (Feb 2022 Q5 pattern).

### R-BUS-055 — Corporation's liability: adoption, ratification and novation
**Rule (write this):** A corporation is not bound by a promoter's pre-incorporation contract until it adopts the contract, expressly (board resolution) or impliedly (knowingly accepting its benefits). Adoption is effective from the time of adoption, not relation back, because the corporation did not exist when the contract was made. A novation — an agreement by all three parties substituting the corporation for the promoter — releases the promoter.

**Elements:** Adoption: 1. corporation formed; 2. knowledge of the contract's material terms; 3. express or implied acceptance. Novation: 1. corporation, promoter and third party all agree; 2. promoter released.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Some courts loosely call adoption "ratification"; on the essay note that true ratification relates back, which is impossible for a nonexistent principal.

**Authority:** *McArthur v. Times Printing Co.*, 48 Minn. 319, 51 N.W. 216 (1892) [S-1805] (adoption effective from the date of adoption [VERIFY V-714]); R3d Agency § 4.04 [VERIFY V-701]; R-CTR-088 (novation).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** After incorporation, Paint Co. moves into the leased premises and pays rent for six months. It impliedly adopted the lease and is liable; the promoter is still liable absent novation.

### R-BUS-056 — Promoters' fiduciary duties
**Rule (write this):** Promoters owe fiduciary duties to the corporation they are forming and to each other, including a duty not to make secret profits on transactions with the corporation. A promoter who sells property to the corporation must disclose the profit to an independent board or to all subscribing shareholders.

**Elements:** 1. Promoter status. 2. Undisclosed profit or self-dealing. 3. Remedy: rescission or disgorgement of the secret profit.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Full disclosure to, and approval by, an independent board or all shareholders (including contemplated subscribers) cures.

**Authority:** *Old Dominion Copper Mining & Smelting Co. v. Lewisohn*, 210 U.S. 206 (1908) (disclosure to existing shareholders where all shares were held by the promoters); common law.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** Before incorporating, Bea buys a warehouse for $300,000 and, as promoter, sells it to the new corporation for $500,000 without telling the investors. She must disgorge the $200,000 profit.

## V. Corporations — powers, directors and officers

### R-BUS-057 — Corporate powers and the ultra vires doctrine
**Rule (write this):** A corporation may engage in any lawful business unless its articles limit its purposes, and it has the powers of a natural person to carry out its business. An act beyond a purpose limitation in the articles (ultra vires) is not void and cannot be raised as a defense between the corporation and a third party; it may be challenged only by a shareholder suit to enjoin it, by the corporation (or shareholders derivatively) against the directors or officers who caused it, or by the state.

**Elements:** 1. Does the articles' purpose clause limit the business? 2. Is the act outside it? 3. Who is challenging, and how?

**Exceptions / defenses:** Under the MBCA, a court may enjoin or set aside the act only if equitable and all affected persons are parties, and may award the third party damages for loss other than anticipated profits (MBCA § 3.04) [VERIFY V-728]. California's § 208(a) allows a shareholder injunction against unauthorized business only where third parties have not acquired rights [VERIFY V-935]. Directors who cause ultra vires acts may be liable for resulting losses.

**Authority:** Corp. Code §§ 206, 207, 208; MBCA §§ 3.01, 3.02, 3.04.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** Corp's articles limit it to "manufacturing televisions." The board's plan to make only bicycles is ultra vires; a shareholder may sue to enjoin it before the third-party contracts are performed (Jul 2022 Q4 pattern) [S-814].

### R-BUS-058 — Corporate charitable donations
**Rule (write this):** A corporation may make reasonable charitable donations for the public welfare, without proof of direct corporate benefit. A donation that is unreasonable in amount, unrelated to any corporate purpose, or made to serve a director's personal interest may be challenged as waste or a breach of the duty of loyalty.

**Elements:** 1. Statutory power to donate. 2. Reasonableness (amount relative to income, tax deductibility, connection to goodwill). 3. No personal benefit to the deciding director.

**Exceptions / defenses:** The business judgment rule protects a reasonable, disinterested donation decision.

**Authority:** Corp. Code § 207(e); DGCL § 122(9); MBCA § 3.02(13); *A.P. Smith Mfg. Co. v. Barlow*, 98 A.2d 581 (N.J. 1953); *Theodora Holding Corp. v. Henderson*, 257 A.2d 398 (Del. Ch. 1969).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** The board donates $100,000 to the university where a director's daughter is seeking a job. The power exists, but the director's interest raises a loyalty challenge (Oct 2020 Q2 pattern) [VERIFY V-071].

### R-BUS-059 — Board management and valid board action
**Rule (write this):** The business and affairs of a corporation are managed by or under the direction of its board of directors. The board acts only collectively: at a properly noticed meeting with a quorum (by default a majority of the authorized number of directors), by a majority vote of directors present, or by unanimous written consent without a meeting. Individual directors are not agents of the corporation merely because they are directors.

**Elements:** 1. Notice for special meetings (as bylaws require). 2. Quorum. 3. Majority of those present. 4. Or unanimous written consent. 5. Meetings may be held by conference telephone or electronic means if all can communicate.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Defective action may be ratified by a later valid board vote. A director's attendance waives notice unless the director attends to object. Committees may exercise delegated board powers except specified fundamental ones.

**Authority:** Corp. Code §§ 300(a), 307, 311; MBCA §§ 8.01, 8.20–8.25; DGCL § 141.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** Two of three directors meet at lunch without notice to the third and "approve" a $1 million contract. The action is invalid unless all three sign a written consent or the board later ratifies.

### R-BUS-060 — Officers' authority to bind the corporation
**Rule (write this):** Officers are agents of the corporation, and their power to bind it is governed by agency law: actual authority from the bylaws or board resolutions (express or implied from the office), apparent authority from the corporation's manifestations to third parties, and ratification. A president or CEO has implied authority over ordinary business matters but not extraordinary ones.

**Elements:** Actual authority (bylaws, resolution, custom of the office); apparent authority (title, course of dealing); ratification by the board.

**Exceptions / defenses:** In California, an instrument signed by the chair or president or a vice president **and** by the secretary, assistant secretary, CFO or assistant treasurer is not invalid for lack of authority as to a person without actual knowledge of the lack (Corp. Code § 313) [VERIFY V-728].

**Authority:** Corp. Code §§ 312–313; MBCA §§ 8.40–8.41; R3d Agency §§ 2.01–2.03 [VERIFY V-701].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule; § 313 is a California add-on.

**Example:** The CEO signs a contract to sell the company's only factory without board approval. It is extraordinary, so no implied authority; the buyer's claim rests on apparent authority or ratification.

### R-BUS-061 — Directors' duty of care
**Rule (write this):** A director must perform director duties in good faith, in a manner the director believes to be in the best interests of the corporation and its shareholders, and with such care, including reasonable inquiry, as an ordinarily prudent person in a like position would use under similar circumstances. A director may rely in good faith on information, reports and opinions of officers, employees, counsel, experts and board committees the director reasonably believes reliable and competent.

**Elements:** 1. Good faith. 2. Reasonable belief in the corporation's best interests. 3. Informed decision-making (reasonable inquiry; adequate information). 4. Causation and damages for liability (in most jurisdictions).

**Exceptions / defenses:** The business judgment rule (R-BUS-062); good-faith reliance on reports (unless the director has knowledge making reliance unwarranted); exculpation clauses (R-BUS-070).

**Authority:** Corp. Code § 309(a)–(b); MBCA §§ 8.30, 8.31; *Smith v. Van Gorkom*, 488 A.2d 858 (Del. 1985) (gross negligence in failing to inform); *Francis v. United Jersey Bank*, 432 A.2d 814 (N.J. 1981) (inattentive director liable).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same standard; California codifies it in § 309, which also protects a director who meets it (§ 309(c)). Write the general rule, then "California codifies this standard in § 309."

**Example:** Directors approve a $20 million dividend after a 15-minute meeting without looking at cash-flow projections. Shareholders argue lack of reasonable inquiry; the directors argue reliance on the CFO's oral report (Oct 2020 Q2 pattern).

### R-BUS-062 — Business judgment rule
**Rule (write this):** Under the business judgment rule, courts presume that in making a business decision, disinterested and independent directors acted on an informed basis, in good faith, and in the honest belief that the action was in the best interests of the corporation. The court will not second-guess the decision unless the plaintiff rebuts the presumption by showing fraud, illegality, a conflict of interest, bad faith, or a failure to become reasonably informed (gross negligence); a decision that cannot be attributed to any rational business purpose (waste) is also unprotected.

**Elements:** To claim the BJR: 1. a business decision (not inaction without deliberation); 2. disinterest and independence; 3. informed basis; 4. good faith; 5. rational business purpose.

**Exceptions / defenses:** If the presumption is rebutted by a conflict, the burden shifts to the defendants to prove entire fairness. A bad outcome alone never rebuts it.

**Authority:** *Aronson v. Lewis*, 473 A.2d 805 (Del. 1984); *Shlensky v. Wrigley*, 237 N.E.2d 776 (Ill. App. 1968); *In re Walt Disney Co. Derivative Litigation*, 906 A.2d 27 (Del. 2006); Corp. Code § 309; *Lee v. Interinsurance Exchange* (1996) 50 Cal.App.4th 694 (California has both the statutory and a common-law BJR); *Gaillard v. Natomas Co.* (1989) 208 Cal.App.3d 1250.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. California courts apply a common-law BJR alongside § 309 (*Lee*; *Oakland Raiders v. National Football League* (2001) 93 Cal.App.4th 572).

**Example:** A board refuses to install stadium lights because it believes night games would harm the neighborhood and long-term attendance. Even if profits suffer, the BJR protects the reasoned decision (*Shlensky*).

### R-BUS-063 — Duty of oversight (Caremark)
**Rule (write this):** Directors must make a good-faith effort to implement and monitor a reasonable system of internal reporting and controls. Directors are liable for failure of oversight only if they utterly failed to implement any reporting or information system or controls, or, having implemented one, consciously failed to monitor or oversee its operation, disabling themselves from learning of risks or problems requiring their attention (for example, ignoring red flags). Because the failure must be in bad faith, the claim sounds in loyalty.

**Elements:** 1. No system at all, especially for mission-critical risks, **or** 2. conscious disregard of red flags. 3. Scienter (knowledge that they were not discharging their duties).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Good-faith but ineffective systems are not enough. Exculpation clauses cannot cover bad-faith oversight failures.

**Authority:** *In re Caremark International Inc. Derivative Litigation*, 698 A.2d 959 (Del. Ch. 1996); *Stone v. Ritter*, 911 A.2d 362 (Del. 2006); *Marchand v. Barnhill*, 212 A.3d 805 (Del. 2019); Corp. Code § 204(a)(10)(iv)–(v); *Kanter v. Reed* (2023) (Cal. Ct. App.) [S-817] [VERIFY V-721].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same idea. In California, conduct showing reckless disregard or an unexcused pattern of inattention amounting to abdication cannot be exculpated (§ 204(a)(10)) [S-817].

**Example:** An ice-cream maker's board never discussed food safety, its most critical risk, and ignored inspection reports of listeria. A *Marchand* oversight claim survives.

### R-BUS-064 — Duty of loyalty and good faith: overview
**Rule (write this):** A director or officer must act in the good-faith belief that the action serves the corporation's best interests and must not use the position to obtain a personal benefit at the corporation's expense. Loyalty issues arise in self-dealing (interested-director) transactions, usurpation of corporate opportunities, competition with the corporation, misuse of corporate information, and excessive compensation. When a fiduciary has a conflict, the BJR does not apply and the fiduciary must show the transaction was fair unless a statutory safe harbor applies.

**Elements:** 1. Conflict or personal benefit (material financial interest; lack of independence from an interested party). 2. Safe harbor (disclosure and disinterested approval) or fairness (fair dealing and fair price).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Disinterested-director or shareholder approval after full disclosure; proof of fairness. Exculpation never covers loyalty breaches.

**Authority:** *Guth v. Loft, Inc.*, 5 A.2d 503 (Del. 1939); *Weinberger v. UOP, Inc.*, 457 A.2d 701 (Del. 1983); *Stone v. Ritter*, 911 A.2d 362 (Del. 2006) (good faith is part of loyalty); Corp. Code §§ 309, 310; *Bancroft-Whitney Co. v. Glen* (1966) 64 Cal.2d 327.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** Director Cate votes to have the corporation lease office space from a building she secretly owns. That is self-dealing; analyze safe harbors next (R-BUS-065).

### R-BUS-065 — Interested-director transactions: California § 310
**Rule (write this):** In California, a contract between a corporation and a director (or an entity in which the director has a material financial interest) is not void or voidable because of the interest if: (1) the material facts of the transaction and the director's interest are disclosed to the shareholders and the transaction is approved by the shareholders in good faith, with the interested director's shares not counted; or (2) the material facts are fully disclosed to or known by the board, and the board authorizes it in good faith by a vote sufficient without counting the interested director, and the transaction is just and reasonable to the corporation when approved; or (3) where not so approved, the person asserting validity proves the transaction was just and reasonable to the corporation when authorized.

**Elements:** 1. Material financial interest (a mere common directorship is not one). 2. Route (1): disclosure + disinterested shareholder approval (no fairness requirement). 3. Route (2): disclosure + disinterested board approval + just and reasonable (the challenger must prove it was not). 4. Route (3): proponent proves just and reasonable.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Interested (or common) directors may be counted toward the quorum of the approving board or committee, but their votes do not count toward the approval (§ 310(a)(2), (c)) [S-1806]. Common-directorship transactions (§ 310(b)) have their own rule. Burden: once disinterested board approval after full disclosure is shown (route (2)), the person attacking the transaction must prove it was not just and reasonable; without qualifying approval (route (3)), the person asserting validity must prove it was (*Harvey v. The Landing Homeowners Assn.* (2008) 162 Cal.App.4th 809, applying the parallel nonprofit provision, § 7233) [S-1793] [S-1794].

**Authority:** Corp. Code § 310(a)–(b) [S-803]; burden allocation [S-1793] [S-1794].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** **differs in detail.** General law (MBCA §§ 8.60–8.63; old DGCL § 144): disclosure plus approval by disinterested directors **or** shareholders, **or** fairness; approval generally restores BJR review. California: board approval still requires that the transaction be just and reasonable. Write both.

**Example:** The board, minus Cate, approves her building lease after she discloses ownership; the rent is 10% above market. Under § 310(a)(2), the approval fails if the lease is not just and reasonable; shareholder approval under (a)(1) would have avoided the fairness question.

### R-BUS-066 — Interested transactions: MBCA, Delaware and the 2025 DGCL § 144 amendments
**Rule (write this):** Under the MBCA and Delaware's traditional § 144, a director's conflicting-interest transaction is protected from challenge on the ground of the interest if, after full disclosure, it is approved by a majority of qualified (disinterested) directors or by a majority of disinterested shareholders' votes, or if it is fair to the corporation. Delaware's 2025 amendments to DGCL § 144 created statutory safe harbors for conflicted and controlling-stockholder transactions: approval by a committee of disinterested directors or by a majority of votes cast by disinterested stockholders, and both for a controller's going-private transaction.

**Elements:** MBCA: 1. director's conflicting interest; 2. required disclosure; 3. qualified directors' or qualified shares' approval, or fairness. Delaware (2025): defined "controlling stockholder"; procedural safe harbors with independence and disclosure conditions.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Approval does not protect waste or fraud. The Delaware Supreme Court upheld the 2025 amendments against constitutional challenge in *Rutledge v. Clearway Energy Group* (Del. 2026) [S-812] [VERIFY V-716].

**Authority:** MBCA §§ 8.60–8.63; DGCL § 144 (as amended 2025) [S-811] [VERIFY V-716]; *Benihana of Tokyo, Inc. v. Benihana, Inc.*, 906 A.2d 114 (Del. 2006).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** use the MBCA/general rule on the essay; add California § 310 as above. Mention the 2025 Delaware change only if a Delaware corporation or a controller is involved.

**Example:** A Delaware company buys assets from its 60% stockholder. If a committee of independent directors approves after full disclosure, the 2025 § 144 safe harbor applies; otherwise entire fairness review.

### R-BUS-067 — Corporate opportunity doctrine
**Rule (write this):** A director or officer may not usurp a business opportunity that belongs to the corporation. An opportunity belongs to the corporation if it is within the corporation's line of business, the corporation has an interest or expectancy in it, and the corporation is financially able to exploit it (*Guth* factors), or, under the ALI approach (§ 5.05), if it came to the fiduciary in a corporate capacity or through corporate information, or (for senior executives) is closely related to the corporation's business; MBCA § 8.70 adds a disclosure-and-disclaimer safe harbor. The fiduciary must first offer it to the corporation with full disclosure; only if the corporation (by disinterested decision) rejects it may the fiduciary take it.

**Elements:** 1. Corporate opportunity (line of business; interest or expectancy; how it was learned; use of corporate resources). 2. Failure to disclose and offer. 3. Taken personally.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Corporation's financial inability is a defense in some courts (rejected or narrowed in others, e.g., where the fiduciary did not try to help the company raise funds); informed rejection by disinterested directors; the opportunity was personal (not related to the business).

**Authority:** *Guth v. Loft, Inc.*, 5 A.2d 503 (Del. 1939); *Broz v. Cellular Information Systems, Inc.*, 673 A.2d 148 (Del. 1996); ALI Principles of Corporate Governance § 5.05; MBCA § 8.70; *Kelegian v. Mgrdichian* (1995) 33 Cal.App.4th 982 (existence is primarily a question of fact; no usurpation where the corporation had no established policy of repurchasing its shares) [S-821] [S-1157].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule; California treats existence of an opportunity as primarily a factual question and uses similar factors.

**Example:** The president of a bicycle maker learns, through a corporate supplier, that a rival bike brand is for sale and buys it himself. Line of business and how he learned point to a corporate opportunity he had to offer first.

### R-BUS-068 — Competition, executive compensation and waste
**Rule (write this):** A director or officer may not compete with the corporation while serving, or use corporate property, personnel or confidential information for personal gain. Compensation set by disinterested directors is protected by the BJR, but compensation approved by interested directors must be fair, and any payment so one-sided that no business person of ordinary judgment would conclude the corporation received adequate consideration is waste.

**Elements:** Competition: 1. fiduciary status; 2. competing business; 3. harm or diversion. Waste: 1. transfer of corporate assets; 2. no rational business purpose / essentially a gift.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Disclosure and disinterested approval; preparation to compete after leaving (without soliciting employees or taking confidential information while still employed).

**Authority:** *Bancroft-Whitney Co. v. Glen* (1966) 64 Cal.2d 327 (officer secretly recruiting employees for competitor); *Brehm v. Eisner*, 746 A.2d 244 (Del. 2000) (waste standard); *In re Walt Disney Co. Derivative Litigation*, 906 A.2d 27 (Del. 2006).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** While still president, Glen recruits the company's key editors to a competitor using confidential salary data. He breached the duty of loyalty.

### R-BUS-069 — Loans to directors and officers
**Rule (write this):** In California, a corporation may not make a loan to, or guarantee an obligation of, a director or officer unless the transaction is approved by the shareholders (not counting the shares of the benefited director), except that, for loans to or guaranties for **officers**, a corporation with 100 or more shareholders of record may, under a bylaw approved by the outstanding shares, let the board approve on a finding that the loan may reasonably be expected to benefit the corporation. Public companies are also barred by federal law from making most personal loans to directors and executive officers.

**Elements:** 1. Loan or guarantee to a director or officer. 2. Required approval. 3. Directors who approve a prohibited loan face liability.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Employee benefit plans and advances for expenses have special treatment. Federal ban: Sarbanes-Oxley § 402 (Exchange Act § 13(k), 15 U.S.C. § 78m(k)).

**Authority:** Corp. Code §§ 315, 316(a)(3) [VERIFY V-712]; 15 U.S.C. § 78m(k).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** **differs.** The MBCA treats such loans as ordinary conflicting-interest transactions; California has a specific shareholder-approval rule.

**Example:** A closely held California corporation's board lends the CEO $200,000 without shareholder approval. The loan violates § 315, and approving directors may be liable.

### R-BUS-070 — Exculpation and indemnification
**Rule (write this):** Articles may eliminate or limit directors' personal liability for monetary damages in suits by or on behalf of the corporation for breach of the duty of care, but not for intentional misconduct or knowing violation of law, bad faith or conduct the director believes contrary to the corporation's interests, improper personal benefit, reckless disregard of a known risk of serious injury, an unexcused pattern of inattention amounting to abdication, interested-director transactions, or unlawful distributions. A corporation must indemnify a director or officer who is successful on the merits, may indemnify one who acted in good faith and in the reasonable belief the conduct was in the corporation's best interests, and may not indemnify amounts paid to the corporation in a derivative suit without court approval.

**Elements:** Exculpation: 1. provision in the articles; 2. claim is a damages claim for breach of care by the corporation or derivatively; 3. no excluded category. Indemnification: mandatory (success), permissive (standard of conduct determined by disinterested directors, shareholders, counsel or court), prohibited.

**Exceptions / defenses:** California's exculpation covers directors only, not officers acting as officers. Delaware's § 102(b)(7) now permits officer exculpation for direct (not derivative) claims after 2022.

**Authority:** Corp. Code §§ 204(a)(10)–(11), 317 [S-817]; DGCL §§ 102(b)(7), 145; MBCA §§ 2.02(b)(4), 8.50–8.59.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same structure; California's carve-outs are broader than Delaware's (reckless disregard, pattern of inattention).

**Example:** PickWinners' articles exculpate directors for care breaches. Shareholders suing derivatively for an uninformed decision face that clause unless they show bad faith or reckless disregard (Jul 2024 Q1 pattern) [VERIFY V-071].

### R-BUS-071 — Controlling shareholders' fiduciary duties
**Rule (write this):** A controlling shareholder (majority owner, or one who exercises actual control) owes fiduciary duties to the corporation and the minority. In California, majority shareholders may not use their power to control corporate activities to benefit themselves alone or in a manner detrimental to the minority; any use of control must benefit all shareholders proportionately and must not conflict with the proper conduct of the corporation's business. Where a controller stands on both sides of a transaction or receives a non-pro-rata benefit, the controller must prove entire fairness.

**Elements:** 1. Control. 2. Self-dealing or non-pro-rata benefit (exclusion of the minority from a benefit). 3. Fairness: fair dealing (process, timing, disclosure) and fair price.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A pro-rata decision (e.g., a dividend paid to all) is reviewed under the BJR (*Sinclair Oil Corp. v. Levien*, 280 A.2d 717 (Del. 1971)). In Delaware, a controller transaction conditioned from the outset on both independent-committee approval and an informed, uncoerced majority-of-the-minority vote gets BJR review (*Kahn v. M&F Worldwide Corp.*, 88 A.3d 635 (Del. 2014)), now partly superseded by the 2025 § 144 safe harbors (R-BUS-066).

**Authority:** *Jones v. H.F. Ahmanson & Co.* (1969) 1 Cal.3d 93; *Sinclair Oil Corp. v. Levien*, 280 A.2d 717 (Del. 1971); *Weinberger v. UOP, Inc.*, 457 A.2d 701 (Del. 1983).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same core; *Jones v. Ahmanson* is California's leading case and is broader (it protects the minority even in transactions that do not involve the corporation directly).

**Example:** Majority shareholders of a savings-and-loan create a holding company, exchange their shares for its marketable shares, and leave the minority with illiquid stock. That breaches the duty to the minority (*Jones v. Ahmanson*).

### R-BUS-072 — Sale of control and freeze-out mergers
**Rule (write this):** A controlling shareholder may generally sell control at a premium, but is liable if it sells to a buyer it knows or should suspect is a looter, or if the premium represents a corporate asset or opportunity (e.g., a business advantage belonging to all shareholders). In a freeze-out (cash-out) merger, the controller must show entire fairness — fair dealing and fair price — unless the deal used adequate procedural protections.

**Elements:** Sale of control: 1. control premium; 2. looting red flags or diversion of corporate opportunity. Freeze-out: 1. controller on both sides; 2. fairness or safe-harbor procedure; 3. minority's remedy is usually appraisal or damages.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Appraisal may be the exclusive remedy absent fraud or unfair dealing.

**Authority:** *Perlman v. Feldmann*, 219 F.2d 173 (2d Cir. 1955); *Weinberger v. UOP, Inc.*, 457 A.2d 701 (Del. 1983); *Jones v. H.F. Ahmanson & Co.* (1969) 1 Cal.3d 93.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** In a steel shortage, a controller sells its block at a large premium to buyers who want to divert the company's steel to themselves. The premium reflects a corporate asset and must be shared (*Perlman*).

## V. Corporations — shareholders

### R-BUS-073 — Shareholder meetings, quorum and voting
**Rule (write this):** Shareholders act at an annual meeting (to elect directors) or a special meeting called by the board, the chair, the president or holders of at least 10% of the votes, on written notice given 10 to 60 days before the meeting stating, for a special meeting, its purpose. Unless the articles provide otherwise, a majority of the shares entitled to vote is a quorum, each share has one vote, and a matter passes by a majority of the shares represented and voting (with that majority also at least a majority of the required quorum); directors are elected by plurality. Shareholders may act without a meeting by written consent of the holders of the votes needed at a meeting.

**Elements:** 1. Proper call. 2. Proper notice (or waiver, including by attendance without objection). 3. Quorum. 4. Required vote: "approved by the shareholders" (majority of votes cast at a meeting with quorum, Corp. Code § 153) vs. "approved by the outstanding shares" (majority of all outstanding voting shares, § 152) for fundamental changes.

**Exceptions / defenses:** California: directors may be elected by written consent only if unanimous (except filling a vacancy). MBCA: written consent must be unanimous unless the articles allow less; Delaware: a majority written consent is allowed unless the certificate forbids.

**Authority:** Corp. Code §§ 152, 153, 600–603, 700; MBCA §§ 7.01–7.05, 7.25, 7.28; DGCL §§ 211, 216, 228.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same structure; note the § 152 vs. § 153 distinction when a fundamental change is voted.

**Example:** Holders of 12% call a special meeting to remove a director, giving 15 days' written notice stating that purpose. The call and notice are proper.

### R-BUS-074 — Cumulative voting
**Rule (write this):** In California, every shareholder entitled to vote in a director election may cumulate votes — casting a number of votes equal to the shares held multiplied by the number of directors to be elected, all for one candidate or spread among several — if the candidate's name was placed in nomination before voting and a shareholder gave notice at the meeting, before voting, of the intention to cumulate. A listed corporation may eliminate cumulative voting by amending its articles or bylaws.

**Elements:** 1. Mandatory default in California (opt-out only for listed corporations). 2. Nomination before voting. 3. Notice at the meeting. 4. Shares needed to elect one director = (shares voting × 1) / (directors to be elected + 1), plus one share.

**Exceptions / defenses:** MBCA and Delaware: no cumulative voting unless the articles/certificate provide it (opt-in). Pseudo-foreign corporations may be subject to California's rule (R-BUS-098).

**Authority:** Corp. Code §§ 301.5, 708; MBCA § 7.28; DGCL § 214.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** **differs.** General law: opt-in. California: mandatory for non-listed corporations. Write both.

**Example:** With 1,000 shares voting for three seats, a holder of 251 shares can guarantee one seat: 1,000/(3+1) = 250, plus 1.

### R-BUS-075 — Removal of directors and vacancies
**Rule (write this):** Shareholders may remove directors with or without cause by a majority of the outstanding shares entitled to vote, but where cumulative voting applies a director may not be removed (unless the whole board is removed) if the votes cast against removal would have been enough to elect the director under cumulative voting. A court may remove a director for fraudulent or dishonest acts or gross abuse of authority in a suit by holders of at least 10% of the shares. Vacancies may be filled by the board or the shareholders.

**Elements:** 1. Who may remove (shareholders; court). 2. Cause required? 3. Cumulative-voting protection.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Delaware: directors on a classified board may be removed only for cause unless the certificate provides otherwise. The board itself cannot remove a director (though California lets the board declare vacant the office of a director judicially declared of unsound mind or convicted of a felony).

**Authority:** Corp. Code §§ 302–305; MBCA §§ 8.08–8.10; DGCL § 141(k).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule in substance.

**Example:** A 30% holder whose candidate won through cumulative voting is protected: the majority cannot remove that director without cause if the 30% vote against removal would have elected him.

### R-BUS-076 — Voting agreements, voting trusts and proxies
**Rule (write this):** Shareholders may agree in a signed writing how they will vote their shares, and such voting (pooling) agreements are generally enforceable, including by specific performance. A voting trust transfers legal title to shares to a trustee who votes them under a written trust agreement filed with the corporation, for a limited term. A proxy is a written authorization for another to vote shares; it is revocable unless it states that it is irrevocable and is coupled with an interest (e.g., given to a pledgee or a party to a voting agreement).

**Elements:** Voting agreement: written, signed, among shareholders. Voting trust: written, deposited with the corporation, trustee holds title. Proxy: writing (or electronic transmission), signed, duration (11 months by default in California and under the MBCA; 3 years in Delaware).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Agreements that bind directors' votes as directors are traditionally invalid (directors must exercise independent judgment), except in close corporations under a statutory shareholders' agreement (R-BUS-094).

**Authority:** Corp. Code §§ 705, 706 [VERIFY V-719]; MBCA §§ 7.22, 7.30–7.32; DGCL §§ 212, 218.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule in outline; California's § 706 treats close-corporation voting agreements expressly [VERIFY V-719].

**Example:** Two founders sign an agreement to vote their shares for each other as directors. A founder who votes the other way can be ordered to comply (Jul 2022 Q4 pattern) [S-814].

### R-BUS-077 — Restrictions on transfer of shares
**Rule (write this):** Reasonable restrictions on the transfer of shares (rights of first refusal, buy-sell agreements, consent requirements that are not manifestly unreasonable) are valid if authorized in the articles, bylaws or a shareholders' agreement. A restriction binds a transferee only if it is noted conspicuously on the share certificate (or in the required notice for uncertificated shares) or the transferee had actual knowledge of it.

**Elements:** 1. Authorized restriction. 2. Reasonable purpose (keeping a close corporation close, preserving tax status, regulatory compliance). 3. Notice: conspicuous legend or actual knowledge.

**Exceptions / defenses:** An outright prohibition on transfer is generally unreasonable.

**Authority:** Corp. Code §§ 204(b), 418; Cal. Com. Code § 8204; MBCA § 6.27; DGCL § 202.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A buyer purchases shares from a founder; the certificate bears no legend, and the buyer did not know of a voting agreement or right of first refusal. The buyer takes free of the restriction (Jul 2022 Q4 pattern) [S-814].

### R-BUS-078 — Shareholder inspection rights
**Rule (write this):** In California, any shareholder may, on written demand, inspect and copy the corporation's accounting books and records and minutes of shareholder, board and committee proceedings at reasonable times, for a purpose reasonably related to the holder's interests as a shareholder. Holders of at least 5% of the voting shares (or 1% who have filed a specified SEC schedule) have an absolute right to inspect the shareholder list; other shareholders may inspect it for a proper purpose. Each director has an absolute right to inspect all books, records and properties.

**Elements:** 1. Shareholder status. 2. Written demand. 3. Proper purpose (valuing shares, investigating mismanagement, communicating with other shareholders about corporate matters). 4. Records within the statute.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Improper purposes: harassment, obtaining trade secrets for a competitor, purely personal or political aims unrelated to shareholder interests. The court may limit or condition inspection and award costs if refusal was unjustified.

**Authority:** Corp. Code §§ 1600–1604; DGCL § 220 (written demand under oath; proper purpose; 2025 amendments narrowed "books and records") [S-811] [VERIFY V-716]; MBCA §§ 16.02–16.04.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same core (proper purpose); California gives directors an absolute right and gives 5% holders an absolute right to the shareholder list.

**Example:** A shareholder who suspects the board is wasting funds demands minutes of the meeting approving a $100,000 donation. Investigating mismanagement is a proper purpose (Oct 2020 Q2 pattern).

### R-BUS-079 — Dividends: board discretion
**Rule (write this):** Whether to declare dividends is within the board's business judgment; shareholders have no right to a dividend until it is declared, and courts compel a dividend only on a showing of bad faith, fraud or abuse of discretion. Once lawfully declared, a cash dividend becomes a debt the corporation owes to shareholders of record. Preferred shares carry priority as the articles provide (cumulative or noncumulative; participating or not).

**Elements:** 1. Board decision (BJR). 2. Legal limits (R-BUS-080). 3. Class rights in the articles.

**Exceptions / defenses:** In close corporations, persistent refusal to pay dividends to squeeze out a minority may be oppression or a breach of fiduciary duty (R-BUS-095).

**Authority:** *Dodge v. Ford Motor Co.*, 170 N.W. 668 (Mich. 1919); *Kamin v. American Express Co.*, 383 N.Y.S.2d 807 (Sup. Ct. 1976); Corp. Code § 500.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** Half the board wants to keep $20 million for expansion; half wants to pay it out. Either choice, made in good faith on adequate information, is protected (Oct 2020 Q2 pattern).

### R-BUS-080 — Distributions: legal limits and liability
**Rule (write this):** In California, a corporation may make a distribution to shareholders only if (1) its retained earnings immediately before the distribution equal or exceed the distribution plus any preferential-dividend arrears, or (2) immediately after the distribution the value of its assets equals or exceeds its liabilities plus the preferential rights of senior shares; and in all events it may not make a distribution if it is, or as a result would be, likely unable to meet its liabilities as they mature. Directors who approve an unlawful distribution are jointly and severally liable to the corporation for the benefit of creditors and senior shareholders; a shareholder who receives one with knowledge of facts indicating its impropriety must return it.

**Elements:** 1. "Distribution" (dividends, share repurchases, redemptions). 2. Retained-earnings **or** balance-sheet test. 3. Equity-insolvency test. 4. Liability: approving directors (defense of good-faith reliance under § 309); knowing shareholder recipients.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Directors who dissented in the minutes avoid liability. Contribution from knowing recipients.

**Authority:** Corp. Code §§ 500, 501, 316, 506 [S-800] [S-801]; MBCA § 6.40; DGCL §§ 170, 173, 174 (surplus or "nimble" dividends).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** **differs in form.** MBCA: equity-insolvency test **and** balance-sheet test. Delaware: surplus or current/prior-year net profits. California (since 2012): retained-earnings **or** balance-sheet test, **plus** insolvency limit. The old California "1¼ asset ratio" tests were repealed by AB 571 (2011) [S-801].

**Example:** A corporation with $2 million of assets, $1.9 million of liabilities and no retained earnings pays a $500,000 dividend. Both § 500 tests fail, and the approving directors are liable to the corporation for the unlawful distribution (§ 316).

### R-BUS-081 — Direct vs. derivative suits
**Rule (write this):** A suit is derivative when the injury is to the corporation and any recovery would flow to the corporation (mismanagement, waste, self-dealing, usurped opportunities); it is direct when the shareholder suffers an injury independent of the corporation and recovery goes to the shareholder (denial of voting or inspection rights, failure to pay a declared dividend, unfair treatment of the minority, a disclosure claim tied to a vote). The procedural hurdles of a derivative suit apply only to derivative claims.

**Elements:** Ask: (1) who suffered the harm — the corporation or the shareholders individually? (2) who would receive the benefit of any recovery? (*Tooley*).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Some claims are both (dilutive self-dealing by a controller). In close corporations, some courts allow minority claims to proceed directly. Class actions aggregate direct claims.

**Authority:** *Tooley v. Donaldson, Lufkin & Jenrette, Inc.*, 845 A.2d 1031 (Del. 2004); *Jones v. H.F. Ahmanson & Co.* (1969) 1 Cal.3d 93 (minority's claim against controllers was direct); ALI Principles § 7.01.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. Make it the first heading of any "on what theories can shareholders sue" answer.

**Example:** A shareholder sues to enjoin an ultra vires change of business and for damages caused by the president's self-dealing. The injunction claim (protecting her rights under the articles) is arguably direct; the damages claim for corporate harm is derivative (Jul 2022 Q4 pattern).

### R-BUS-082 — Derivative suit: standing (contemporaneous and continuous ownership)
**Rule (write this):** A derivative plaintiff must have been a shareholder at the time of the transaction complained of (or have received the shares by operation of law from such a holder), must remain a shareholder throughout the litigation, and must fairly and adequately represent the corporation's interests. California allows a court to waive contemporaneous ownership if the plaintiff shows a strong prima facie case, no similar action, acquisition of shares before disclosure of the wrongdoing, that the defendant would otherwise keep a gain from a willful breach of fiduciary duty, and that relief will not unjustly enrich the corporation or any shareholder.

**Elements:** 1. Contemporaneous ownership (or statutory exception). 2. Continuous ownership (*Grosset*). 3. Adequate representation.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Loss of shares in a merger generally ends standing; equitable exceptions are narrow.

**Authority:** Corp. Code § 800(b)(1) (strong prima facie case, no similar action, acquisition before disclosure [S-1795]; remaining findings [VERIFY V-707]); *Grosset v. Wenaas* (2008) 42 Cal.4th 1100; MBCA § 7.41; DGCL § 327; Fed. R. Civ. P. 23.1.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** **differs.** General law: strict contemporaneous ownership. California: discretionary exception under § 800(b)(1). Write both.

**Example:** Ella bought her shares a month after the board approved the challenged contract but before anyone knew it was rigged. Under general law she lacks standing; in California she can ask the court for leave under § 800(b)(1).

### R-BUS-083 — Derivative suit: demand on the board and futility
**Rule (write this):** Before suing derivatively, a shareholder must make a demand on the board to bring the suit, or plead with particularity why demand would be futile. Under Delaware's current test, demand is excused if, for at least half of the directors, a director (1) received a material personal benefit from the alleged misconduct, (2) faces a substantial likelihood of liability on the claims, or (3) lacks independence from someone who did. The MBCA requires a written demand in every case (universal demand) and a 90-day wait unless the demand is rejected earlier or irreparable injury would result.

**Elements:** California: the complaint must allege with particularity the plaintiff's efforts to secure board action or the reasons for not making the effort, and that the plaintiff informed the corporation or board in writing of the ultimate facts of each cause of action or delivered a copy of the complaint. Futility: particularized facts creating a reasonable doubt about the directors' disinterest/independence or the validity of the business judgment.

**Exceptions / defenses:** If demand is made and refused, the refusal is reviewed under the BJR (wrongful-refusal claim). In Delaware, making a demand concedes the board's independence.

**Authority:** Corp. Code § 800(b)(2); *Bader v. Anderson* (2009) 179 Cal.App.4th 775 and *Shields v. Singleton* (1993) 15 Cal.App.4th 1611 (California courts look to Delaware's *Aronson*/*Rales* tests) [S-824]; *United Food & Commercial Workers Union v. Zuckerberg* (Del. 2021) (three-part universal test) [S-813] [VERIFY V-715]; *Aronson v. Lewis*, 473 A.2d 805 (Del. 1984); *Rales v. Blasband*, 634 A.2d 927 (Del. 1993); MBCA § 7.42.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** general rule: demand or futility (cite both MBCA universal demand and Delaware futility). California: § 800(b)(2) particularity, applying Delaware tests as persuasive; at least one California appellate court has applied *Zuckerberg* to a Delaware company (*Tola v. Bryant*) [S-825] [VERIFY V-715].

**Example:** Two of three directors (Alex and Baker) approved a contract with Alex's company. Alex is interested; if Baker lacks independence from Alex, a majority is compromised and demand is futile (Jul 2024 Q1 pattern) [VERIFY V-071].

### R-BUS-084 — Derivative suit: security for expenses, special litigation committees and recovery
**Rule (write this):** In California, within 30 days after service the corporation or a defendant may move for an order requiring the plaintiff to post a bond for the defendants' reasonable expenses, including attorney's fees, on the ground that there is no reasonable possibility the action will benefit the corporation or its shareholders (or that the moving defendant did not participate in the transaction); the bond may not exceed $50,000 in total. A committee of independent directors may move to dismiss a derivative suit; courts defer to its good-faith, informed decision (and in Delaware, where demand was excused, also apply their own business judgment). Any recovery goes to the corporation, settlement requires court approval, and a successful plaintiff's attorney's fees are paid by the corporation.

**Elements:** Bond: timely motion; showing; cap. SLC: independence, good faith, reasonable investigation (and, under *Zapata*, the court's own judgment).

**Exceptions / defenses:** The bond secures the moving defendants' reasonable expenses if the action fails; case law reportedly treats it as their exclusive source of recovery under § 800 [VERIFY V-936]. If the plaintiff fails to post the ordered bond, the action is dismissed.

**Authority:** Corp. Code § 800(c)–(f) [VERIFY V-707]; *Hale v. Southern California IPA Medical Group, Inc.* (2001) 86 Cal.App.4th 919 ($50,000 total cap) [S-802]; *Zapata Corp. v. Maldonado*, 430 A.2d 779 (Del. 1981); *Auerbach v. Bennett*, 47 N.Y.2d 619 (1979); MBCA §§ 7.44–7.46.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** **differs.** The MBCA and Delaware have no security-for-expenses requirement; California does (and so do some other states).

**Example:** PickWinners moves for a bond, arguing the suit has no reasonable possibility of benefiting the company. If granted, the plaintiff must post up to $50,000 or face dismissal.

## V. Corporations — fundamental changes

### R-BUS-085 — Fundamental changes: amendment, merger, sale of assets, dissolution
**Rule (write this):** Fundamental corporate changes require board approval followed by shareholder approval. Amendments of the articles, mergers and other reorganizations, and the sale of all or substantially all assets outside the regular course of business require approval by the board and the shareholders — in California and Delaware, a majority of the outstanding shares entitled to vote (and class votes where a class is adversely affected); under the current MBCA, more votes cast for than against at a meeting at which a quorum of a majority of the votes entitled to be cast is present (MBCA §§ 10.03, 11.04, 12.02; many states still use the older MBCA's majority of all votes entitled to be cast) [S-1151]; voluntary dissolution may be elected by shareholders holding 50% of the voting power. A parent owning at least 90% of a subsidiary may merge it without the subsidiary's shareholders' vote (short-form merger).

**Elements:** 1. Identify the change. 2. Board resolution. 3. Shareholder vote (California/Delaware: majority of outstanding shares; current MBCA: more votes for than against at a meeting with a majority quorum [S-1151]; class votes). 4. Dissenters' rights (R-BUS-086). 5. Filing.

**Exceptions / defenses:** California: no vote is required by the shareholders of a constituent corporation if it or its shareholders will own more than five-sixths of the voting power of the surviving or acquiring corporation [VERIFY V-711]. Delaware: whale-minnow (20% dilution) exception for the survivor's stockholders (DGCL § 251(f)).

**Authority:** Corp. Code §§ 152, 902–903, 1001, 1101, 1110, 1200–1201, 1900 [VERIFY V-711]; MBCA §§ 10.03, 11.04–11.05, 12.02, 14.02; DGCL §§ 242, 251, 253, 271, 275.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. Same structure, but the vote differs: California (and Delaware) count a majority of the outstanding shares, the current MBCA counts votes cast at a meeting with a majority quorum [S-1151]; California's "reorganization" vocabulary (merger, exchange, sale-of-assets reorganizations) is a California label.

**Example:** A television maker sells its TV plants (90% of its assets) to pivot to bicycles. That is a sale of substantially all assets requiring board and outstanding-share approval.

### R-BUS-086 — Dissenters' (appraisal) rights
**Rule (write this):** A shareholder who does not vote for certain fundamental changes — chiefly mergers and other reorganizations, and in many states sales of substantially all assets — may demand that the corporation buy the shares at their fair value (in California, fair market value as of the day of, and immediately before, the first announcement of the terms, excluding any appreciation or depreciation caused by the proposed transaction), by following the statutory steps precisely. Appraisal is generally the exclusive remedy, except to challenge a transaction that was not validly approved or (under the MBCA and Delaware) that involves fraud or unfair dealing by a controller. California is stricter: a shareholder entitled to appraisal may not attack the validity of the reorganization except to test whether the required shares were legally voted for it (Corp. Code § 1312(a)); the exception is a reorganization in which one party is controlled by, or under common control with, another party, where a shareholder who has not demanded payment may sue to set it aside or rescind it but not for damages (§ 1312(b)); and *Steinberg v. Amplica, Inc.* (1986) 42 Cal.3d 1198 held appraisal exclusive even though the shareholder alleged that fraud and breach of fiduciary duty by the majority depressed the price.

**Elements:** 1. Triggering transaction. 2. Did not vote in favor. 3. Timely written demand. 4. Tender of shares. 5. Valuation by agreement or court.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Market-out: shares listed on a national securities exchange generally have no appraisal right, with exceptions (e.g., transfer restrictions; in Delaware, when holders must take cash for listed shares) [S-816]. California's former 5% demand carve-back was eliminated in 2012 [VERIFY V-711].

**Authority:** Corp. Code §§ 1300–1312 [S-816] [S-1159] [S-1160] [S-1153]; *Steinberg v. Amplica, Inc.* (1986) 42 Cal.3d 1198 [S-1152]; *Busse v. United PanAm Financial Corp.* (Cal. Ct. App. 2014) (§ 1312(b) rescission, not damages) [S-1153] [VERIFY V-938]; MBCA §§ 13.01–13.40; DGCL § 262.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** not on the MBE. Same core; the market-out and valuation date differ, and California makes appraisal more exclusive — no attack on the reorganization except to test the vote unless one party controls, or is under common control with, another party (Corp. Code § 1312; *Steinberg v. Amplica*). Pending in late 2026: *Li v. ArcSoft, Inc.*, No. S294190, where the California Supreme Court accepted a Ninth Circuit certified question on whether § 1312(a) and *Steinberg* bar shareholders who did not seek appraisal from suing for damages [S-1162] [S-1799] [S-1800]; until decided, apply *Steinberg*, and check for a decision before the exam [VERIFY V-1040].

**Example:** A minority holder in a closely held company votes against a cash-out merger and demands payment. She is entitled to a court appraisal of fair value.

### R-BUS-087 — Defensive measures and sale of the company (Unocal and Revlon)
**Rule (write this):** When a board adopts defensive measures against a takeover threat, it must show that it had reasonable grounds to believe a danger to corporate policy and effectiveness existed (after good-faith, reasonable investigation) and that its response was reasonable in relation to the threat (not preclusive or coercive). Once a sale or break-up of the company becomes inevitable, or a change of control is underway, the directors' duty becomes to obtain the best value reasonably available for the shareholders.

**Elements:** *Unocal*: 1. threat; 2. proportionate response. *Revlon*: 1. sale of control or break-up; 2. value-maximizing process.

**Exceptions / defenses:** BJR applies once the enhanced-scrutiny burden is met; fully informed, uncoerced stockholder approval can restore BJR review (*Corwin v. KKR Financial Holdings LLC*, 125 A.3d 304 (Del. 2015)).

**Authority:** *Unocal Corp. v. Mesa Petroleum Co.*, 493 A.2d 946 (Del. 1985); *Revlon, Inc. v. MacAndrews & Forbes Holdings, Inc.*, 506 A.2d 173 (Del. 1986).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** low yield on California essays; mention only if a takeover is in the facts.

**Example:** A board facing a hostile bid adopts a poison pill and simultaneously agrees to sell to a friendly bidder for less. Once it chose to sell, *Revlon* required it to seek the best price.

## V. Corporations — securities

### R-BUS-088 — Rule 10b-5: the private action
**Rule (write this):** Rule 10b-5 makes it unlawful, in connection with the purchase or sale of any security, to employ any device, scheme or artifice to defraud, to make an untrue statement of a material fact or omit a material fact necessary to make statements not misleading, or to engage in any act or practice that operates as a fraud. A private plaintiff must prove (1) a material misrepresentation or omission, (2) scienter, (3) a connection with the purchase or sale of a security, (4) reliance, (5) economic loss, and (6) loss causation, using an instrumentality of interstate commerce.

**Elements:** **Material**: a substantial likelihood that a reasonable investor would consider it important (for contingent events, probability × magnitude). **Scienter**: intent to deceive or recklessness (not negligence). **Standing**: actual purchaser or seller (*Blue Chip Stamps*). **Reliance**: presumed for public statements in an efficient market (fraud on the market) or for omissions where there was a duty to disclose (*Affiliated Ute*). **Loss causation**: the misstatement caused the loss.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Applies to **any** security, including shares of a private closely held corporation, as long as interstate means (phone, mail, internet) are used. No private aiding-and-abetting liability; only the "maker" of a statement is liable under 10b-5(b). Limitations: 2 years after discovery, 5 years after the violation.

**Authority:** Securities Exchange Act § 10(b), 15 U.S.C. § 78j(b); 17 C.F.R. § 240.10b-5; *Basic Inc. v. Levinson*, 485 U.S. 224 (1988); *TSC Industries, Inc. v. Northway, Inc.*, 426 U.S. 438 (1976); *Ernst & Ernst v. Hochfelder*, 425 U.S. 185 (1976); *Blue Chip Stamps v. Manor Drug Stores*, 421 U.S. 723 (1975); *Affiliated Ute Citizens v. United States*, 406 U.S. 128 (1972); *Dura Pharmaceuticals, Inc. v. Broudo*, 544 U.S. 336 (2005); *Stoneridge Investment Partners, LLC v. Scientific-Atlanta, Inc.*, 552 U.S. 148 (2008); *Janus Capital Group, Inc. v. First Derivative Traders*, 564 U.S. 135 (2011); 28 U.S.C. § 1658(b).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** federal law; same on any essay.

**Example:** A CEO tells a shareholder of their private company that "sales are flat" while knowing a huge contract has been signed, and buys her shares. The shareholder can sue under 10b-5 even though the stock is not publicly traded.

### R-BUS-089 — Insider trading: the classical theory
**Rule (write this):** A corporate insider — a director, officer, employee, controlling shareholder, or a temporary insider such as the company's lawyer, accountant or consultant — who trades in the company's securities on the basis of material nonpublic information violates Rule 10b-5 unless the insider first discloses the information or abstains from trading. The duty arises from the relationship of trust and confidence between the insider and the corporation's shareholders; a stranger with no such relationship has no general duty to disclose.

**Elements:** 1. Insider (or temporary insider). 2. Material, nonpublic information. 3. Trade "on the basis of" it (awareness of the information when trading suffices under Rule 10b5-1). 4. Scienter. 5. Breach of duty (disclose or abstain).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Information that is public or immaterial; trades under a pre-existing written trading plan (Rule 10b5-1(c)) adopted in good faith before awareness, subject to cooling-off periods added in 2022 [VERIFY V-718]. Remedies: SEC civil penalty, set by the court, of up to three times the profit gained or loss avoided (Exchange Act § 21A(a)(2)) [S-1700]; § 21A(a)(1) reaches only trades on or through the facilities of a national securities exchange or from or through a broker or dealer (and not part of an issuer's public offering), so a privately negotiated face-to-face trade falls outside the treble penalty [S-1790] — on such facts write "civil penalties" without the multiple; criminal prosecution; private suits by contemporaneous traders capped at the profit (Exchange Act § 20A).

**Authority:** *Chiarella v. United States*, 445 U.S. 222 (1980); *Dirks v. SEC*, 463 U.S. 646 (1983) (temporary insiders, fn. 14); 17 C.F.R. § 240.10b5-1; 15 U.S.C. §§ 78t-1, 78u-1 [S-1700] [S-1790].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** federal law; California has a parallel statute (R-BUS-093).

**Example:** A director learns at a board meeting that the company will be acquired at a premium and buys shares the next morning before the announcement. Classic 10b-5 violation.

### R-BUS-090 — Tipper and tippee liability
**Rule (write this):** A tippee who trades on material nonpublic information is liable if the tipper breached a fiduciary duty by disclosing it for a personal benefit and the tippee knew or should have known of the breach. A personal benefit includes a pecuniary gain, a reputational benefit translating into future earnings, or a gift of confidential information to a trading relative or friend. The tipper is liable for the tippee's trades even if the tipper does not trade.

**Elements:** 1. Tipper owes a duty (insider or misappropriator). 2. Disclosure for a **personal benefit** (gift to friend/relative suffices). 3. Tippee **knew or should have known** of the breach. 4. Tippee trades. Remote tippees: same knowledge test.

**Exceptions / defenses:** No tipper breach (e.g., a whistleblower exposing fraud for no personal benefit, as in *Dirks*) → no tippee liability.

**Authority:** *Dirks v. SEC*, 463 U.S. 646 (1983); *Salman v. United States*, 580 U.S. 39 (2016).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** federal law.

**Example:** An officer tells his brother, "Buy now — the FDA approval comes Friday," as a gift. Both are liable: the officer as tipper (gift = personal benefit), the brother as a tippee who knew the source.

### R-BUS-091 — Misappropriation theory and Rule 14e-3
**Rule (write this):** A person who trades on confidential information in breach of a duty owed to the **source** of the information (an employer, client or other person who entrusted it) commits securities fraud under the misappropriation theory, even though no duty is owed to the counterparty shareholders. Separately, Rule 14e-3 prohibits anyone who possesses material nonpublic information about a tender offer, knowing or having reason to know it came from the offeror, the target or their agents, from trading once substantial steps toward the offer have begun — without any breach of duty.

**Elements:** Misappropriation: 1. duty of trust or confidence to the source (Rule 10b5-2: agreement to keep confidential; history or pattern of sharing confidences; spouse, parent, child or sibling presumption); 2. undisclosed use of the information to trade (deception of the source); 3. materiality, nonpublic, scienter. Rule 14e-3: tender offer context; information from offeror/target insiders; substantial steps.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Disclosure to the source that one plans to trade defeats deception under 10b-5 (but may breach other duties).

**Authority:** *United States v. O'Hagan*, 521 U.S. 642 (1997); 17 C.F.R. §§ 240.10b5-2, 240.14e-3.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** federal law. Crossover with PR: a lawyer who trades on a client's tender-offer plans violates both securities law and duties of loyalty and confidentiality (Jul 2014 Q3 pattern; R-PRF-026) [VERIFY V-071].

**Example:** A law-firm partner learns that client Sid will launch a tender offer for Chipco and buys 10,000 Chipco shares. Under *O'Hagan* he deceived his source (the client) and violated Rule 14e-3; he also breached his fiduciary duties to the client.

### R-BUS-092 — Section 16(b) short-swing profits
**Rule (write this):** Any profit realized by a director, an officer, or a beneficial owner of more than 10% of a class of a reporting company's registered equity securities from any purchase and sale, or sale and purchase, of the company's equity securities within a period of less than six months belongs to the company, regardless of intent or the use of inside information. The company may sue, and a shareholder may sue on the company's behalf if the company fails to do so within 60 days after request.

**Elements:** 1. Issuer has a class of equity registered under Exchange Act § 12 (public company). 2. Defendant is a director or officer (at the time of purchase **or** sale) or a >10% holder (at the time of **both** purchase and sale; the purchase that makes one a 10% holder does not count). 3. Matching purchase and sale within less than six months. 4. Profit computed by matching the lowest-priced purchase with the highest-priced sale (no netting of losses). 5. Suit within two years.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Strict liability: good faith and lack of inside information are irrelevant. Does not apply to private (non-registered) companies.

**Authority:** Exchange Act § 16(a)–(b), 15 U.S.C. § 78p(a)–(b); *Foremost-McKesson, Inc. v. Provident Securities Co.*, 423 U.S. 232 (1976); *Reliance Electric Co. v. Emerson Electric Co.*, 404 U.S. 418 (1972); *Gollust v. Mendell*, 501 U.S. 115 (1991); *Smolowe v. Delendo Corp.*, 136 F.2d 231 (2d Cir. 1943).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** federal law.

**Example:** A public company's CFO buys 1,000 shares at $20 in January and sells 1,000 at $35 in May. The company recovers $15,000, even if she knew nothing confidential.

### R-BUS-093 — California securities law: insider trading statute
**Rule (write this):** California's Corporate Securities Law separately prohibits an issuer, or an officer, director or controlling person of an issuer (and certain others with access to information through their relationship), from purchasing or selling the issuer's securities in California while knowing material information about the issuer not generally available to the public that would significantly affect the market price, unless the other party knows the information. The defrauded counterparty may recover damages, and in specified larger issuers the issuer may recover up to three times the insider's profit.

**Elements:** 1. Covered person. 2. Purchase or sale "in this state". 3. Knowledge of material, nonpublic, price-sensitive information. 4. Counterparty's lack of knowledge.

**Exceptions / defenses:** The issuer's treble-damages remedy is a disgorgement statute; the issuer need not show harm (*Friese v. Superior Court* (2005) (Cal. Ct. App.)) [S-827] [VERIFY V-722].

**Authority:** Corp. Code §§ 25402, 25502, 25502.5 [S-827] [VERIFY V-722].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** add one California sentence after the 10b-5 analysis if the trade occurred in California.

**Example:** A San Diego officer sells shares to another Californian while knowing of an undisclosed product recall. Besides 10b-5, § 25402 gives the buyer a California damages claim.

## V. Corporations — close and nonprofit corporations; choice of law

### R-BUS-094 — Close corporations and shareholders' agreements
**Rule (write this):** A closely held corporation has few shareholders, no public market for its shares, and substantial shareholder participation in management. California recognizes a statutory close corporation whose articles say "This corporation is a close corporation" and limit record holders to a specified number not exceeding 35; its shareholders may by written agreement among all of them govern the corporation's affairs, including management by shareholders instead of a board, and the failure to observe formalities under such an agreement is not a factor in alter-ego liability. Under general law, shareholders of a non-public corporation may by unanimous agreement vary the statutory governance norms.

**Elements:** California: 1. articles statement; 2. limit of 35 holders; 3. legend on certificates; 4. written agreement signed by all shareholders. Shareholders who take over management assume directors' liabilities.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A non-statutory close corporation must follow ordinary formalities; agreements restricting board discretion may be invalid unless unanimous or otherwise authorized.

**Authority:** Corp. Code §§ 158, 186, 300(b)–(e), 418, 706(a) [VERIFY V-719]; MBCA § 7.32; DGCL §§ 341–356.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same idea; MBCA § 7.32 is the general-law vehicle, Corp. Code § 158/§ 300(b) the California one.

**Example:** Three founders elect statutory close corporation status and sign an agreement that they will manage the business directly without a board. The agreement is valid, and skipping board meetings does not support piercing.

### R-BUS-095 — Fiduciary duties among shareholders of a close corporation
**Rule (write this):** Some jurisdictions hold that shareholders of a close corporation owe one another a partner-like duty of utmost good faith and loyalty, so that a controlling group may not freeze out a minority (firing them, cutting off dividends, denying equal opportunity to sell shares) without a legitimate business purpose; even then, the minority may prevail by showing that the same objective could have been achieved by an alternative less harmful to its interests. California reaches similar results through the controlling-shareholder duty of *Jones v. Ahmanson* and the involuntary-dissolution statute.

**Elements:** 1. Close corporation. 2. Majority action harming the minority's reasonable expectations. 3. Legitimate business purpose (majority's burden)? 4. Less harmful alternative (minority's burden)?

**Exceptions / defenses:** Delaware rejects a special close-corporation duty and leaves protection to contracts (*Nixon v. Blackwell*, 626 A.2d 1366 (Del. 1993)).

**Authority:** *Donahue v. Rodd Electrotype Co.*, 328 N.E.2d 505 (Mass. 1975); *Wilkes v. Springside Nursing Home, Inc.*, 353 N.E.2d 657 (Mass. 1976); *Jones v. H.F. Ahmanson & Co.* (1969) 1 Cal.3d 93; MBCA § 14.30 (oppression).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** argue *Donahue/Wilkes* as general law and *Jones v. Ahmanson* as California's rule.

**Example:** The majority fires the minority shareholder-employee, cuts off her salary and pays no dividends, leaving her with unsellable shares. That is a classic freeze-out.

### R-BUS-096 — Involuntary dissolution and buyout
**Rule (write this):** In California, a court may order involuntary dissolution on the petition of holders of at least one-third of the shares (excluding shares of the wrongdoers) or of any shareholder of a close corporation, on grounds including deadlock among directors or shareholders, persistent and pervasive fraud, mismanagement or abuse of authority, persistent unfairness toward shareholders, waste of corporate property, or, in a corporation with 35 or fewer shareholders, that liquidation is reasonably necessary to protect the complaining shareholders' rights. The corporation, or holders of 50% or more of the voting power, may avoid dissolution by buying out the moving shareholders at fair value.

**Elements:** 1. Standing. 2. Statutory ground. 3. Buyout election and court-appraised fair value (based on liquidation value, taking into account the possibility of a sale of the entire business as a going concern).

**Exceptions / defenses:** The court may appoint a receiver or provisional director instead of dissolving.

**Authority:** Corp. Code §§ 1800 [S-1155], 1802–1804 [VERIFY V-932], 2000 [S-815]; *Stuparich v. Harbor Furniture Mfg., Inc.* (2000) 83 Cal.App.4th 1268 (applying § 1800(b)(5)) [S-1155]; MBCA §§ 14.30, 14.34.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same idea; the MBCA calls the ground "oppressive" conduct and offers an election to purchase (§ 14.34).

**Example:** A 40% holder of a four-shareholder company is deadlocked with the 60% holder over every decision. She can petition to dissolve, and the majority can buy her out at fair value to avoid dissolution.

### R-BUS-097 — Nonprofit corporations
**Rule (write this):** A nonprofit corporation has no shareholders and may not distribute profits to members, directors or officers; it is formed under a nonprofit statute (in California, as a public benefit, mutual benefit or religious corporation). Directors owe duties of care and loyalty similar to business-corporation directors, but self-dealing in a California public benefit corporation is tightly regulated, no more than 49% of its directors may be "interested persons" (compensated persons and their relatives), and the Attorney General supervises charitable assets.

**Elements:** 1. Type of nonprofit. 2. Duty of care (California § 5231 mirrors § 309). 3. Self-dealing transactions: approval in advance by the Attorney General or court, or by the board under specified conditions (fair and reasonable, for the corporation's benefit, after reasonable investigation that no more advantageous arrangement was available). 4. Standing to sue: the corporation, members, directors, officers, persons with a reversionary interest, and the Attorney General.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Charitable-trust principles may restrict use of donated assets.

**Authority:** Corp. Code §§ 5110 et seq., 5142, 5227, 5231, 5233 [S-822] [VERIFY V-717]; *Turner v. Victoria* (2023) 15 Cal.5th 99 (directors who lose office during litigation keep standing) [VERIFY V-717]; Model Nonprofit Corporation Act (3d ed. 2008).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** general principles on essays; nonprofit governance appears more often in PTs.

**Example:** A charity's board approves buying supplies from a director's company at market price without considering alternatives. Under § 5233 the transaction is self-dealing unless the statutory approval conditions were met.

### R-BUS-098 — Internal affairs doctrine and pseudo-foreign corporations
**Rule (write this):** Under the internal affairs doctrine, the law of the state of incorporation governs a corporation's internal affairs — the relations among the corporation, its directors, officers and shareholders (fiduciary duties, voting, distributions, derivative suits). California's Corporations Code § 2115 departs from this for "pseudo-foreign" corporations: an out-of-state corporation (other than one listed on a major exchange) that does more than half its business in California (by an average of property, payroll and sales factors) and has more than half of its voting securities held by California residents is subject to listed California provisions, such as cumulative voting, director removal, distribution limits, director liability, dissenters' rights and inspection.

**Elements:** 1. Foreign corporation. 2. Business factor test. 3. Shareholder test. 4. Not exempt. 5. Applicable California section.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Delaware's Supreme Court held that § 2115 cannot govern a Delaware corporation's internal affairs (*VantagePoint Venture Partners 1996 v. Examen, Inc.*, 871 A.2d 1108 (Del. 2005)) [S-804]; a California appellate court earlier enforced § 2115's cumulative-voting rule against a Utah corporation (*Wilson v. Louisiana-Pacific Resources, Inc.* (1982)) [S-828] [VERIFY V-708].

**Authority:** Corp. Code § 2115 [S-804] [VERIFY V-708]; *Edgar v. MITE Corp.*, 457 U.S. 624 (1982); *CTS Corp. v. Dynamics Corp. of America*, 481 U.S. 69 (1987).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** **California only.** If facts say a Delaware corporation does most of its business in California with mostly California shareholders, add one paragraph on § 2115 and the *VantagePoint* conflict.

**Example:** A Nevada corporation headquartered and selling almost entirely in California, with 80% California shareholders, refuses cumulative voting. California's § 2115 would impose it.

## V. Corporations — insider loans in bankruptcy

### R-BUS-099 — Equitable subordination of insider claims
**Rule (write this):** In bankruptcy, the court may, under principles of equitable subordination, subordinate all or part of an allowed claim to other allowed claims. An insider's claim (such as a controlling shareholder's or officer's loan to the company) is subordinated when (1) the claimant engaged in inequitable conduct, (2) the conduct injured other creditors or gave the claimant an unfair advantage, and (3) subordination is consistent with the Bankruptcy Code. Courts scrutinize insider and fiduciary claims rigorously, and gross undercapitalization combined with insider control and misuse supports subordination.

**Elements:** 1. Insider status (director, officer, person in control, relative). 2. Inequitable conduct: fraud, breach of fiduciary duty, using the company as an instrumentality, undercapitalization plus self-dealing. 3. Harm to creditors. 4. Remedy limited to the extent needed to offset the harm.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Insider status alone is not enough; a fair, documented loan by an insider to an adequately capitalized company is paid like other unsecured debt. Courts may not subordinate a whole category of claims without case-specific inquiry (*United States v. Noland*, 517 U.S. 535 (1996)).

**Authority:** 11 U.S.C. §§ 101(31), 510(c); *Pepper v. Litton*, 308 U.S. 295 (1939); *Taylor v. Standard Gas & Electric Co.*, 306 U.S. 307 (1939) (the "Deep Rock" doctrine, named for the subsidiary); *In re Mobile Steel Co.*, 563 F.2d 692 (5th Cir. 1977) [S-820].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** federal bankruptcy law; same on any essay.

**Example:** Art, the president and controlling shareholder of a thinly capitalized retailer, "lends" it $50,000, then has it repay him first while trade creditors go unpaid. In bankruptcy, Supplier asks the court to subordinate Art's claim behind all outside creditors (Feb 2017 Q4 pattern).

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: PRC bankruptcy practice also ranks some controlling-shareholder claims behind ordinary creditors (the "deep rock" idea is cited in PRC judicial guidance), but the US rule rests on an express Code section and case-specific inequity (approx.).

### R-BUS-100 — Recharacterization of insider loans and insider preferences
**Rule (write this):** A bankruptcy court may recharacterize a purported insider loan as an equity contribution if, in substance, it was risk capital rather than debt; equity is paid only after all creditors. Courts weigh the labels and documentation, a fixed maturity and interest, a repayment source independent of profits, security, the company's capitalization and ability to borrow from outsiders, the identity of interest between lender and shareholder, and how the funds were used. Separately, a payment to an insider within one year before the bankruptcy filing on an antecedent debt may be avoided as a preference.

**Elements:** Recharacterization factors (above); in the Ninth Circuit, the question is decided by reference to state law on whether the claim is a "right to payment" (*In re Fitness Holdings Int'l, Inc.*, 714 F.3d 1141 (9th Cir. 2013)) [S-820] [VERIFY V-724]. Preference: transfer to an insider, on an antecedent debt, while insolvent, within one year, enabling the insider to receive more than in a liquidation.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Contemporaneous exchange, ordinary-course payments and new-value defenses to preferences.

**Authority:** 11 U.S.C. §§ 502(b), 547(b)(4)(B); *Bayer Corp. v. MascoTech, Inc. (In re AutoStyle Plastics, Inc.)*, 269 F.3d 726 (6th Cir. 2001); *In re Fitness Holdings Int'l, Inc.*, 714 F.3d 1141 (9th Cir. 2013) [S-820].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** federal bankruptcy law.

**Example:** Art's "loan" had no note, no interest, no maturity date and was needed because no bank would lend. The trustee argues it was equity; if so, Art is paid only after every creditor.

## VI. Other business structures

### R-BUS-101 — Limited liability company: formation and nature
**Rule (write this):** A limited liability company is formed by filing articles of organization with the Secretary of State and is a separate legal entity whose members are not personally liable for its obligations solely by reason of being members. Its internal affairs are governed by the operating agreement (written, oral or implied) and, where the agreement is silent, by the LLC statute. It is member-managed unless designated manager-managed: in California, by a statement in the articles of organization that it is managed by one or more managers; under the uniform act, by an express provision in the operating agreement (RULLCA § 407(a)).

**Elements:** 1. Filed articles. 2. Operating agreement (cannot override nonwaivable provisions such as eliminating the duty of loyalty or the obligation of good faith). 3. Management structure.

**Exceptions / defenses:** If nothing was filed, the business is a general partnership (or a sole proprietorship). A California LLC may not render licensed professional services, such as law (R-BUS-108).

**Authority:** Corp. Code §§ 17701.02, 17701.04, 17701.10, 17702.01 [S-826] [VERIFY V-729]; § 17704.07(a) (member-managed unless the articles contain the § 17702.01(b)(5) statement) [S-1796]; RULLCA §§ 104, 105, 201, 407.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same core rule; California differs in two respects: the professional-services bar, and manager management designated in the articles rather than in the operating agreement.

**Example:** Two founders file articles for "Bright Paint LLC" and start trading. Even without a written operating agreement, the LLC exists and the statute fills the gaps.

### R-BUS-102 — LLC members' liability and veil piercing
**Rule (write this):** An LLC member or manager is not personally liable for the LLC's debts, obligations or liabilities solely by reason of being a member or manager. A member is subject to alter-ego (veil-piercing) liability under the same standards that apply to corporate shareholders, except that in California the failure to hold meetings or observe formalities about meetings is not a factor tending to establish alter-ego liability where the articles or operating agreement do not expressly require meetings.

**Elements:** 1. Shield. 2. Alter-ego test (unity of interest + inequitable result; R-BUS-047). 3. California's formalities exception.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Members remain liable for their own torts, guarantees and wrongful distributions received.

**Authority:** Corp. Code § 17703.04 [S-805]; RULLCA § 304.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same core; California statutory formalities exception — write it.

**Example:** A single-member LLC never holds meetings (its agreement does not require them) but the owner keeps separate books and capitalizes it adequately. No piercing.

### R-BUS-103 — LLC management and agency authority
**Rule (write this):** In a member-managed LLC, each member has equal management rights, ordinary matters are decided by a majority of members, and acts outside the ordinary course and amendments to the operating agreement require all members' consent. In a manager-managed LLC, managers decide ordinary matters and members' consent is needed only for specified acts. In California, every member of a member-managed LLC, and every manager of a manager-managed LLC, is an agent of the LLC and binds it by acts for apparently carrying on in the usual way the LLC's business, unless the actor lacked authority and the third party actually knew; a member of a manager-managed LLC acting solely as a member is not an agent.

**Elements:** 1. Member- vs. manager-managed (look at the articles). 2. Actual authority (agreement, votes). 3. Apparent authority (statutory in California; common-law agency under RULLCA).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Uniform RULLCA § 301 provides that a member is **not** an agent solely by being a member and leaves authority to agency law; California's version keeps statutory apparent authority [S-807] [S-1154].

**Authority:** Corp. Code § 17703.01 [S-807] [S-1154]; § 17704.07(b)(3), (b)(5), (c)(3)–(4) (default management rules: majority of members for ordinary-course matters; all members to amend the operating agreement; in a manager-managed LLC, majority of managers for ordinary matters and all members for acts outside the ordinary course) [S-1796]; § 17704.07(b)(4) (member-managed: acts outside the ordinary course need all members) [VERIFY V-931]; RULLCA §§ 301, 407.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** **differs.** General (RULLCA): no statutory agency for members; use agency law. California: statutory apparent authority for members (member-managed) and managers (manager-managed).

**Example:** A member of a member-managed California LLC signs a routine supply contract despite an internal rule requiring two signatures; the supplier did not know of the rule. The LLC is bound.

### R-BUS-104 — LLC fiduciary duties
**Rule (write this):** In a member-managed LLC, each member owes the LLC and the other members a duty of loyalty (account for benefits and LLC opportunities, refrain from adverse dealing, refrain from competing) and a duty of care limited to refraining from grossly negligent or reckless conduct, intentional misconduct or knowing violation of law, and must act consistently with good faith and fair dealing. In a manager-managed LLC, the managers owe these duties; members owe no fiduciary duty solely by reason of being members.

**Elements:** Same structure as partners' duties (R-BUS-034, R-BUS-035).

**Exceptions / defenses:** The operating agreement may modify but not eliminate loyalty, may not unreasonably reduce care, and may authorize specific conflicted acts after disclosure.

**Authority:** Corp. Code §§ 17701.10, 17704.09 [S-806]; RULLCA § 409.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A member-manager of a California LLC that develops apartments buys an adjacent lot offered to the LLC. He usurped an LLC opportunity in breach of § 17704.09(b).

### R-BUS-105 — LLC transfers, dissociation and dissolution
**Rule (write this):** An LLC member's transferable interest is only the right to distributions; a transferee does not become a member without the consent the agreement requires, and a member's judgment creditor reaches the interest by charging order. A member may dissociate, but under RULLCA a dissociated member does not get a buyout and becomes a mere transferee. An LLC is dissolved by agreed events, consent of the members, or judicial decree (for example, when it is not reasonably practicable to carry on the business, or management is deadlocked or acting illegally, fraudulently or oppressively).

**Elements:** 1. Transfer → economic rights only. 2. Dissociation → no buyout default. 3. Dissolution → winding up, creditors first.

**Exceptions / defenses:** The operating agreement commonly adds buy-sell provisions.

**Authority:** Corp. Code §§ 17705.02–17705.03, 17706.02–17706.03, 17707.01–17707.03 [VERIFY V-729]; RULLCA §§ 502–503, 601–603, 701.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule in outline.

**Example:** A 25% member quits her LLC. Absent an agreement, she keeps her right to distributions but loses management rights and cannot force a buyout.

### R-BUS-106 — Joint ventures
**Rule (write this):** A joint venture is an association of persons who combine property, skill or knowledge to carry out a single business enterprise for profit, with an express or implied agreement to share profits (and usually losses) and a right of joint control. It is treated as a partnership for a limited purpose: partnership law governs, so each co-venturer is an agent of the venture within its scope, co-venturers are jointly and severally liable for its obligations, and they owe each other partners' fiduciary duties.

**Elements:** 1. Joint interest in a common enterprise. 2. Agreement to share profits (and losses). 3. Right of joint control. 4. Limited scope or duration.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A co-venturer's authority is narrower than a general partner's because the venture's business is limited. The agreement may be implied from conduct.

**Authority:** *Holtz v. United Plumbing & Heating Co.* (1957) 49 Cal.2d 501 [S-819]; CACI No. 3712; *Meinhard v. Salmon*, 249 N.Y. 458, 164 N.E. 545 (1928).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** Two developers agree to build and sell one apartment building and split profits, each approving major decisions. They are joint venturers; one cannot secretly take a related deal from the landlord.

### R-BUS-107 — Sole proprietorships
**Rule (write this):** A sole proprietorship is a business owned and run by one individual with no separate legal existence. The owner holds all business assets, is personally liable without limit for all business debts and torts (including employees' torts under respondeat superior), and reports business income on the owner's own tax return. If the business uses a name other than the owner's surname, it must file a fictitious business name statement.

**Elements:** 1. One owner. 2. No entity filing. 3. Unlimited personal liability.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Once a second owner shares profits and control, the business becomes a general partnership (R-BUS-021).

**Authority:** Bus. & Prof. Code § 17910 et seq. (fictitious business names).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** Maria runs "Sunset Bakery" alone. A delivery driver she employs causes a crash; Maria is personally liable, and her home is reachable.

### R-BUS-108 — Choosing an entity; professional firms in California
**Rule (write this):** The main entity choices trade off liability protection, formation formalities, management structure and tax treatment: general partnerships and sole proprietorships arise without filing and expose owners to unlimited liability; LPs, LLPs, LLCs and corporations require a state filing and shield owners to varying degrees. In California, licensed professionals such as lawyers may practice through a professional corporation (for lawyers, a law corporation registered with the State Bar) or a registered LLP, but not through an LLC.

**Elements:** 1. Was anything filed? 2. What does the filing create? 3. Is the business a licensed profession (California restrictions)? 4. Who are owners vs. employees (associates are usually employees; profit-sharing and control indicate partner status)?

**Exceptions / defenses:** Titles such as "partner" given to non-owners can create partnership-by-estoppel exposure (R-BUS-023).

**Authority:** Corp. Code §§ 13400 et seq. (Moscone-Knox Professional Corporation Act), 16101, 16953, 17701.04(e) [S-826]; Bus. & Prof. Code § 6160 et seq. (law corporations).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** California-specific on professional firms; otherwise general law.

**Example:** Three lawyers open an office and share profits without filing anything. They are a general partnership; their associates are employees, and a "senior attorney" who shares profits and votes on firm matters may be a partner (Feb 2020 Q5 pattern) [VERIFY V-071].

### R-BUS-109 — Crossover: the lawyer for the organization
**Rule (write this):** A lawyer employed or retained by a corporation, partnership or LLC represents the entity acting through its authorized constituents, not its directors, officers, shareholders or partners individually. When a constituent's interests diverge from the entity's, the lawyer must clarify the role, may represent both only with the consent the conflict rules require, and must report up within the organization when a constituent's wrongdoing is likely to cause substantial injury to it.

**Elements:** See R-PRF-055 (organization as client; reporting up), R-PRF-056 (reporting out: California vs. ABA), R-PRF-024 (joint representation of entity and constituent), R-PRF-026 (business transactions with a client).

**Exceptions / defenses:** California forbids reporting out confidential information (rule 1.13 and Bus. & Prof. Code § 6068(e)); the ABA permits it in limited circumstances.

**Authority:** CRPC rule 1.13; ABA Model Rule 1.13; Bus. & Prof. Code § 6068(e).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** follow the PR call ("California and ABA authorities").

**Example:** The corporation's general counsel learns the CEO is diverting a corporate opportunity. She must report up to the board; she may not represent the CEO personally against the corporation.
