# Business Associations — worked examples

Each pattern is original. Read the facts, decide the answer, then check the analysis. Rule IDs point to [`rule-statements.md`](rule-statements.md).

## Agency

### WE-1 — The capped purchasing manager (R-BUS-008, R-BUS-009)
**Facts.** Hardware Co. appoints Lia "Purchasing Manager," tells her privately not to sign contracts over $20,000, and for a year lets her place orders of every size with Bolt Supply. Lia signs a $45,000 order with Bolt.
**Analysis.** *Actual authority:* none — the express $20,000 cap was communicated to Lia. *Apparent authority:* Hardware gave her the title and let her deal with Bolt without limit; Bolt did not know of the cap. A reasonable supplier would believe a purchasing manager with that history could place a $45,000 order, and the belief is traceable to Hardware's own conduct. **Conclusion:** Hardware is bound; Lia is liable to Hardware for exceeding her instructions.

### WE-2 — The fired salesman and the old customer (R-BUS-006, R-BUS-007)
**Facts.** Gem Co. fires sales rep Raj on May 1 but sends no notice to its customers. On May 10, Raj takes a $3,000 deposit from long-time customer Ivy and disappears.
**Analysis.** Raj's actual authority ended on May 1. Apparent authority lingers as to a customer who dealt with him before and has no notice of termination. Ivy reasonably believed Raj still represented Gem. **Conclusion:** Gem must honor the order or refund the deposit; it should have notified known customers.

### WE-3 — Signing for "a client" (R-BUS-012)
**Facts.** Broker Max signs a warehouse lease "Max Lin, as agent for a client." His client is NovaTech, which authorized the lease. NovaTech stops paying rent.
**Analysis.** NovaTech was an unidentified (partially disclosed) principal: the landlord knew there was a principal but not who. Max had actual authority, so NovaTech is bound, but an agent for an unidentified principal is also a party to the contract. **Conclusion:** The landlord may sue NovaTech and Max; Max may seek indemnity from NovaTech.

### WE-4 — The delivery driver's coffee stop (R-BUS-014, R-BUS-015)
**Facts.** Flour Co.'s driver, on his delivery route, drives three blocks off-route to buy coffee and rear-ends a car. That evening, in the company van, he drives 40 miles to a concert and causes a second crash.
**Analysis.** He is an employee (Flour Co. controls routes, hours and the van). The coffee stop is a minor deviation — a detour — so the first crash is within the scope of employment. The concert trip is a substantial personal departure — a frolic — so the second crash is outside the scope despite the company van. **Conclusion:** Flour Co. is vicariously liable for the first crash only; the driver is personally liable for both.

### WE-5 — The bouncer and the parking-lot fight (R-BUS-016)
**Facts.** A nightclub bouncer ejects a rowdy patron and punches him in the doorway. An hour later, off shift, the bouncer sees the patron in the parking lot and attacks him over a personal insult.
**Analysis.** The first punch was force used in performing the job of ejecting patrons — within scope even if excessive. The second attack served only a personal grudge, after work, and was not engendered by the job in the California sense (*Lisa M.*). **Conclusion:** The club is liable for the first injury, not the second (unless negligent retention can be shown).

## General partnerships and other unincorporated entities

### WE-6 — Three friends and a shoe store (R-BUS-021, R-BUS-028, R-BUS-029)
**Facts.** Ana, Ben and Cy agree to open a shoe store, each contributing $10,000, sharing profits equally and taking turns managing. They file nothing. The store borrows $30,000 from Delta Bank and defaults.
**Analysis.** Two or more persons carrying on a business for profit as co-owners form a general partnership regardless of intent (§ 16202). Shared profits and management confirm co-ownership. All partners are jointly and severally liable (§ 16306), but Delta must first exhaust partnership assets (§ 16307(d)). **Conclusion:** Delta can recover the full $30,000 from any partner after exhausting the store's assets; the partners then adjust among themselves.

### WE-7 — The lender with a profit share (R-BUS-022)
**Facts.** Pia lends a café $50,000 in return for "15% of net profits until $70,000 is repaid." She has no management role and does not hold herself out as an owner.
**Analysis.** Receipt of profits raises a presumption of partnership, but profits received as interest or charge on a loan fall within a statutory exception (§ 16202(c)(3)). Pia has no control and shares no losses. **Conclusion:** Pia is a creditor, not a partner, and is not liable for the café's debts.

### WE-8 — The retired name partner (R-BUS-023)
**Facts.** Dr. Ko retires but lets her old clinic keep "Ko & Lee, Partners" on its signs and letterhead. A supplier, relying on the letterhead and Ko's reputation, extends $40,000 of credit.
**Analysis.** Ko consented to being represented as a partner, and the supplier relied on the representation in extending credit. **Conclusion:** Ko is liable to the supplier as a purported partner (§ 16308), even though she is not a partner.

### WE-9 — The partner who exceeded the limit (R-BUS-025)
**Facts.** Partners in a bakery agree that no partner may spend more than $5,000 without consent. Partner Dee buys a $12,000 oven from Oven Co., which knows nothing of the agreement.
**Analysis.** Buying an oven is apparently in the ordinary course of a bakery's business. Although Dee lacked actual authority, Oven Co. did not know or have notice of the limit (§ 16301(1)). **Conclusion:** The partnership is bound; Dee may be liable to her partners for breaching the agreement.

### WE-10 — The money partner and the work partner (R-BUS-032)
**Facts.** Ray contributes $80,000; Sal contributes only his labor. They agree to split profits 50/50 but say nothing about losses. The business fails and all $80,000 is lost.
**Analysis.** Under § 16401(b), losses follow profits, so Sal would owe Ray $40,000. Under *Kovacik v. Reed* (1957) 49 Cal.2d 166, where one partner contributes only money and the other only services and they did not agree on losses, the services partner is not liable for the capital loss — he has already lost the value of his work. Whether *Kovacik* survives the 1994 Act is unsettled [VERIFY V-706]. **Conclusion:** Argue both; a California court may follow *Kovacik*.

### WE-11 — The limited partner who ran the show (R-BUS-042)
**Facts.** Lia is a limited partner in Vine LP. When the general partner falls ill, Lia negotiates a $200,000 loan from Bank, telling Bank, "I make the decisions here." Vine LP defaults.
**Analysis.** Under ULPA (2001) § 303, Lia is shielded even though she participated in control. California keeps a narrow control rule: a limited partner who participates in control is liable as a general partner to persons who transact business with actual knowledge of that participation and a reasonable belief, based on her conduct, that she is a general partner (Corp. Code § 15903.03) [S-1150]. Bank knew of her control and her statements support a reasonable belief. **Conclusion:** Under general law Lia is not liable; in California, Bank has a strong argument that she is.

### WE-12 — The law firm that never filed (R-BUS-044, R-BUS-045, R-BUS-108)
**Facts.** Three lawyers open "Ash, Birch & Cole," share profits and decisions, and hire two salaried associates. They file nothing. Partner Ash commits malpractice.
**Analysis.** Without a filing the firm is a general partnership, not an LLP or law corporation; a California LLC was not an option for law practice (§ 17701.04(e)). The associates are employees, not partners (no profit share or control). The firm is liable for Ash's malpractice in the ordinary course (§ 16305), and Birch and Cole are jointly and severally liable (§ 16306(a)). Had the firm registered as an LLP, Birch and Cole would be shielded (§ 16306(c)). **Conclusion:** All three partners are exposed; registration would have protected the non-negligent partners.

## Corporations

### WE-13 — The lease signed before filing (R-BUS-054, R-BUS-055)
**Facts.** On March 1, Ed signs a 3-year lease "for Paint Co., a corporation to be formed." Articles are filed March 20. In April the board votes to "assume the lease," and Paint Co. pays rent for a year, then fails.
**Analysis.** Ed was a promoter contracting for a nonexistent corporation, so he is personally liable unless the lease shows the landlord agreed to look only to the corporation. Paint Co. adopted the lease expressly (board vote) and impliedly (paying rent), so it is liable from adoption. Adoption does not release Ed; only a novation would. **Conclusion:** Both Paint Co. and Ed are liable to the landlord; Ed may seek indemnity from Paint Co.

### WE-14 — The rejected articles (R-BUS-052, R-BUS-053)
**Facts.** Fay mails articles for "Fay's Tools, Inc." to the Secretary of State; they are rejected for a name conflict, but the rejection letter is lost. Fay runs the business as "Fay's Tools, Inc." for a year, buying on credit from Gear Co., which never asked for a personal guarantee.
**Analysis.** *De facto corporation:* a statute existed, Fay made a good-faith attempt, and she used corporate powers — she did not know the filing failed. *Estoppel:* Gear dealt with the business as a corporation and relied on corporate credit. Under MBCA § 2.04, only those who knew there was no incorporation are liable; Fay did not know. **Conclusion:** Fay likely avoids personal liability to Gear; a tort victim, who did not choose to deal with a corporation, would have a stronger claim against her.

### WE-15 — $1,000 of capital (R-BUS-047, R-BUS-048)
**Facts.** Two owners form Toy Co. with $1,000, never hold meetings, pay personal car loans from its account, and withdraw $40,000 in "bonuses" while owing Supplier $55,000.
**Analysis.** *Unity of interest:* gross undercapitalization relative to inventory needs (*Automotriz*), commingling, personal use of corporate funds, no formalities (*Associated Vendors* factors). *Inequitable result:* withdrawing $40,000 while the debt went unpaid is siphoning, not mere non-payment. Supplier is a contract creditor, which weakens but does not defeat piercing given the siphoning. **Conclusion:** A court will likely pierce and hold both active owners personally liable.

### WE-16 — The uninformed dividend vote (R-BUS-061, R-BUS-062, R-BUS-080)
**Facts.** After a 20-minute meeting, with no financial statements in front of them, three directors approve a $5 million dividend that leaves the corporation with liabilities exceeding its assets.
**Analysis.** *Care/BJR:* declaring dividends is a business judgment, but the BJR presumes an informed decision; approving a large distribution without reviewing financial statements is a failure of reasonable inquiry (§ 309; *Van Gorkom*). *Legality:* in California, a distribution must satisfy the retained-earnings or balance-sheet test and not leave the corporation unable to pay debts as they mature (§§ 500–501); here the balance-sheet test fails and nothing suggests sufficient retained earnings. Directors who approve an unlawful distribution are jointly and severally liable (§ 316), and exculpation does not cover § 316 liability. **Conclusion:** The directors are likely liable for the unlawful portion; shareholders who knew of the impropriety must repay (§ 506).

### WE-17 — The director's building (R-BUS-065, R-BUS-066)
**Facts.** Director Cate owns the building Corp. wants to lease. She discloses her ownership; the other four directors approve a 5-year lease at a rent 25% above market. A shareholder sues.
**Analysis.** Self-dealing triggers the duty of loyalty. *General law (MBCA §§ 8.60–8.63):* disclosure and approval by qualified directors provide a safe harbor. *California § 310(a)(2):* disinterested-board approval protects the lease only if it was also "just and reasonable" when approved; 25% above market with no offsetting benefit likely fails. Shareholder approval under § 310(a)(1) would have avoided the fairness inquiry. **Conclusion:** Under California law the lease is voidable and Cate may owe the excess rent.

### WE-18 — The opportunity on the golf course (R-BUS-067)
**Facts.** Bike Corp.'s CEO hears from a Bike Corp. supplier that a rival e-bike maker is for sale. He buys it personally without telling the board. Bike Corp. had cash and had discussed entering e-bikes.
**Analysis.** The opportunity is in Bike Corp.'s line of business, Bike Corp. had an expectancy (it was considering e-bikes), it could finance the deal, and the CEO learned of it through a corporate relationship. He did not offer it to the corporation. **Conclusion:** He usurped a corporate opportunity; a court may impose a constructive trust on the e-bike company (R-REM-044).

### WE-19 — Shareholder suit with an interested board (R-BUS-081 to R-BUS-084)
**Facts.** Pat, a shareholder since 2020, wants to sue directors Al and Bea over a 2025 contract with Al's company; the third director, Cy, never attends meetings. Pat makes no demand.
**Analysis.** *Direct or derivative:* the harm (overpayment) is to the corporation, so the claim is derivative. *Standing:* Pat owned shares at the time and still does. *Demand:* under the MBCA, a written demand and 90-day wait are required. Under Delaware's *Zuckerberg* test and California § 800(b)(2), Pat may plead futility: Al received a material personal benefit, and if Bea is not independent of Al, at least half the board is compromised. *California bond:* the defendants may move within 30 days for security of up to $50,000. **Conclusion:** In California, Pat can likely proceed after pleading futility with particularity, subject to a possible bond.

### WE-20 — The lawyer and the tender offer (R-BUS-091, R-BUS-109)
**Facts.** Lawyer Lin, whose firm represents Acquirer in a planned tender offer for Target, buys 5,000 Target shares the day before the announcement.
**Analysis.** Lin owes no duty to Target's shareholders, so the classical theory does not fit. Under the misappropriation theory (*O'Hagan*), Lin deceived the source of the information — his firm and its client — by trading on it without disclosure. Rule 14e-3 independently prohibits trading on nonpublic tender-offer information from the bidder. Lin also breached duties of loyalty and confidentiality to the client. **Conclusion:** Lin is liable under Rule 10b-5 and Rule 14e-3, and faces discipline (PR crossover).

### WE-21 — The CFO's quick profit (R-BUS-092)
**Facts.** The CFO of a NYSE-listed company sells 2,000 shares at $50 in February and buys 2,000 at $30 in June, with no inside information.
**Analysis.** She is an officer of a reporting company; a sale and a purchase within less than six months are matched regardless of intent. Profit = (50 − 30) × 2,000 = $40,000. **Conclusion:** The company (or a shareholder after a 60-day demand) recovers $40,000 under § 16(b).

### WE-22 — The president's loan in bankruptcy (R-BUS-099, R-BUS-100)
**Facts.** Art formed Retail, Inc. with $5,000 of equity and funded operations with a $50,000 undocumented "loan." As the business failed, Art had Retail pay suppliers late while repaying himself $10,000. Retail files bankruptcy.
**Analysis.** *Recharacterization:* no note, interest or maturity, and no outside lender would have lent to so thinly capitalized a company — the $50,000 looks like equity. *Equitable subordination:* even if debt, Art engaged in inequitable conduct (undercapitalization plus preferring himself), which harmed trade creditors; subordination under § 510(c) is consistent with the Code (*Pepper v. Litton*; *Mobile Steel*). *Preference:* the $10,000 repayment within one year to an insider is avoidable (§ 547(b)(4)(B)). **Conclusion:** Art likely recovers nothing until outside creditors are paid, and must return the $10,000.

### WE-23 — The LLC member's supply contract (R-BUS-102, R-BUS-103)
**Facts.** Moe is one of three members of member-managed Lumen LLC (California). Its operating agreement requires two signatures for contracts, but Moe alone signs a routine $8,000 supply contract; the vendor knows nothing of the rule. Lumen never holds member meetings, and its agreement does not require them.
**Analysis.** In California, each member of a member-managed LLC binds it for acts apparently in the usual course unless the third party actually knows of the lack of authority (§ 17703.01) [S-1154]; the vendor did not know. Under uniform RULLCA, the vendor would need apparent authority under agency law. Missed meetings are not an alter-ego factor where the agreement does not require them (§ 17703.04). **Conclusion:** Lumen is bound; members are not personally liable merely because meetings were skipped.

### WE-24 — The majority's holding-company scheme (R-BUS-071, R-BUS-095)
**Facts.** Holders of 85% of a closely held savings company transfer their shares to a new holding company, take its shares public, and refuse to let the 15% minority exchange on the same terms. The minority's shares become unsellable.
**Analysis.** A controlling group may not use its control to benefit itself alone or to the detriment of the minority (*Jones v. H.F. Ahmanson & Co.* (1969) 1 Cal.3d 93). Creating a market only for the majority's shares is a non-pro-rata benefit without a legitimate business purpose. The claim is direct because the minority was harmed as shareholders. **Conclusion:** The minority can recover damages (or equivalent relief) directly from the majority.
