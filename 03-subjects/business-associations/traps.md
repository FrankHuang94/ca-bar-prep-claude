# Business Associations — traps

Business Associations is essay-only, so these are the mistakes graders punish, not MBE distractors. Rule IDs point to [`rule-statements.md`](rule-statements.md).

## Entity and formation
1. **Calling an unfiled business a corporation or LLC because the owners used "Inc." or "LLC".** Without a filing there is no shield; two co-owners are a general partnership (R-BUS-021). Test de facto/estoppel only if there was a real attempt to file (R-BUS-052).
2. **Requiring intent to form a partnership.** Intent to be "partners" is irrelevant; intent to carry on as co-owners is what counts (R-BUS-021).
3. **Treating every profit share as partnership.** Loan interest, wages, rent, annuities and goodwill payments are exceptions to the presumption (R-BUS-022).
4. **Forgetting the pre-incorporation partnership.** When co-owners run the business before filing, debts from that period are partnership debts — a separate theory from promoter liability (R-BUS-021, R-BUS-054).
5. **Saying adoption releases the promoter.** Adoption makes the corporation liable too; only a novation releases the promoter (R-BUS-054, R-BUS-055).
6. **Calling adoption "ratification" without comment.** A nonexistent principal cannot ratify; adoption takes effect from adoption, not relation back (R-BUS-010, R-BUS-055).
7. **Applying corporation by estoppel to a tort victim.** Estoppel protects owners only against creditors who chose to deal with a "corporation" (R-BUS-053).

## Agency
8. **Letting the agent create apparent authority.** "He told the supplier he could sign" is not enough; the belief must be traceable to the principal (R-BUS-009).
9. **Stopping at actual authority.** Most essays turn on apparent authority or ratification after actual authority fails (R-BUS-008 to R-BUS-010).
10. **Assuming termination ends all authority.** Apparent authority lingers until third parties who dealt with the agent get notice (R-BUS-007).
11. **Forgetting the agent's own liability.** Unidentified and undisclosed principals leave the agent personally bound; unauthorized agents breach the implied warranty of authority (R-BUS-012, R-BUS-013).
12. **Using respondeat superior for independent contractors.** First decide employee status by right to control (R-BUS-002, R-BUS-017).
13. **Treating a forbidden act as automatically outside scope.** Doing the job in a forbidden way is still within scope (*Perez*) (R-BUS-014).
14. **Applying California's ABC test to tort liability.** The ABC test governs Labor Code status, not common-law respondeat superior (R-BUS-002).

## Partnerships
15. **Saying a secret internal limit protects the partnership.** It does not unless the third party knew or had notice (R-BUS-025).
16. **Skipping exhaustion.** Partners are jointly and severally liable, but creditors usually must exhaust partnership assets first — except against the partner who personally committed the tort (R-BUS-029).
17. **Holding a new partner personally liable for old debts.** Only the new partner's capital is at risk for pre-admission obligations (R-BUS-030).
18. **Using the old UPA rule that any departure dissolves the partnership.** Under RUPA/California, dissociation leads to a buyout unless a dissolution event occurs (R-BUS-037, R-BUS-038).
19. **Ordinary negligence as a partner's breach of care.** The statutory duty of care requires gross negligence, recklessness, intentional misconduct or knowing violation of law (R-BUS-035).
20. **Shielding a California limited partner automatically.** California keeps a narrow control rule; ULPA (2001) does not (R-BUS-042).
21. **Thinking an LLP shields the negligent partner.** The shield covers partnership obligations, not the partner's own wrongs; the LLP itself remains liable (R-BUS-045).

## Corporations
22. **Piercing on undercapitalization alone, or on "creditor wasn't paid."** You need unity of interest **and** an inequitable result (R-BUS-047).
23. **Piercing against every shareholder.** Only owners who participated in the abuse are liable (R-BUS-047).
24. **Confusing veil piercing with personal tort liability.** An officer who commits the tort is liable without piercing (R-BUS-050).
25. **Applying the BJR to a conflicted director.** A conflict removes BJR protection; go to loyalty, safe harbors and fairness (R-BUS-062, R-BUS-064).
26. **Judging the decision by its outcome.** The BJR asks about process (informed, good faith, disinterested), not results (R-BUS-062).
27. **Analyzing the board as a block.** Graders reward director-by-director analysis: the interested director, the inattentive director and the careful director reach different results (R-BUS-061, R-BUS-063).
28. **Treating board approval as a complete cure in California.** § 310(a)(2) also requires the deal be just and reasonable; shareholder approval under § 310(a)(1) does not (R-BUS-065).
29. **Saying exculpation covers loyalty or bad-faith claims.** It never does, and California also excludes reckless disregard and an unexcused pattern of inattention (R-BUS-070).
30. **Forgetting legality after BJR in dividend questions.** A dividend can be a protected business judgment yet unlawful under §§ 500–501 (R-BUS-079, R-BUS-080).
31. **Treating shareholders as having a right to dividends.** Only after declaration (R-BUS-079).
32. **Skipping direct vs. derivative.** Make it the first heading of any shareholder-suit answer (R-BUS-081).
33. **Using the MBCA demand rule as the only rule.** Discuss MBCA universal demand and Delaware futility; add California's § 800(b)(2) and the $50,000 bond (R-BUS-083, R-BUS-084).
34. **Assuming ultra vires contracts are void.** They bind third parties; the remedies are a shareholder injunction, suits against managers, or state action (R-BUS-057).
35. **Mixing up cumulative voting defaults.** Opt-in under the MBCA and Delaware; mandatory on notice in California unless listed (R-BUS-074).

## Securities and bankruptcy
36. **Saying Rule 10b-5 applies only to public companies.** It covers any security traded through interstate means, including private-company shares; § 16(b) is the one limited to public reporting companies (R-BUS-088, R-BUS-092).
37. **Finding insider trading without a duty.** Strangers who overhear information owe no duty (*Chiarella*); identify classical, tippee or misappropriation duty — or Rule 14e-3 for tender offers (R-BUS-089 to R-BUS-091).
38. **Requiring inside information for § 16(b).** It is strict liability; intent and information are irrelevant (R-BUS-092).
39. **Counting the purchase that makes someone a 10% holder.** A 10% holder must hold more than 10% at the time of both purchase and sale (*Foremost-McKesson*) (R-BUS-092).
40. **Treating an insider loan as automatically subordinated.** Insider status alone is not enough; show inequitable conduct and harm, or that the "loan" was really equity (R-BUS-099, R-BUS-100).
