# Torts — worked examples

Each example is an original fact pattern followed by a short IRAC answer of the kind you would write under one heading. Rule IDs point to [`rule-statements.md`](rule-statements.md). Where California differs, the last line says so.

---

### 1. The snatched phone (R-TRT-002, R-TRT-003)
**Facts.** On a crowded train, D yanks P's phone out of her hand and runs. P never saw D coming.

**Analysis.** Battery requires intent to cause a harmful or offensive contact with Π's person, which includes things closely connected to it. D intended to grab the phone P was holding; snatching an object from someone's hand offends a reasonable sense of dignity. P's lack of advance awareness does not matter for battery. It does defeat assault, which needs apprehension of an imminent contact.

**Conclusion.** Battery yes; assault no. Conversion of the phone is a separate claim (R-TRT-008).

### 2. The locked fitting room (R-TRT-004, R-TRT-013)
**Facts.** A clerk sees P carry three shirts into a fitting room and leave with two. The clerk locks P in the back office for three hours until police come, refusing to check the room. The third shirt was on the fitting-room floor.

**Analysis.** False imprisonment: intentional confinement, no escape, P aware. The shopkeeper's privilege protects a reasonable belief of theft, but only for a reasonable time and in a reasonable manner. The belief was arguably reasonable. Holding P for three hours without making a one-minute check of the fitting room was not.

**Conclusion.** The privilege likely fails; the store is liable for false imprisonment.

### 3. Spring gun in the cabin (R-TRT-012)
**Facts.** Tired of break-ins at his empty mountain cabin, D wires a shotgun to the door. P, a burglar, is shot in the leg.

**Analysis.** A possessor may use only reasonable, nondeadly force to protect property. A device is privileged only to the extent the owner could have used the same force in person. D could not have shot a non-threatening burglar in an empty cabin.

**Conclusion.** D is liable for battery (*Katko v. Briney*). P's criminal conduct may limit damages in some states. **CA:** Civ. Code § 847 may limit a felony trespasser's recovery [VERIFY V-609].

### 4. The storm and the dock (R-TRT-017)
**Facts.** A sudden squall forces B to tie her sailboat to P's private dock. P cuts the line, and the boat is wrecked on rocks. The waves had also cracked P's dock before P cut the line.

**Analysis.** Private necessity privileged B's entry, so P could not expel her and is liable for the boat. The privilege is incomplete, so B must pay for the actual damage to the dock.

**Conclusion.** B owes P for the dock; P owes B for the boat.

### 5. The drowning stranger (R-TRT-021)
**Facts.** D, a strong swimmer, watches a stranger struggle in a lake and does nothing. Later D's own canoe tips over and D's guest is pinned underneath; D swims away.

**Analysis.** There is no general duty to rescue a stranger, so D is not liable to the stranger. As host who took the guest out in D's canoe, D created the peril and has a special relationship with the guest, so D owed the guest reasonable care.

**Conclusion.** No liability to the stranger; liability to the guest.

### 6. The hospital guard (R-TRT-022)
**Facts.** A hospital knows that a visitor has threatened a patient twice. To save money, the CEO removes the ward's only guard, and the visitor assaults the patient.

**Analysis.** No general duty to protect from third parties, but the hospital–patient custodial relationship is a special relationship. Under California's *Brown* two-step, the *Rowland* factors do not limit that duty: the attack was highly foreseeable, the burden small, and the moral blame significant.

**Conclusion.** Duty and likely breach. If the hospital is public, add Gov. Code § 815.2 vicarious liability and § 820.2 discretionary immunity: a ward-level staffing choice is operational, not basic policy (R-TRT-075).

### 7. The teen on the jet ski (R-TRT-026)
**Facts.** A 15-year-old operating a jet ski at speed near swimmers hits P.

**Analysis.** Children are normally judged as a reasonable child of like age, intelligence and experience. Operating a powered watercraft is an adult activity, so the adult standard applies. A reasonable adult would not speed near swimmers.

**Conclusion.** Breach.

### 8. The texting statute (R-TRT-033)
**Facts.** A statute bans texting while driving "to reduce traffic collisions". D, texting, drifts across the line and sideswipes P's car.

**Analysis.** P, a motorist, is within the protected class; a collision is the harm the statute targets; there is no excuse.

**Conclusion.** Negligence per se establishes duty and breach (majority); P must still prove causation. **CA:** Evid. Code § 669 presumes negligence, and D may try to rebut it.

### 9. The falling sign (R-TRT-035)
**Facts.** A metal sign bolted above the entrance of D's store falls on P. No one knows why.

**Analysis.** Signs do not ordinarily fall without negligence in installation or maintenance. The sign was within D's control as possessor, and P did nothing to it.

**Conclusion.** Res ipsa lets the jury infer D's negligence, so P survives a directed verdict. **CA:** Evid. Code § 646 makes it a presumption affecting the burden of producing evidence.

### 10. Two careless contractors (R-TRT-038, R-TRT-041)
**Facts.** Contractor A negligently leaves a trench unguarded, and contractor B negligently removes a warning light. P falls in and breaks her hip ($100,000 of damages, $40,000 of them economic). The jury finds A 60% and B 40% at fault; A is insolvent.

**Analysis.** Each was a substantial factor in one indivisible injury.
- **MBE default (joint and several):** P collects all $100,000 from B, who may seek contribution from A.
- **California (Prop 51):** B is jointly liable for the $40,000 economic damages but only 40% of the $60,000 non-economic damages ($24,000).

**Conclusion.** B pays $100,000 under the MBE default; $64,000 in California.

### 11. The careless driver and the careless surgeon (R-TRT-046)
**Facts.** D negligently breaks P's arm. At the hospital, Dr. X negligently damages a nerve while setting it.

**Analysis.** Negligent medical treatment of an injury is a foreseeable intervening cause, not a superseding one.

**Conclusion.** D is liable for the arm and the nerve damage; Dr. X is liable for the nerve damage. D and Dr. X are jointly liable for the aggravation.

### 12. The unlocked car and the joyrider (R-TRT-046)
**Facts.** D leaves her car running with the keys in it outside a convenience store in an area known for car thefts. A thief takes it and crashes into P.

**Analysis.** A criminal act is superseding only if unforeseeable. Theft was the very risk that made leaving the car running negligent, and the area made it foreseeable.

**Conclusion.** Not superseding; D's negligence is a proximate cause. Some courts disagree as to harm from the thief's driving, so argue both sides.

### 13. The swimming pool and the child (R-TRT-048, R-TRT-051)
**Facts.** D's backyard pool, next to a park, has a broken gate. A 6-year-old wanders in and nearly drowns.

**Analysis.** Attractive nuisance:
- D knew children use the park and could foresee trespass.
- A pool is a man-made condition posing a risk of death.
- Young children do not appreciate drowning risks in pools.
- Fixing a gate is a slight burden.

**Conclusion.** Liability. **CA:** under *Rowland*, ask simply whether D used reasonable care, with the child's status only a factor. Same result.

### 14. The mother at the crosswalk (R-TRT-055)
**Facts.** M is waiting at a corner when she hears a crash and turns to see her daughter knocked down by D's car. Her neighbor N sees it too, and M's sister learns of it an hour later by phone.

**Analysis.** Under *Thing*:
- M is closely related to the victim, was present, was aware the event was injuring her daughter, and suffered serious distress, so M recovers.
- N is not closely related.
- The sister was not present.

**Conclusion.** M recovers; N and the sister do not.

### 15. Misdiagnosis (R-TRT-056)
**Facts.** A clinic negligently tells P she has a fatal illness. For three weeks before the error is caught, P suffers severe anxiety, though she has no physical symptoms.

**Analysis.** Majority: a special-relationship exception for negligent communications by physicians. **CA:** *Burgess* and *Molien* direct-victim theory, because the clinic's duty ran directly to P as its patient.

**Conclusion.** Recovery without physical injury.

### 16. The restaurant's lost profits (R-TRT-061)
**Facts.** A tanker truck negligently spills chemicals; the city closes the street for a week, and P's restaurant loses $30,000 in sales. The restaurant suffered no physical damage.

**Analysis.** There is no duty to avoid purely economic loss to strangers absent a special relationship. P is one of many businesses, an indeterminate class (*Southern California Gas Leak Cases*).

**Conclusion.** No recovery in negligence; a public-nuisance claim requires special injury different in kind, which lost business may or may not be.

### 17. The bouncer (R-TRT-063, R-TRT-067)
**Facts.** A nightclub bouncer, ejecting a rowdy patron, breaks his nose with excessive force. The club knew of the bouncer's two prior assaults on patrons.

**Analysis.** Force is part of a bouncer's job, so the battery was within the scope of employment and the club is vicariously liable. The club is also directly liable for negligent retention, given its knowledge of his prior assaults.

**Conclusion.** The club is liable on both theories; punitive damages are possible against the club in California if an officer, director or managing agent knew of the bouncer's prior assaults and kept him on in conscious disregard of patrons' safety, or ratified the battery (Civ. Code § 3294(b)).

### 18. The hospital caterer (R-TRT-064)
**Facts.** A hospital contracts with an independent caterer for patient meals. The caterer ignores an allergy flag and serves peanuts; the patient goes into anaphylaxis.

**Analysis.** The general rule is no vicarious liability for an independent contractor. But a hospital's duty to provide safe care and food to patients in its custody is arguably nondelegable. If the hospital also failed to verify the caterer's allergy procedures, it is directly negligent.

**Conclusion.** The hospital is likely liable.

### 19. The soccer coach (R-TRT-073)
**Facts.** A high-school coach, angry after a loss, orders a 14-year-old with no goalkeeping training to stand in goal while adult players take full-power shots. She is hit in the face and injured.

**Analysis.** **CA:** primary assumption of risk bars claims for risks inherent in the sport. A coach is liable only for intentional injury or for conduct totally outside the range of ordinary coaching (*Kahn*). Forcing an untrained minor into an obviously dangerous drill out of anger is arguably reckless conduct outside ordinary coaching.

**Conclusion.** Likely triable and likely liable. Majority/MBE: implied assumption of risk requires the player's knowing, voluntary choice, which an ordered minor did not make.

### 20. The chimpanzee and the puppy (R-TRT-078, R-TRT-079)
**Facts.** At a breeder's home, a visitor enters a room he was told to avoid and is bitten by a pet chimpanzee. Another visitor is nipped by a puppy with no history of biting.

**Analysis.**
- **Chimpanzee:** a wild animal, so strict liability for a bite, a characteristic danger. If he knew of the chimp, his knowing and unreasonable encounter reduces his recovery under comparative fault (MBE default; California) and bars it only in a contributory-negligence jurisdiction (R-TRT-080). In some states a trespasser in the room must prove negligence.
- **Puppy:** no known propensity, so no common-law strict liability; negligence only. **CA:** Civ. Code § 3342 imposes strict liability for the bite regardless of knowledge.

**Conclusion.** The chimp claim is likely strong, subject to comparative fault; the puppy claim depends on § 3342 in California.

### 21. The detergent and the aluminum pans (R-TRT-083, R-TRT-084, R-TRT-089, R-TRT-090)
**Facts.** DetCo's dishwasher powder, labeled "safe for all cookware", leaves a residue that reacts with aluminum pans. DetCo's own lab had noted the reaction. P becomes ill.

**Analysis.**
- **Strict liability:** the design is arguably defective (risk-benefit: an alternative formula was feasible), and there is clearly a warning defect, since the risk was known and unwarned (*Anderson*).
- **Negligence:** DetCo ignored its own test result.
- **Express warranty:** "safe for all cookware" is an affirmation of fact.
- **Merchantability:** the powder is not fit for ordinary use.
- **§ 402B and fraud:** a public misrepresentation, possibly with scienter.
- **Battery:** fails without substantial certainty.

**Conclusion.** Liability on several theories; punitive damages possible.

### 22. The theater next door (R-TRT-092, R-TRT-094, R-TRT-006)
**Facts.** P buys a new home next to an outdoor theater that has operated for 20 years; concerts run until 11 p.m. The theater manager also enters P's yard without permission to measure sound levels.

**Analysis.**
- **Nuisance:** loud late-night music is a substantial interference to a normal person. Unreasonableness is balanced: the theater's value and its prior location, against residential use. Coming to the nuisance weighs against P but is not a bar.
- **Trespass:** the manager's entry was intentional; no harm is needed.

**Conclusion.** Trespass yes (nominal damages, injunction against repeated entry). Nuisance is a close question; a court might limit hours instead of closing the theater.

### 23. The professor's email (R-TRT-096 to R-TRT-100)
**Facts.** A professor emails the whole department that a named graduate student "plagiarized her thesis". The professor had not read the thesis, and it was original.

**Analysis.**
- The statement is a false factual statement, defamatory, about the student and published: libel.
- The student is a private figure, and the matter is arguably of limited public concern.
- The professor was at least negligent (*Gertz*; **CA:** *Brown v. Kelly Broadcasting*).
- A common-interest privilege (Civ. Code § 47(c)) is lost by reckless disregard: accusing someone without reading the work is reckless.

**Conclusion.** Liability. Damages are presumed for libel on its face; IIED is a separate, harder claim.

### 24. The broker's silence (R-TRT-107, R-TRT-108)
**Facts.** A seller's broker knows the house's foundation is cracked, hides the cracks behind new drywall, and tells the buyer "no structural issues".

**Analysis.**
- **Affirmative misrepresentation** with scienter, intended to induce reliance; the buyer's reliance was justifiable, since the cracks were concealed.
- **Active concealment** is independently actionable.
- **Damages:** out-of-pocket loss or benefit of the bargain; **CA:** Civ. Code § 3343 for property transactions.

**Conclusion.** Fraud; punitive damages possible.

### 25. The rival studio (R-TRT-110, R-TRT-111)
**Facts.** Studio D lures actor A away from a fixed two-year contract with Studio P by doubling his pay. D also persuades a director with no contract, whom P was about to hire, to sign with D instead, by truthfully pointing to D's larger budget.

**Analysis.**
- **Inducing breach of a fixed-term contract** is improper interference, and D is liable.
- **The director:** only a prospective relationship. **CA:** P must show an independently wrongful act; truthful competition is not wrongful (*Della Penna*).

**Conclusion.** Liable for the actor; not liable for the director.

### 26. The surviving family (R-TRT-059, R-TRT-060)
**Facts.** D's negligent driving injures V, who dies in the hospital two months later in 2027. V leaves a wife and two children.

**Analysis.**
- **Survival action (estate):** V's medical bills and lost earnings for two months, plus punitive damages if warranted. No pain and suffering in an action filed in 2027 (§ 377.34).
- **Wrongful death (wife and children under CCP § 377.60):** lost support and services, funeral costs, and loss of V's love, companionship and comfort. No grief.

**Conclusion.** Two claims, with distinct damages.
