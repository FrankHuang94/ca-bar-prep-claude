# Torts — MBE rules

## 1. NCBE's assumptions for Torts
NCBE's outline sets two default assumptions [S-040]:
- **Survival actions and claims for wrongful death are available.** You do not need a statute to let the estate or the heirs sue.
- **Joint and several liability, with pure comparative negligence, is the relevant rule unless otherwise indicated.**

Practical consequences:
- **Pure comparative fault.** A plaintiff who is 70% at fault still recovers 30%. Do not bar recovery unless the question says the jurisdiction uses contributory negligence or a modified (50% or 51%) system. If it does, apply exactly what the question states.
- **Last clear chance** matters only if the question states that contributory negligence applies.
- **Joint and several liability.** Π can collect the whole judgment from any one defendant who caused an indivisible injury. The paying defendant then seeks contribution. The MBE does **not** apply California's Proposition 51.
- **Statutes** in the fact pattern are there for a reason. Ask: (1) negligence per se? (2) a modified comparative-fault statute? (3) a dram-shop, dog-bite or recreational-use statute? Apply the text as given.
- **Generally accepted principles.** Answer by the majority common-law or Restatement rule, not the California rule (*Rowland*, *Thing*, *Knight*, *Barker* burden shifting). When the majority is unclear, NCBE usually writes the question so the outcome is the same under any view.

## 2. Weighting
- **About half** of the 25 scored Torts questions come from **II Negligence**, about 12–13 questions. The other half come from **I Intentional torts, III Strict and products liability and IV Other torts** together [S-040].
- Within negligence, the heaviest areas are:
  - duty (including affirmative duties and landowners);
  - breach (standard of care, negligence per se, res ipsa);
  - causation (proximate cause and intervening causes);
  - defenses (comparative fault and assumption of risk).
- Within the other half, the heaviest areas are:
  - **products liability** (several questions per exam);
  - **intentional torts and privileges**;
  - **defamation and privacy** (with constitutional rules);
  - **nuisance**.
  - Misrepresentation and interference with business relations are lighter. IV.D's inclusion is unconfirmed [VERIFY V-023].
- Plan your practice in roughly that ratio: about 50% negligence, 15–20% products and strict liability, 15% intentional torts and defenses, 15% defamation, privacy, nuisance and misrepresentation. See `05-mbe/subject-weighting.md`.

## 3. Black-letter rules the MBE favors

### Intentional torts (I)
1. **Intent = purpose or substantial certainty**; a child can intend (R-TRT-001).
2. **Battery needs no awareness and no injury**; offensive = offends a reasonable sense of dignity (R-TRT-002).
3. **Assault needs awareness and imminence**; words alone usually are not enough; apparent ability suffices (R-TRT-003).
4. **False imprisonment** requires no reasonable known escape and awareness **or** harm (R-TRT-004).
5. **IIED** requires outrageousness and severe distress. Third-party bystanders recover only under R2d § 46(2): immediate family present, known to Δ (R-TRT-005).
6. **Trespass to land:** intent to enter, not intent to trespass; mistake is no defense; no damage required (R-TRT-006).
7. **Trespass to chattels vs. conversion** depends on the seriousness of the interference. Conversion means a forced sale at value when converted (R-TRT-007, R-TRT-008).
8. **Consent:** apparent consent counts; exceeding the scope is battery; fraud only as to the essential nature of the act (R-TRT-009).
9. **Self-defense and defense of others:** a reasonable mistake is OK. **Defense of property:** never deadly force; mistake about the intruder's privilege is not protected (R-TRT-010 to R-TRT-012).
10. **Private necessity is incomplete:** pay for actual damage; the owner may not expel. **Public necessity:** no liability (R-TRT-016, R-TRT-017).
11. **Shopkeeper's privilege:** reasonable belief, reasonable time and manner (R-TRT-013).

### Negligence (II)
12. **Duty to foreseeable plaintiffs** (Cardozo, *Palsgraf*); rescuers are foreseeable (R-TRT-019, R-TRT-024).
13. **No duty to rescue,** unless there is a special relationship, Δ created the peril, or Δ voluntarily undertook to help (R-TRT-021).
14. **Duty to control third persons** requires a special relationship and knowledge of the danger (R-TRT-022).
15. **Reasonable person:** objective; physical disability counted; mental deficiency not; superior skill counted (R-TRT-025).
16. **Children:** like age, intelligence and experience, except **adult activities** (R-TRT-026).
17. **Professionals:** the profession's standard (specialists: national standard), proven by experts (R-TRT-027). **Informed consent:** the physician must disclose material risks and alternatives; jurisdictions split between a reasonable-physician and a reasonable-patient (*Canterbury*) disclosure standard, so apply the standard the stem gives. The undisclosed risk must materialize, and causation is usually objective — a reasonable patient, properly informed, would have declined (R-TRT-028).
18. **Negligence per se:** class of persons + type of harm + unexcused violation = breach. **Causation is still required** (R-TRT-033, R-TRT-034).
19. **Custom** is evidence, not conclusive (R-TRT-032).
20. **Res ipsa:** a permissible inference; Π gets to the jury; Δ's control (R-TRT-035).
21. **Slip-and-fall:** actual or constructive notice (R-TRT-036).
22. **But-for causation,** then substantial factor for merged causes; *Summers* burden shift; concert of action (R-TRT-037 to R-TRT-039).
23. **Proximate cause:** foreseeable type of harm; manner and extent need not be foreseeable; **eggshell plaintiff** (R-TRT-044, R-TRT-045).
24. **Intervening causes:** medical malpractice, rescuers, foreseeable negligence and foreseeable crimes **do not** supersede; unforeseeable intentional crimes do (R-TRT-046).
25. **Landowners:** trespasser, licensee, invitee duties; attractive nuisance (R2d § 339) (R-TRT-047 to R-TRT-050).
26. **NIED:** zone of danger; bystander must be a close relative who perceived the event contemporaneously (R-TRT-054, R-TRT-055).
27. **Pure economic loss:** no recovery in negligence absent a special relationship (R-TRT-061).
28. **Respondeat superior:** within scope (detour yes, frolic no); intentional torts if job-related (R-TRT-063).
29. **Independent contractors:** no vicarious liability, except inherently dangerous work, nondelegable duties and the hirer's own negligence (R-TRT-064).
30. **Pure comparative fault; joint and several liability; contribution** (R-TRT-041, R-TRT-043, R-TRT-070).
31. **Assumption of risk:** knowledge plus voluntariness. Under pure comparative fault, implied secondary assumption is just fault apportionment (R-TRT-072).

### Strict and products liability (III)
32. **Abnormally dangerous activities:** strict liability, but only for the kind of harm that makes the activity dangerous (R-TRT-077).
33. **Wild animals:** strict liability. **Domestic animals:** strict liability only with known dangerous propensity (R-TRT-078, R-TRT-079).
34. **Plaintiff's fault and strict liability:** under the MBE default (pure comparative fault), the plaintiff's negligence, including a knowing and unreasonable encounter with the risk, reduces recovery. Only if the stem says contributory negligence applies: mere failure to discover the danger is no defense, and a knowing, unreasonable encounter is a complete bar (R-TRT-080).
35. **Strict products liability:**
    - any commercial seller, including retailers (R-TRT-085);
    - defect existed when the product left Δ;
    - no privity needed; bystanders recover (R-TRT-086);
    - personal injury and other property only.
36. **Three defect types.** Manufacturing defect = deviation from intended design. Design defect = consumer expectations or risk-utility with a feasible alternative. Warning defect = foreseeable, knowable risks (R-TRT-082 to R-TRT-084).
37. **Foreseeable misuse** is no defense; **unforeseeable misuse and substantial alteration** are defenses; comparative fault reduces recovery (R-TRT-087).
38. **Products negligence** requires fault; **warranty** claims need merchant status (merchantability) or reliance (fitness) (R-TRT-088, R-TRT-089).

### Other torts (IV)
39. **Private nuisance:** substantial (normal person) and unreasonable (balancing). Coming to the nuisance is only a factor. Public nuisance: special injury (R-TRT-092 to R-TRT-094).
40. **Defamation:**
    - libel: presumed damages;
    - slander: special damages unless per se;
    - **public figure: actual malice** (knowing or reckless);
    - private figure, public concern: at least negligence, plus actual malice for presumed or punitive damages;
    - opinion is protected only if it implies no false facts (R-TRT-096 to R-TRT-100).
41. **Privileges:** absolute (judicial, legislative, spousal); qualified (common interest), lost by abuse (R-TRT-100).
42. **Privacy:** four torts; truth is no defense to public disclosure; appropriation needs use of the identity for Δ's benefit (R-TRT-101 to R-TRT-104).
43. **Fraud:** scienter and justifiable reliance. **Negligent misrepresentation:** business context, limited class, pecuniary loss (R-TRT-107, R-TRT-109).
44. **Interference with contract:** knowledge, intent, improper means or motive; competition privilege for at-will contracts (R-TRT-110).

## 4. How the MBE tests each category

| Category | Typical question form | What usually decides it |
|---|---|---|
| I.A Intentional torts | "Will P prevail in an action for assault?" | One missing element (awareness, imminence, escape, severity) |
| I.B Defenses | "Is D's best defense…?" / "Will D's privilege defeat the claim?" | Proportionality; mistake rules; incomplete privilege |
| II.A Duty | Failure to rescue; landowner; third-party crime | Special relationship; foreseeability; entrant status |
| II.B Standard | Child driver; professional; statute | Adult activity; expert evidence; protected class or type of harm |
| II.C Proof | Falling object; surgery mishap; spill | Res ipsa elements; notice |
| II.D Causation and apportionment | Two negligent Δs; dollar amounts | But-for vs. substantial factor; pure comparative + joint and several arithmetic |
| II.E Limits | Chain of events; bystander distress; economic loss | Foreseeable intervening act; contemporaneous perception; no duty for pure economic loss |
| II.F Vicarious | Employee's errand; contractor's work | Scope; nondelegable duty; inherently dangerous |
| II.G Defenses | P's own carelessness; signed release; sports | Pure comparative arithmetic; release scope; inherent risk |
| III Strict and products | Blasting; pet; defective product sold by a retailer | Kind of harm; knowledge of propensity; defect type; misuse |
| IV Other | Neighbor's noise; newspaper story; misstatement | Substantial and unreasonable; public-figure status and fault; reliance |

## 5. Recurring fact patterns
1. **The unaware victim.** A gun pointed at a sleeping person: no assault. A kiss on a sleeping person: battery.
2. **The bystander who arrives later.** No NIED recovery for one who arrives at the hospital after the accident.
3. **The statute that protects someone else.** A trespasser hurt by a broken railing required by a code protecting tenants: no negligence per se. Use ordinary negligence.
4. **The child driving a snowmobile, car or boat:** adult standard.
5. **The rescuer:** the original negligent Δ owes him a duty. A professional firefighter is barred by the firefighter's rule.
6. **Retailer liability.** "The retailer could not have discovered the defect" is a distractor. Strict liability still applies; indemnity comes from the manufacturer.
7. **Two hunters, one pellet:** *Summers* burden shift.
8. **Negligent driver plus negligent doctor:** the first Δ is liable for the aggravation.
9. **Landowner and trespassing child:** the attractive-nuisance factors, especially an artificial condition and the child not appreciating the risk.
10. **Shopkeeper detains a suspect:** a reasonable belief and reasonable time protect a mistake.
11. **Private necessity:** boat, dock and storm. The owner who unties the boat is liable; the boater pays the dock damage.
12. **Newspaper and politician:** actual malice requires serious doubts, not just a failure to check.
13. **Credit report to a few subscribers:** private concern, so presumed damages are allowed without actual malice (*Dun & Bradstreet*).
14. **Blasting startles animals:** often outside the kind of harm that makes blasting dangerous.
15. **Plaintiff 60% at fault:** under the MBE default (pure comparative), Π still recovers 40%.
16. **Employer and an employee's assault on a customer:** within scope only if the job involves force or the dispute arose from the work; otherwise negligent hiring.
17. **Release signed at a gym:** bars ordinary negligence claims, not gross negligence or intentional harm.

## 6. Quick decision rules for close answer choices
- If an answer says "because Δ did not intend to cause harm" for battery, check: did Δ intend the **contact**? (The dual-intent split is rarely the key.)
- Prefer an answer that applies the **correct standard** over one that only describes the facts.
- "Strict liability" answers are wrong for ordinary activities (driving, a normal dog without known aggression).
- "Contributory negligence bars recovery" is wrong unless the question says the jurisdiction follows it.
- In defamation, first decide **public or private Π** and **public or private concern**, then pick the fault standard.
- For causation questions, first identify the negligent aspect of Δ's conduct, then ask but-for.
