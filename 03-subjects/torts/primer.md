# Torts — primer

## 1. What tort law does
A **tort** is a civil wrong, other than a breach of contract, for which the law gives the injured person a remedy, usually money damages. Tort law answers one question: **when must one person pay for harm to another?** It does so with three levels of blame:

| Level of blame | Family of torts | Typical question |
|---|---|---|
| **Intent** (purpose or substantial certainty) | Battery, assault, false imprisonment, IIED, trespass, conversion; fraud; defamation of public figures (actual malice); interference with business relations | Did Δ mean to cause the contact, confinement, entry or deception? |
| **Negligence** (carelessness) | Negligence, negligent misrepresentation, NIED; most product negligence | Did Δ fail to act as a reasonable person would? |
| **No fault** (strict liability) | Abnormally dangerous activities, wild animals, defective products | Did Δ's dangerous activity or defective product cause the harm, however careful Δ was? |

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: the PRC Civil Code's tort book (侵权责任编) also uses fault liability, presumed fault and no-fault liability. Common-law torts are a list of named causes of action with separate elements, each developed by cases, rather than one general clause (approx.).

## 2. Map of the subject
The NCBE outline has four parts [S-040]:
- **I Intentional torts**, with the privileges (consent, self-defense, necessity and others).
- **II Negligence:** duty, standard of care, proof of breach, causation, special limits (proximate cause, land, emotional harm, economic loss), liability for others, and defenses. This is **about half** the MBE Torts questions.
- **III Strict liability and products liability.**
- **IV Other torts:** nuisance; defamation and privacy; misrepresentation; interference with business relations.

The California essay outline covers the same ground, and adds wrongful death and survival, malicious prosecution and abuse of process, and immunities [S-092] [VERIFY V-024].

## 3. Key vocabulary
- **Plaintiff (Π) / defendant (Δ):** the injured party and the person sued.
- **Prima facie case:** the elements Π must prove before Δ needs a defense.
- **Duty / breach:** whether the law requires care toward Π, and whether Δ fell short of it.
- **Actual (but-for) cause / proximate (legal) cause:** factual connection, then a policy limit based on foreseeability.
- **Reasonable person:** the objective yardstick for negligence.
- **Negligence per se:** a statute supplies the standard of care.
- **Res ipsa loquitur:** "the thing speaks for itself"; negligence inferred from the type of accident.
- **Joint and several liability:** each of several defendants can be made to pay the whole.
- **Contributory / comparative negligence:** Π's own carelessness bars recovery (old rule) or reduces it (modern rule).
- **Assumption of risk:** Π knowingly accepted a risk.
- **Respondeat superior:** an employer pays for an employee's torts within the scope of employment.
- **Privilege:** a justification that defeats an intentional-tort claim.
- **Special damages:** specific money losses. **General damages:** pain, suffering, reputation.
- **Punitive damages:** extra damages to punish malice, oppression or fraud.

The English–Chinese terms are in `02-foundations/legal-english-glossary.md` (all approximate).

## 4. How the pieces connect
1. **Start with the facts, not the doctrine.** Ask what Δ did and what Δ was thinking. Deliberate act → intentional torts. Careless act → negligence. Dangerous activity, animal or product → strict liability as well.
2. **One set of facts often supports several torts.** A store guard who grabs and locks up a shopper raises battery, false imprisonment and IIED, then the shopkeeper's privilege. A defective product raises strict liability, negligence, warranty and misrepresentation. On essays, you are rewarded for **every** plausible theory, each with its own conclusion.
3. **Defenses track the level of blame.**
   - Consent and privileges answer intentional torts.
   - Comparative fault and assumption of risk answer negligence and, partly, strict liability.
   - Π's negligence generally does **not** reduce damages for intentional torts.
4. **Damages close every claim.**
   - Intentional torts: nominal damages without harm, compensatory damages, and punitive damages for malice.
   - Negligence: actual injury required.
   - Death: survival (the estate's claim) and wrongful death (the family's claim).
5. **Liability for others** lets Π add a solvent Δ: the employer, the hiring company, the hospital.

## 5. The ideas that matter most
1. **Negligence = duty + breach + actual cause + proximate cause + damages.** Write each element as its own short paragraph. Most points on Torts essays are here, and half the MBE.
2. **Duty is the gatekeeper.** Ordinary conduct creates a duty to foreseeable people. Failure to act creates a duty only through a special relationship, a peril Δ created, or an undertaking. Land entrants, emotional harm and pure economic loss have special rules. California starts from the statutory general duty (Civ. Code § 1714) and the *Rowland* factors.
3. **Foreseeability decides proximate cause.** The harm must be of the type that made Δ careless. Foreseeable intervening acts, such as doctors, rescuers and foreseeable crimes, do not break the chain.
4. **Comparative fault splits the loss.**
   - MBE: pure comparative fault with joint and several liability [S-040].
   - California: pure comparative fault (*Li*), but each defendant pays only its share of **non-economic** damages (Prop 51).
5. **Products: three defects, four theories.** Manufacturing, design and warning defects under strict liability, plus negligence, warranty and misrepresentation. Every commercial seller, including the retailer, is strictly liable.
6. **Intent is narrow but powerful.** Purpose or substantial certainty; transferred intent; mistake is no defense to trespass. Privileges require reasonableness and proportionality. Never deadly force for property.
7. **The First Amendment reshapes defamation.** Public figures must prove actual malice; private figures at least negligence. Decide the plaintiff's status first.
8. **California twists are short add-ons**, worth one sentence each:
   - no land-entrant categories (*Rowland*);
   - *Thing* bystander test;
   - *Knight* primary assumption of risk for sports;
   - *Barker* burden shift in design defect;
   - Prop 51;
   - Evid. Code § 669 presumption;
   - dog-bite statute.

## 6. How it is tested
- **MBE:** 25 scored questions [S-007].
  - Short fact patterns test one element at a time: awareness in assault, the protected class in negligence per se, the type of harm in proximate cause, the defect type in products.
  - Answer under general principles and NCBE's defaults.
- **California essays:** 12 of 31 administrations (2012–2026).
  - Recent calls asked about negligence by several actors, products theories, nuisance and trespass with remedies, defamation with IIED, and sports injuries.
  - Torts also appears inside Remedies, Business Associations and Real Property questions.
  - General law governs unless the call says otherwise [S-077].

## 7. For a PRC-trained lawyer: common pitfalls
- **Separate the elements.** PRC analysis often merges fault and causation. US graders want duty, breach, actual cause and proximate cause discussed separately, each with facts.
- **Argue both sides.** Breach and proximate cause are jury questions; show why reasonable people could differ, then conclude.
- **Name the case or Restatement test when you know it** (*Palsgraf*, *Rowland*, *Thing*, *Barker*). This is how US lawyers signal the rule's source.
- **Use the plaintiff–defendant structure.** Organize by "P v. D", not by abstract topic.
