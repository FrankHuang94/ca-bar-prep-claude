# Torts — rule statements

**How to use this file.** Each block gives a rule you can write word for word on an essay, then its elements, exceptions, authority, the MBE/California split and a short example. `build_anki.py` turns each **Rule (write this)** paragraph into a card. The order follows [`outline.md`](outline.md) (NCBE numbering), with the California-only and damages rules at the end.

**Abbreviations.**
- **R2d** = Restatement (Second) of Torts. **R3d PL** = Restatement (Third) of Torts: Products Liability. **R3d PEH** = Restatement (Third) of Torts: Liability for Physical and Emotional Harm. Section numbers for the core sections (battery § 13, assault § 21, false imprisonment § 35, IIED § 46, trespass § 158, conversion § 222A, products § 402A, abnormally dangerous activities §§ 519–520, nuisance §§ 821B, 821D) are standard. Less common section numbers carry [VERIFY V-600].
- **Civ. Code / CCP / Gov. Code / Evid. Code** = California codes.
- **Π** = plaintiff; **Δ** = defendant.

**Essay default.** California Torts essays are answered under **general common-law principles** (the majority rule, the Restatement) unless the call asks for California law [S-077]. Where California law differs, add one sentence ("In California, …"). Graders reward correct California rules on comparative fault, duty (*Rowland*), bystander distress (*Thing*), assumption of risk (*Knight*) and design defect (*Barker*). See [`ca-distinctions.md`](ca-distinctions.md).

**MBE default.** Assume survival and wrongful-death actions are available, and that **joint and several liability with pure comparative negligence** applies unless the question says otherwise [S-040]. See [`mbe-rules.md`](mbe-rules.md).

## I.A Intentional torts: harms to the person and to property

### R-TRT-001 — Intent and transferred intent
**Rule (write this):** A defendant acts with intent if he acts with the purpose of bringing about the consequence or knows with substantial certainty that it will result. Under transferred intent, intent to commit assault, battery, false imprisonment, trespass to land or trespass to chattels against one person, or by one of those torts, satisfies the intent element when a different person or a different one of those torts results.

**Elements:** 1. Purpose, **or** 2. knowledge to a substantial certainty (R2d § 8A). 3. Transfer works between persons (aim at A, hit B) and between the five "trespass-family" torts (aim to frighten, actually touch).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Transferred intent does not extend to IIED (except under R2d § 46(2)) or to conversion. Motive is irrelevant. Children and mentally ill persons can form intent; the question is only whether they had the purpose or knowledge. Mistake about consent or ownership does not negate intent.

**Authority:** R2d §§ 8A, 16, 20, 32, 43 [VERIFY V-600]; *Garratt v. Dailey*, 46 Wash.2d 197 (1955) (five-year-old who pulled a chair away liable if he knew to a substantial certainty the woman would sit where the chair had been); *Talmage v. Smith*, 101 Mich. 370 (1894) (transferred intent).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** D throws a rock to scare A; it strikes B. D intended an assault on A, so the intent transfers and D is liable to B for battery.

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: close to 故意 (direct and indirect intent) in PRC tort law, but US "substantial certainty" is narrower than PRC indirect intent (放任) (approx.).

### R-TRT-002 — Battery
**Rule (write this):** A defendant is liable for battery if he acts intending to cause a harmful or offensive contact with the plaintiff's person, or an imminent apprehension of such contact, and a harmful or offensive contact with the plaintiff's person directly or indirectly results. A contact is offensive if it offends a reasonable sense of personal dignity.

**Elements:** 1. Act (volitional). 2. Intent (R-TRT-001). 3. Harmful or offensive contact. 4. With Π's person, including anything closely connected to it (clothing, a held plate, a car Π is sitting in). 5. Causation, direct or indirect (setting a trap counts).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Consent and privileges (R-TRT-009 to R-TRT-017). Π need not be aware of the contact when it happens (a kiss on a sleeping person). No actual damages are needed: nominal damages and, for malice, punitive damages are available. Under the eggshell-plaintiff rule Δ is liable for all resulting harm, even if unforeseeably severe.

**Authority:** R2d §§ 13, 18; *Vosburg v. Putney*, 80 Wis. 523 (1891); *Fisher v. Carrousel Motor Hotel*, 424 S.W.2d 627 (Tex. 1967) (snatching a plate from Π's hand).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. Courts split on whether Δ must intend the harm or offense ("dual intent") or only the contact; the MBE usually avoids the split. On an essay, state the majority wording above.

**Example:** At a party D grabs P's hat off her head as a joke. The hat is closely connected to P's person and the grab offends a reasonable sense of dignity, so this is a battery even though P was not hurt.

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: PRC law treats this as infringement of the right to bodily integrity/personality rights (身体权、人格权) with fault liability; US battery needs no physical injury at all (approx.).

### R-TRT-003 — Assault
**Rule (write this):** A defendant is liable for assault if he acts intending to cause a harmful or offensive contact, or an imminent apprehension of one, and the plaintiff is thereby put in reasonable apprehension of an imminent harmful or offensive contact. Words alone are usually not enough, but words can make conduct threatening or negate a threat.

**Elements:** 1. Act. 2. Intent (to contact or to cause apprehension). 3. Π's reasonable **apprehension** (awareness, not fear). 4. Of **imminent** contact. 5. Causation.

**Exceptions / defenses:** No assault if Π is unaware (a gun pointed at a sleeping person). Threats of future harm are not imminent. Conditional threats are assault if Δ has no right to impose the condition ("your money or your life"). Apparent ability is enough; actual ability is not required (an unloaded gun Π reasonably believes loaded). Words negating intent ("If you weren't my friend, I'd hit you") defeat the claim.

**Authority:** R2d §§ 21, 24, 31 [VERIFY V-600]; *I de S et ux v. W de S* (Assizes 1348) (hatchet swung at a wife).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** D swings a bat toward P's head and P ducks, so the bat misses. P was aware of an imminent contact: assault (and battery had it landed).

### R-TRT-004 — False imprisonment
**Rule (write this):** A defendant is liable for false imprisonment if he acts intending to confine the plaintiff within boundaries fixed by the defendant, his act directly or indirectly results in such a confinement, and the plaintiff is conscious of the confinement or is harmed by it.

**Elements:** 1. Intent to confine. 2. Confinement in a bounded area, for any appreciable time. 3. No reasonable, known means of escape. 4. Means: physical barriers, physical force, threats of force (to Π, family or property), invalid assertion of legal authority, duress such as withholding Π's property, or failure to release when under a duty to do so. 5. Π is aware of the confinement **or** harmed by it.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Moral pressure or shame alone is not confinement. A reasonable means of escape that Π knows of defeats the claim, unless using it would be dangerous, offensive (nakedness) or involve harm to property. Shopkeeper's privilege (R-TRT-013); lawful arrest (R-TRT-015); consent.

**Authority:** R2d §§ 35, 36, 38–42 [VERIFY V-600]; *Bird v. Jones*, 7 Q.B. 742 (1845) (blocking one route is not confinement); CCP § 340(c) (one-year limitations period in California).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A store guard locks P in a back room for two hours on a hunch, with no evidence, and threatens arrest if P leaves. That is confinement by force and an assertion of authority, and the shopkeeper's privilege fails because the detention was unreasonable.

### R-TRT-005 — Intentional infliction of emotional distress
**Rule (write this):** A defendant who by extreme and outrageous conduct intentionally or recklessly causes severe emotional distress to another is liable for that distress, and for any resulting bodily harm. Conduct is outrageous if it goes beyond all possible bounds of decency and is regarded as atrocious and utterly intolerable in a civilized community.

**Elements:** 1. Extreme and outrageous conduct. 2. Intent to cause severe distress, or reckless disregard of a high probability of it. 3. Causation. 4. **Severe** emotional distress (no physical injury needed).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Outrageousness is more likely where Δ abuses a position of power (employer, landlord, debt collector, police), knows of Π's special sensitivity, or repeats the conduct; common carriers and innkeepers are liable even for gross insults to patrons. Mere insults, indignities and threats are not enough. **Third parties:** Δ who directs outrageous conduct at a third person is liable to (a) a member of that person's immediate family who is present, if Δ knows of the presence, and (b) any other person present, if the distress causes bodily harm (R2d § 46(2)). **First Amendment:** a public figure cannot recover for a parody without a false statement of fact made with actual malice (*Hustler*); speech on a matter of public concern in a public place may be protected (*Snyder*).

**Authority:** R2d § 46; *State Rubbish Collectors Assn. v. Siliznoff* (1952) 38 Cal.2d 330; *Hughes v. Pair* (2009) 46 Cal.4th 1035 [S-721]; *Christensen v. Superior Court* (1991) 54 Cal.3d 868; *Hustler Magazine v. Falwell*, 485 U.S. 46 (1988); *Snyder v. Phelps*, 562 U.S. 443 (2011).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same elements. California requires the conduct to be directed at Π or to occur in the presence of a Π whom Δ knows is present (*Christensen*).

**Example:** A debt collector phones a widow nightly, falsely telling her she will be jailed and her children taken unless she pays her late husband's debt. She becomes unable to sleep or work. The abuse of power and repetition make the conduct outrageous, and her distress is severe.

### R-TRT-006 — Trespass to land
**Rule (write this):** A defendant is liable for trespass to land if he intentionally enters land in the possession of another, causes a thing or third person to enter it, remains on it after a privilege to be there ends, or fails to remove a thing he is under a duty to remove. The intent required is only the intent to enter the land; a mistaken belief that one has the right to enter is no defense, and no actual harm is required.

**Elements:** 1. Intent to be on (or send something onto) the particular land. 2. Physical invasion of Π's land, including its surface, the subsurface and the airspace within the immediate reaches of the land. 3. Π in possession (owner, tenant, adverse possessor). 4. Damages are presumed (nominal), with actual and punitive damages where proved.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Consent; necessity (R-TRT-016, R-TRT-017); recapture of chattels (R-TRT-013); legal authority. An unintentional entry is actionable only if negligent or abnormally dangerous **and** it causes actual harm (R2d § 165). Intangible intrusions (noise, odor, light) are normally nuisance, not trespass; in California, they are trespass only if they cause physical damage to the property or deposit particulate matter (*Wilson v. Interlake Steel*).

**Authority:** R2d §§ 158, 159, 164, 165 [VERIFY V-600]; *Wilson v. Interlake Steel Co.* (1982) 32 Cal.3d 229 [S-718]; damages: R-REM-020.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. On a California essay, cite *Wilson* when the "intrusion" is noise, vibration or smell.

**Example:** D builds a fence one foot over the line, honestly believing the land is his. He intended to build where he built, so the mistake is irrelevant: trespass, with ejectment or damages as the remedy.

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: compare 排除妨害 claims under the PRC property rules; in the US, trespass gives damages even without harm, and the mistaken good-faith entrant is still liable (approx.).

### R-TRT-007 — Trespass to chattels
**Rule (write this):** A defendant is liable for trespass to chattels if he intentionally dispossesses another of a chattel or uses or intermeddles with a chattel in the possession of another, and the dispossession or intermeddling causes actual harm: damage to the chattel, deprivation of its use for a substantial time, or harm to the possessor or to something in which he has a legal interest.

**Elements:** 1. Intent to act upon the chattel (mistake about ownership is no defense). 2. Dispossession or intermeddling. 3. Actual damages (no nominal recovery for mere intermeddling).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Consent; privileges. If the interference is serious enough to justify a forced sale, the claim is conversion (R-TRT-008). In California, unwanted electronic contact with a computer system is not trespass to chattels unless it damages or impairs the system's functioning (*Intel v. Hamidi*).

**Authority:** R2d §§ 217, 218; *Intel Corp. v. Hamidi* (2003) 30 Cal.4th 1342; damages: R-REM-022.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** D takes P's bicycle for a ten-minute ride and returns it undamaged. There is no actual harm, so P has no trespass-to-chattels claim; had D crashed it, P would recover the cost of repair.

### R-TRT-008 — Conversion
**Rule (write this):** A defendant is liable for conversion if he intentionally exercises dominion or control over a chattel that so seriously interferes with the right of another to control it that the defendant may justly be required to pay the other the chattel's full value. Seriousness is measured by the extent and duration of the control, the defendant's intent to assert a right inconsistent with the owner's, his good or bad faith, and the harm done.

**Elements:** 1. Intent to exercise control (good faith and mistake are no defense). 2. Serious interference: acquiring (theft, buying from a thief), transferring, withholding after demand, substantially changing, severely damaging, destroying or misusing the chattel. 3. Π's right to possession. 4. Damages: full market value at the time of conversion (a forced sale), with interest; or Π may sue to recover the chattel (replevin).

**Exceptions / defenses:** A good-faith purchaser from a thief is a converter; one who buys from a person with voidable title, or from a merchant entrusted with goods, may take good title (UCC § 2-403). Only tangible chattels and documents merging intangible rights (stock certificates, checks) are traditionally convertible. If Δ offers to return the chattel, damages may be reduced but the claim remains.

**Authority:** R2d §§ 222A, 223, 229 [VERIFY V-600]; UCC § 2-403; Civ. Code § 3336 (conversion damages) [S-468]; damages: R-REM-021.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** D, a valet, takes P's car on a 300-mile joyride and crashes it. The extent and duration of use and the damage make this conversion; P may recover the car's full value at the time D took it.

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: the forced-sale idea has no direct PRC counterpart; PRC law would order return of the original thing (返还原物) plus compensation (approx.).

## I.B Defenses to intentional torts: consent and privileges

### R-TRT-009 — Consent
**Rule (write this):** Consent is a complete defense to an intentional tort if the plaintiff, having capacity, actually or apparently consented to the defendant's conduct and the defendant stayed within the scope of that consent. Consent may be express or implied from custom, the plaintiff's conduct, or law in an emergency, but consent obtained by fraud about the essential nature of the act, or by duress, is ineffective.

**Elements:** 1. Express consent, or implied consent: **apparent** consent (words or conduct a reasonable person would read as consent, *O'Brien*), custom (contact sports, crowded places), or consent implied by law (an unconscious emergency patient when a reasonable person would consent and no contrary wish is known). 2. Capacity (not a young child, an incompetent, or a severely intoxicated person). 3. Within scope: same act, same body part, same actor (*Mohr*: consent to surgery on one ear is not consent to the other). 4. Not vitiated by fraud going to the essential nature of the invasion (not a collateral matter) or by duress.

**Exceptions / defenses:** **Sports:** participants consent to contacts within the rules and customs of the game, not to intentional or reckless blows far outside them (*Hackbart*). **Consent to a crime:** the majority (R2d § 892C) treats consent as effective even if the act is illegal (mutual combat), but consent is ineffective where a statute protects a class against its own consent (statutory rape). A minority voids consent to a crime. Medical: lack of **informed** consent is usually negligence (R-TRT-028), but a substantially different procedure is battery.

**Authority:** R2d §§ 892, 892A, 892B, 892C [VERIFY V-600]; *O'Brien v. Cunard S.S. Co.*, 154 Mass. 272 (1891); *Mohr v. Williams*, 95 Minn. 261 (1905); *Hackbart v. Cincinnati Bengals, Inc.*, 601 F.2d 516 (10th Cir. 1979).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. California sports cases are usually framed as primary assumption of risk (R-TRT-073), with the same "inherent risks" idea.

**Example:** P, a football player, is tackled hard during play (consented to) and then punched in the back of the head after the whistle (outside the scope). The punch is a battery.

### R-TRT-010 — Self-defense
**Rule (write this):** A person is privileged to use reasonable force to defend himself against force he reasonably believes is about to be used against him. Deadly force is privileged only if he reasonably believes he faces death or serious bodily harm. A reasonable mistake does not destroy the privilege.

**Elements:** 1. Reasonable belief (objective) of an imminent attack. 2. Force proportional to the threat. 3. Deadly force only against a threat of death or serious bodily harm. 4. Privilege ends when the threat ends (no retaliation).

**Exceptions / defenses:** **Retreat:** no duty to retreat before using nondeadly force. Under R2d § 65, a person must retreat before using deadly force if he can do so in complete safety, except in his own dwelling; many states reject any retreat duty. An **initial aggressor** has no privilege unless he withdraws or the other side escalates to deadly force. **Bystanders:** an actor who injures a third person while defending himself is not liable unless he was negligent toward that person (R2d § 75).

**Authority:** R2d §§ 63, 65, 75 [VERIFY V-600]; Civ. Code § 50 (any necessary force may be used to protect oneself and listed family members, guests and others) [VERIFY V-609].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. The MBE favors "reasonable force, reasonable belief" and does not expect a retreat duty for nondeadly force.

**Example:** A larger man lunges at D with fists raised; D punches him once. That is privileged. If D had shot him, the force would be disproportionate unless D reasonably feared serious bodily harm.

### R-TRT-011 — Defense of others
**Rule (write this):** A person may use reasonable force to defend a third person when he reasonably believes that the third person would be privileged to use such force in self-defense. Under the majority rule a reasonable mistake about the need to intervene is protected.

**Elements:** 1. Reasonable belief that the third person faces imminent unprivileged force. 2. The third person would be privileged to use the same force. 3. Proportional force.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Minority ("steps into the shoes"): the intervenor has no privilege if the person defended was not actually privileged (e.g., was the aggressor or was being lawfully arrested). Civ. Code § 50 extends the privilege to listed relatives, wards, employees, employers and guests [VERIFY V-609].

**Authority:** R2d § 76 [VERIFY V-600]; Civ. Code § 50 [VERIFY V-609].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule; use the majority (reasonable-mistake) version.

**Example:** D sees a plain-clothes officer wrestling a teenager to the ground and pulls the officer off. If D reasonably believed the teenager was being mugged, D is privileged under the majority rule.

### R-TRT-012 — Defense of property
**Rule (write this):** A possessor may use reasonable, nondeadly force to prevent or end another's intrusion on his land or chattels if he reasonably believes the intrusion is occurring and that force is necessary, after first asking the intruder to stop unless a request would be futile or dangerous. Deadly force, including a deadly mechanical device, is never privileged to protect property alone.

**Elements:** 1. Reasonable belief in an intrusion. 2. Request to desist first, unless futile or dangerous. 3. Reasonable, nondeadly force. 4. A device (spring gun, electrified fence) is privileged only to the extent the possessor could have used the same force in person, and only with warning where appropriate.

**Exceptions / defenses:** **Mistake:** the privilege is lost if the "intruder" was actually privileged to enter (e.g., under necessity, R-TRT-016 and R-TRT-017), unless the intruder caused the mistake. If the intruder threatens the occupants' safety, self-defense rules apply and deadly force may be justified.

**Authority:** R2d §§ 77, 79, 85 [VERIFY V-600]; *Katko v. Briney*, 183 N.W.2d 657 (Iowa 1971) (spring gun in an unoccupied farmhouse: liable).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A farmer rigs a shotgun to fire when a shed door opens; a burglar is wounded. The farmer could not have shot the burglar in person to protect property, so the device is unprivileged and the farmer is liable for battery.

### R-TRT-013 — Recapture of chattels; the shopkeeper's privilege
**Rule (write this):** A person whose chattel was wrongfully taken may use reasonable, nondeadly force to recapture it in fresh pursuit, after a demand for return unless a demand would be futile or dangerous. A shopkeeper who reasonably believes a person has stolen merchandise may detain that person for a reasonable time and in a reasonable manner, on or near the premises, to investigate.

**Elements:** **Recapture:** 1. Wrongful taking (not a mere failure to return, as by a lessee). 2. Fresh pursuit. 3. Demand first. 4. Reasonable force. 5. Entry onto the wrongdoer's land is privileged; entry onto an innocent person's land requires a reasonable time and manner, and the entrant pays for actual damage. **Shopkeeper:** 1. Reasonable belief of theft. 2. Detention on or near the premises. 3. Reasonable manner (no excessive force). 4. Reasonable time (investigation only).

**Exceptions / defenses:** A mistake about the wrongful taking defeats recapture (the actor takes the risk). The shopkeeper's privilege protects a reasonable mistake. In California a merchant may detain a person for a reasonable time with probable cause to believe he is attempting to take merchandise unlawfully (Pen. Code § 490.5(f)) [VERIFY V-609].

**Authority:** R2d §§ 100–106, 120A [VERIFY V-600]; Pen. Code § 490.5(f) [VERIFY V-609].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A clerk sees P slip a watch into her bag, stops her at the door and holds her in the office for fifteen minutes until a manager checks the video. That is privileged even if the video shows P put the watch back.

### R-TRT-014 — Parental discipline
**Rule (write this):** A parent may use reasonable force, and impose reasonable confinement, that he reasonably believes necessary for the child's proper control, training or education. Persons in loco parentis, such as teachers, share a similar but narrower privilege, subject to statutes and school rules.

**Elements:** 1. Δ is a parent (or one with parental authority). 2. Purpose: control, training or education. 3. Reasonableness: the child's age, sex and condition, the nature of the offense, whether the force was needed, and whether it was disproportionate or degrading.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Excessive or malicious force is battery. California prohibits corporal punishment of pupils in public schools (Ed. Code § 49001) [VERIFY V-609].

**Authority:** R2d §§ 147, 150, 151 [VERIFY V-600].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule; rarely tested.

**Example:** A father briefly grounds his 12-year-old in her room for lying: privileged. Locking her in a closet for two days is false imprisonment.

### R-TRT-015 — Protection of public interests: arrest and authority of law
**Rule (write this):** Officers and private persons are privileged to arrest, and to use reasonable force to do so, within limits that depend on the offense. An officer may arrest without a warrant for a felony on reasonable grounds, but a private person is protected only if the felony was in fact committed and he reasonably believed the arrestee committed it; either may arrest for a misdemeanor amounting to a breach of the peace committed in his presence.

**Elements:** 1. Felony: officer — reasonable belief that a felony was committed and that the arrestee did it; private citizen — felony actually committed plus reasonable belief. 2. Misdemeanor: breach of the peace in the arrester's presence. 3. With a warrant valid on its face, the officer is privileged even if the warrant was improperly issued. 4. Reasonable force; deadly force only to prevent the escape of a felon who threatens death or serious bodily harm.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Public officials have immunity for discretionary acts (R-TRT-075). California allows a private person to arrest for a public offense committed or attempted in his presence, or for a felony actually committed when he has reasonable cause to believe the arrestee committed it (Pen. Code § 837).

**Authority:** R2d §§ 119, 121, 131 [VERIFY V-600]; Pen. Code § 837; *Tennessee v. Garner*, 471 U.S. 1 (1985) (deadly force against a fleeing suspect under the Fourth Amendment).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A shopper tackles a man he wrongly believes robbed a bank; no robbery occurred. A private person's mistake about whether a felony happened is not protected, so the shopper is liable for battery and false imprisonment.

### R-TRT-016 — Public necessity
**Rule (write this):** A person who enters or damages another's property is completely privileged if he reasonably believes the act is necessary to avert an imminent public disaster, such as the spread of fire or disease. Under the majority rule he pays nothing for the damage.

**Elements:** 1. Imminent threat to the community or many people. 2. Reasonable belief that the act is necessary. 3. Reasonable action.

**Exceptions / defenses:** If the action is unreasonable, there is no privilege. Government takings law may separately require compensation in some settings (Constitutional Law).

**Authority:** R2d §§ 196, 262; *Surocco v. Geary* (1853) 3 Cal. 69 (blowing up a house to stop a city fire).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** Firefighters bulldoze P's barn to create a firebreak that saves a town. Public necessity: no liability.

### R-TRT-017 — Private necessity (an incomplete privilege)
**Rule (write this):** A person may enter or use another's property when he reasonably believes it necessary to protect himself, his property or a few others from serious harm. The privilege is incomplete: he is not a trespasser, and the owner may not expel him, but he must pay for any actual damage he causes.

**Elements:** 1. Threat of serious harm to Δ or a limited group. 2. Reasonable belief that entry or use is necessary. 3. Liability for actual damage (no nominal or punitive damages).

**Exceptions / defenses:** An owner who resists a privileged entrant is liable for the resulting harm (*Ploof*). The entrant must leave when the emergency ends.

**Authority:** R2d §§ 197, 263 [VERIFY V-600]; *Ploof v. Putnam*, 81 Vt. 471 (1908); *Vincent v. Lake Erie Transp. Co.*, 109 Minn. 456 (1910).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. "Incomplete privilege" (NCBE I.B.2.f) means exactly this: privileged entry, but liability for actual harm.

**Example:** In a sudden storm a boater ties up at P's dock and the waves smash the boat into it, causing $5,000 of damage. The boater is privileged to stay, but owes $5,000. If P had cut the boat loose, P would be liable for the loss of the boat.

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: like 紧急避险 under the PRC Civil Code, where the person who caused the danger, or the beneficiary, bears appropriate compensation; the US private-necessity actor pays actual damage (approx.).

## II.A Negligence: duty

### R-TRT-018 — Negligence: the prima facie case
**Rule (write this):** To recover for negligence, the plaintiff must prove that the defendant owed a duty to conform to a standard of care, breached that duty, and that the breach was an actual and proximate cause of the plaintiff's actual damages. Each element must be analyzed separately for each plaintiff against each defendant.

**Elements:** 1. Duty. 2. Breach (failure to meet the standard of care). 3. Actual (factual) cause. 4. Proximate (legal) cause. 5. Damages: actual personal injury or property damage (no nominal damages; purely economic or emotional loss only under special rules, R-TRT-054 to R-TRT-061).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Contributory or comparative negligence, assumption of risk, immunities, limitations periods (R-TRT-069 to R-TRT-076).

**Authority:** R3d PEH §§ 6, 7; Civ. Code § 1714(a).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. On a California essay, give each element its own sub-heading under each Π v. Δ pairing.

**Example:** D runs a red light and hits P's car, breaking P's arm. D owed other drivers reasonable care, breached it, caused the crash in fact and foreseeably, and P has physical injuries.

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: like general fault liability (过错责任) under the PRC Civil Code: fault, harm, causation; US law splits causation into two distinct elements and treats duty as a separate question of law (approx.).

### R-TRT-019 — Duty to foreseeable plaintiffs (Palsgraf)
**Rule (write this):** A defendant owes a duty of reasonable care to all persons who are foreseeably endangered by his conduct. Under the majority (Cardozo) view, a plaintiff outside the zone of foreseeable danger is owed no duty; under the minority (Andrews) view, a defendant who breaches a duty to anyone owes a duty to everyone injured as a result, subject to proximate cause.

**Elements:** 1. Δ's conduct created a foreseeable risk of physical harm. 2. Π was within the foreseeable zone of danger (majority). 3. Special plaintiffs: rescuers are foreseeable (R-TRT-024); unborn children may recover for prenatal injuries if born alive.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Modern law (R3d PEH § 7) frames duty as a general duty of reasonable care whenever conduct creates a risk of physical harm, and moves foreseeability questions into breach and scope of liability.

**Authority:** *Palsgraf v. Long Island R.R. Co.*, 248 N.Y. 339 (1928); R3d PEH § 7.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. The MBE treats the Cardozo view as the majority. In California, foreseeability is the first *Rowland* factor (R-TRT-020).

**Example:** A guard negligently pushes a passenger carrying an unmarked package of fireworks; it explodes and scales fall on P at the far end of the platform. Under Cardozo, P was outside the zone of foreseeable danger and no duty was owed.

### R-TRT-020 — California: general duty (Civ. Code § 1714) and the Rowland factors
**Rule (write this):** In California, everyone is responsible for injury caused to another by his want of ordinary care, and courts depart from this general duty only where clearly supported by public policy, weighing the Rowland factors. The analysis is made at a broad categorical level, not on the specific facts of the case.

**Elements:** *Rowland* factors: 1. foreseeability of harm to Π; 2. degree of certainty that Π suffered injury; 3. closeness of the connection between Δ's conduct and the injury; 4. moral blame attached to Δ's conduct; 5. policy of preventing future harm; 6. extent of the burden to Δ and consequences to the community of imposing a duty; 7. availability, cost and prevalence of insurance.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Recent no-duty holdings: employers owe no duty to employees' household members to prevent take-home COVID-19 (*Kuciemba*, policy factors; the derivative-injury doctrine did not bar the spouse's claim) [S-700]; a drug maker owes no duty to users of a nondefective drug in deciding whether and when to commercialize a safer alternative (*Gilead Tenofovir Cases*, Aug. 2026) [S-709] [VERIFY V-603]. A duty **was** recognized to household members exposed to take-home asbestos (*Kesner*).

**Authority:** Civ. Code § 1714(a); *Rowland v. Christian* (1968) 69 Cal.2d 108; *Cabral v. Ralphs Grocery Co.* (2011) 51 Cal.4th 764; *Kesner v. Superior Court* (2016) 1 Cal.5th 1132; *Kuciemba v. Victory Woodworks, Inc.* (2023) 14 Cal.5th 993 [S-700].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: general duty to foreseeable plaintiffs (R-TRT-019). California essay: state § 1714 as the general rule; run the *Rowland* factors only where Δ argues for a categorical exception.

**Example:** A spouse claims she caught a virus that her husband brought home from a negligent workplace. Under *Kuciemba*, the burden on employers and the community of a duty to every household member outweighs foreseeability, so no duty is owed.

### R-TRT-021 — No general duty to act; exceptions
**Rule (write this):** There is generally no duty to take affirmative action to aid or protect another, however easy the rescue. A duty to act arises where the defendant has a special relationship with the plaintiff, created the peril, voluntarily undertook to help, or is required to act by statute or contract; one who undertakes a rescue must act with reasonable care and is liable if he leaves the victim worse off.

**Elements:** Exceptions: 1. **Special relationship** with Π: common carrier–passenger, innkeeper–guest, possessor of land open to the public–invitee, custodian–person in custody (jailer, hospital), employer–employee in peril, school–student. 2. **Δ created the peril**, even innocently. 3. **Voluntary undertaking**: Δ is liable if his failure to use reasonable care increased the risk or Π (or another) relied on the undertaking (R2d §§ 323, 324A). 4. Statute or contract.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Misfeasance (affirmative risky conduct) is not "failure to act"; a driver must brake. Good Samaritan statutes protect volunteers; California protects a person who renders emergency care at the scene in good faith and not for compensation, except for gross negligence or willful misconduct (Health & Saf. Code § 1799.102) [VERIFY V-609].

**Authority:** R2d §§ 314, 314A, 321, 323, 324A [VERIFY V-600]; *Yania v. Bigan*, 397 Pa. 316 (1959); *Regents of University of California v. Superior Court* (2018) 4 Cal.5th 607 (college–student special relationship).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A stranger watches a toddler drown in a shallow pond and does nothing: no liability. If he had waded in, then given up and stopped others from helping, he would be liable for his negligent undertaking.

### R-TRT-022 — Duty to protect from or control third persons
**Rule (write this):** A defendant generally has no duty to control the conduct of a third person or to protect the plaintiff from it. A duty arises when the defendant has a special relationship either with the plaintiff that obligates him to protect the plaintiff, or with the third person that obligates him to control that person's conduct, and the harm is foreseeable.

**Elements:** Relationships with the **dangerous person**: parent–minor child (knowledge of a specific dangerous tendency and ability to control), employer–employee, one who takes charge of a person with dangerous propensities (psychiatric hospital, jail), possessor of land–licensee using it. Relationships with the **victim**: see R-TRT-021 (carrier, innkeeper, landowner open to the public, school, custodian).

**Exceptions / defenses:** **California two-step:** (1) ask whether a special relationship or other basis creates a duty; (2) if so, consult the *Rowland* factors to decide whether to limit that duty (*Brown v. USA Taekwondo*). Premises owners' duty to provide security against crime turns on foreseeability (R-TRT-053).

**Authority:** R2d §§ 315, 316, 317, 319 [VERIFY V-600]; *Brown v. USA Taekwondo* (2021) 11 Cal.5th 204; *Regents of University of California v. Superior Court* (2018) 4 Cal.5th 607.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same core rule. On a California essay, use the *Brown* two-step.

**Example:** A hospital moves its only security guard from a ward where a visitor has repeatedly threatened a patient, and the visitor attacks the patient. The hospital–patient custodial relationship creates a duty to protect, and *Rowland* does not limit it, because the attack was foreseeable and a guard was easy to provide.

### R-TRT-023 — Psychotherapist's duty to protect (Tarasoff)
**Rule (write this):** A psychotherapist who knows, or under professional standards should know, that a patient presents a serious danger of violence to a reasonably identifiable victim must use reasonable care to protect that victim. In California, liability arises only if the patient communicated a serious threat of physical violence against a reasonably identifiable victim, and the duty is discharged by reasonable efforts to communicate the threat to the victim and to a law enforcement agency.

**Elements:** 1. Therapist–patient relationship. 2. Serious threat of physical violence communicated by the patient (CA). 3. Reasonably identifiable victim. 4. Breach: no reasonable protective steps. 5. Causation and damages.

**Exceptions / defenses:** The statutory safe harbor (warning the victim and police) protects the therapist from liability. Confidentiality yields to the duty.

**Authority:** *Tarasoff v. Regents of University of California* (1976) 17 Cal.3d 425; Civ. Code § 43.92.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: common-law *Tarasoff* duty (reasonable care to protect a foreseeable victim). California essay: add § 43.92's "communicated threat" requirement and safe harbor.

**Example:** A patient tells his therapist he will kill his ex-girlfriend, by name, this weekend. The therapist does nothing and the patient attacks her. The therapist is liable; calling her and the police would have discharged the duty.

### R-TRT-024 — Rescuers and the firefighter's rule
**Rule (write this):** Danger invites rescue, so a defendant who negligently imperils a person or himself owes a duty to a rescuer who is injured making a reasonable rescue attempt. Under the firefighter's rule, however, a professional rescuer such as a police officer or firefighter cannot recover from a person whose negligence caused the emergency for injuries from the risks inherent in responding to it.

**Elements:** **Rescue doctrine:** 1. Δ negligently created peril. 2. Π attempted a rescue that was not reckless. 3. Π's injury. **Firefighter's rule:** 1. Π is a public safety professional. 2. Injured by a risk inherent in the duty. 3. Caused by the negligence that summoned Π.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A rescuer's own negligence reduces recovery under comparative fault. The firefighter's rule does not bar claims for independent negligence after Π arrives, hidden dangers, or intentional misconduct. In California, it does not apply to private safety employees (*Neighbarger*) [S-707] [VERIFY V-611], and Civ. Code § 1714.9 preserves claims for conduct after Δ knew of the responder's presence, statutory violations that were not the cause of the call, intentional injury and arson [S-722].

**Authority:** *Wagner v. International Ry. Co.*, 232 N.Y. 176 (1921); *Neighbarger v. Irwin Industries, Inc.* (1994) 8 Cal.4th 532 [S-707]; Civ. Code § 1714.9 [S-722].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule; California adds the § 1714.9 exceptions.

**Example:** D negligently starts a kitchen fire; a neighbor who runs in to carry out D's child is burned. D is liable to the neighbor. A city firefighter burned in the same fire is barred.

## II.B Negligence: the standard of care

### R-TRT-025 — The reasonably prudent person
**Rule (write this):** The standard of care is that of a reasonably prudent person under the same or similar circumstances. The standard is objective: the defendant's own good faith or low intelligence is no excuse, but a physical disability is taken into account and a defendant's superior knowledge or skill raises the standard.

**Elements:** 1. Objective standard (*Vaughan v. Menlove*). 2. Physical disability: a reasonable person with that disability (blind person using a cane). 3. Mental illness or low intelligence: no allowance (adult held to the normal standard). 4. Voluntary intoxication: no allowance. 5. Superior knowledge or skill must be used.

**Exceptions / defenses:** **Sudden incapacity:** a driver who suffers an unforeseeable heart attack or seizure is not negligent; one who knew of the risk may be negligent for driving at all. Emergency doctrine (R-TRT-030).

**Authority:** *Vaughan v. Menlove*, 132 Eng. Rep. 490 (C.P. 1837); R2d §§ 283, 283B, 283C [VERIFY V-600]; R3d PEH §§ 3, 11, 12.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A driver with a known seizure disorder who skipped his medication has a seizure and crashes. The seizure is not negligence, but driving with a known unmanaged risk is.

### R-TRT-026 — Children
**Rule (write this):** A child is held to the standard of a reasonable child of like age, intelligence and experience. A child engaged in an adult activity, such as driving a car or a motorboat, is held to the adult standard.

**Elements:** 1. Subjective-objective test: like age, intelligence, experience. 2. Adult activity → adult standard (activities normally done only by adults, often licensed). 3. Very young children (commonly under about 5) may be held incapable of negligence.

**Exceptions / defenses:** The same rules apply to a child's contributory negligence. Parents are not vicariously liable for a child's negligence at common law (R-TRT-066).

**Authority:** R2d § 283A; R3d PEH § 10.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A 14-year-old drives a jet ski into a swimmer. Operating a powered watercraft is an adult activity, so the adult standard applies.

### R-TRT-027 — Professionals and specialists
**Rule (write this):** A professional must exercise the knowledge and skill of a member of the profession in good standing, and a specialist must meet the standard of specialists in the field. In medical cases the standard is usually national; the customs of the profession set the standard and must ordinarily be proven by expert testimony.

**Elements:** 1. Professional relationship. 2. Standard: skill and learning commonly possessed by members of the profession (or specialty) in good standing, in similar communities (locality rule is now largely replaced by a national standard, especially for specialists). 3. Expert testimony, unless the negligence is obvious to a layperson (a sponge left inside the patient). 4. Causation and damages.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A professional's honest error of judgment among accepted methods is not negligence. Legal malpractice requires a "case within a case" to prove causation. In California, medical negligence damages are subject to MICRA (R-TRT-115).

**Authority:** R2d § 299A [VERIFY V-600]; CCP § 340.5 (medical negligence limitations period).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A general practitioner who performs a procedure normally done by specialists is held to the specialist's standard.

### R-TRT-028 — Informed consent
**Rule (write this):** A physician must disclose the material risks of a proposed treatment, and its alternatives, that a reasonable patient would consider significant in deciding whether to undergo it. The patient recovers if the undisclosed risk materialized and a reasonable person in the patient's position, properly informed, would have declined the treatment.

**Elements:** 1. Duty to disclose material information (patient-centered standard, majority and California). 2. Nondisclosure. 3. Undisclosed risk materialized. 4. Causation: objective test (a reasonable patient would have refused). 5. Damages.

**Exceptions / defenses:** No duty to disclose risks that are commonly known, remote, or already known to the patient; emergency; patient's waiver; therapeutic privilege (rare). A minority uses a professional-custom standard. In California, a physician must also disclose the risks of **refusing** a recommended test (*Truman*). Treatment that differs substantially from the consented procedure is battery, not negligence.

**Authority:** *Cobbs v. Grant* (1972) 8 Cal.3d 229; *Truman v. Thomas* (1980) 27 Cal.3d 285; *Canterbury v. Spence*, 464 F.2d 772 (D.C. Cir. 1972).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule (patient standard, objective causation).

**Example:** A surgeon does not mention a 5% risk of permanent nerve damage from an elective procedure. Damage occurs, and a reasonable patient would have chosen physical therapy instead. The surgeon is liable even though the operation was performed skillfully.

### R-TRT-029 — Common carriers and innkeepers
**Rule (write this):** Common carriers and innkeepers owe their passengers and guests the highest degree of care consistent with the practical operation of their business, and are liable for slight negligence. They also have an affirmative duty to protect passengers and guests from foreseeable harm, including harm from third persons.

**Elements:** 1. Δ is a common carrier (holds itself out to the public for hire) or innkeeper. 2. Π is a passenger or guest. 3. Breach of the heightened standard. 4. Causation and damages.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Many modern courts and R3d PEH apply ordinary reasonable care, with the circumstances (passengers' dependence) raising what reasonable care requires. Private carriers and social hosts owe ordinary care.

**Authority:** Civ. Code § 2100 (a carrier of persons for reward must use the utmost care and diligence for their safe carriage); R2d § 314A.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: the traditional "highest degree of care" rule still appears; read the call. California essay: cite § 2100.

**Example:** A bus driver brakes hard to avoid a squirrel, throwing a standing passenger to the floor. Under the heightened standard, even a modest lapse is breach.

### R-TRT-030 — Emergency doctrine
**Rule (write this):** A person confronted with a sudden emergency that he did not create is judged by what a reasonable person would do in the same emergency; a choice that turns out badly is not negligent if it was reasonable under the pressure of the moment.

**Elements:** 1. Sudden and unexpected emergency. 2. Not created by Δ's own negligence. 3. Δ's response was reasonable in the circumstances.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Δ who caused the emergency cannot invoke it. The doctrine is not a separate standard, only an application of reasonable care.

**Authority:** R3d PEH § 9.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A child darts into the road; D swerves and clips a parked car rather than hit the child. D's choice was reasonable in the emergency.

### R-TRT-031 — Breach and the risk-utility analysis
**Rule (write this):** A defendant breaches the duty of care when his conduct falls below what a reasonable person would do in the circumstances. Courts weigh the foreseeable probability and severity of harm against the burden of precautions that would have avoided it; failing to take a precaution whose burden is less than the probability multiplied by the gravity of the harm is negligent.

**Elements:** 1. Probability of harm (P). 2. Gravity of harm (L). 3. Burden of adequate precautions (B), including lost social value of the activity. 4. Negligence if B < P × L.

**Exceptions / defenses:** The jury decides breach except where reasonable minds could not differ.

**Authority:** *United States v. Carroll Towing Co.*, 159 F.2d 169 (2d Cir. 1947); R3d PEH § 3.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. On an essay, argue both sides: Π lists cheap precautions; Δ points to cost, low probability and social utility.

**Example:** A store lets a spilled-yogurt puddle sit for an hour at a busy aisle. Mopping costs almost nothing compared to the foreseeable risk of serious falls: breach.

### R-TRT-032 — Custom
**Rule (write this):** Evidence that the defendant complied with, or departed from, the custom of his trade or community is admissible on breach but is not conclusive, because a whole industry may lag behind reasonable precautions. In professional malpractice, by contrast, the profession's custom generally sets the standard of care.

**Elements:** 1. Proof of the custom. 2. Compliance → evidence of due care. 3. Departure → evidence of negligence.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Medical and other professional cases (R-TRT-027).

**Authority:** *The T.J. Hooper*, 60 F.2d 737 (2d Cir. 1932); R3d PEH § 13.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** No tugboat in 1930 carried radios, but radios were cheap and would have warned of a storm. Following the industry custom did not excuse the tug owner.

### R-TRT-033 — Negligence per se (statutory standard of care)
**Rule (write this):** A statute that imposes a criminal or regulatory duty may set the standard of care if it was designed to protect the class of persons that includes the plaintiff against the type of harm the plaintiff suffered. Under the majority rule an unexcused violation is negligence per se, establishing duty and breach; the plaintiff must still prove causation and damages.

**Elements:** 1. Statute clearly defines required conduct. 2. Π within the protected class. 3. Harm of the type the statute was meant to prevent. 4. Violation without excuse. 5. Causation and damages (proven separately).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Excuse (R-TRT-034). Licensing statutes usually do not set a standard (an unlicensed driver is not negligent for that reason alone). Minority: violation is only evidence of negligence. **California:** violation raises a **presumption** of negligence when the four statutory conditions are met; Δ rebuts by showing he did what a reasonably prudent person who desired to comply would have done (Evid. Code § 669).

**Authority:** R2d §§ 286, 288B [VERIFY V-600]; R3d PEH § 14; *Martin v. Herzog*, 228 N.Y. 164 (1920); Evid. Code § 669.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: negligence per se (majority). California essay: cite Evid. Code § 669's presumption. A past California question turned on a newly enacted texting-while-driving statute (Jul 2017 Q5), which invites this analysis [VERIFY V-071].

**Example:** A statute bars texting while driving to prevent crashes. D, texting, rear-ends P. P is in the protected class (road users) and suffered the targeted harm (collision): negligence per se (in California, a presumption of negligence).

### R-TRT-034 — Excused violations; compliance with a statute
**Rule (write this):** A statutory violation is excused if compliance was impossible or would have created a greater risk of harm, the actor was incapacitated or reasonably unaware of the facts requiring compliance, or he faced an emergency not of his own making. Conversely, compliance with a statute is evidence of due care but does not prove it, since the statute sets only a minimum.

**Elements:** Excuses (R2d § 288A): 1. Incapacity. 2. Neither knew nor should have known of the occasion for compliance. 3. Unable to comply after reasonable diligence. 4. Emergency not caused by Δ. 5. Compliance would involve greater risk to the actor or others.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Regulatory compliance can be a stronger defense in some product cases (preemption, R-TRT-087).

**Authority:** R2d §§ 288A, 288C [VERIFY V-600]; *Tedla v. Ellman*, 280 N.Y. 124 (1939).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A statute requires pedestrians to walk facing traffic, but heavy traffic makes that side dangerous; P walks on the other side and is hit. The violation is excused, so it is not contributory negligence per se.

## II.C Problems relating to proof of fault

### R-TRT-035 — Res ipsa loquitur
**Rule (write this):** Under res ipsa loquitur, the jury may infer negligence when the accident is of a kind that ordinarily does not occur without negligence and the instrumentality was in the defendant's exclusive control, so that the negligence is probably attributable to the defendant. The plaintiff then survives a directed verdict even without direct evidence of what the defendant did wrong.

**Elements:** 1. Accident normally does not happen without negligence. 2. Δ's exclusive control of the instrumentality (or other responsible causes are sufficiently eliminated). 3. Traditionally, Π did not contribute (largely absorbed by comparative fault).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Effect (majority): a permissible inference; the jury may still find for Δ. **California:** res ipsa creates a presumption affecting the burden of producing evidence (Evid. Code § 646). **Multiple defendants:** where an unconscious patient is injured during surgery, all those with control may be required to explain (*Ybarra*).

**Authority:** *Byrne v. Boadle*, 159 Eng. Rep. 299 (Ex. 1863); *Ybarra v. Spangard* (1944) 25 Cal.2d 486; *Escola v. Coca Cola Bottling Co.* (1944) 24 Cal.2d 453; R2d § 328D; Evid. Code § 646.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: inference; Π gets to the jury. California essay: mention § 646's presumption.

**Example:** A barrel rolls out of D's warehouse window onto P on the sidewalk. Barrels don't fall from windows without negligence, and D controlled the warehouse: res ipsa lets the jury infer D's negligence.

### R-TRT-036 — Proof of breach: circumstantial evidence and notice
**Rule (write this):** Breach may be proved by circumstantial evidence. For a dangerous condition the defendant did not create, the plaintiff must show the defendant knew of it (actual notice) or that it existed long enough that a reasonable inspection would have discovered and corrected it (constructive notice).

**Elements:** 1. Dangerous condition. 2. Δ created it, or had actual or constructive notice. 3. Failure to remedy or warn within a reasonable time.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Some courts excuse notice where the owner's mode of operation makes the hazard foreseeable (self-service produce bins). Without evidence of how long the condition existed, Π fails.

**Authority:** R3d PEH § 51 cmt. [VERIFY V-600]; *Ortega v. Kmart Corp.* (2001) 26 Cal.4th 1200 (constructive notice from failure to inspect within a reasonable time) [VERIFY V-611].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** P slips on a banana peel in D's store. A black, gritty peel suggests it lay there long enough to be found; a fresh yellow one suggests no notice.

## II.D Causation and apportionment

### R-TRT-037 — Actual cause: the but-for test
**Rule (write this):** The defendant's breach is an actual cause of the plaintiff's harm if the harm would not have occurred but for the breach. The plaintiff must prove this by a preponderance of the evidence.

**Elements:** 1. Identify the negligent aspect of Δ's conduct. 2. Ask: without that breach, would the harm still have happened? 3. If not, but-for causation exists.

**Exceptions / defenses:** If the harm would have occurred anyway (the warning would not have been read; the guardrail would not have held), causation fails. Special tests apply to multiple causes (R-TRT-038, R-TRT-039).

**Authority:** R3d PEH § 26.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A lifeguard is absent, and a swimmer drowns in deep water. If evidence shows a lifeguard would have saved him, the absence is a but-for cause; if he died instantly of a heart attack, it is not.

### R-TRT-038 — Substantial factor; multiple sufficient causes
**Rule (write this):** Where two or more causes combine and each alone would have been sufficient to cause the harm, the but-for test fails, and each defendant's conduct is an actual cause if it was a substantial factor in bringing about the harm. California uses the substantial-factor test as its general causation standard.

**Elements:** 1. Two or more causes. 2. Each independently sufficient (merged fires) or each a substantial contributor. 3. Each is an actual cause.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A trivial or remote contribution is not a substantial factor. In asbestos cases, Π may show that exposure to Δ's product was a substantial factor contributing to the risk (*Rutherford*).

**Authority:** R2d §§ 431, 432(2) [VERIFY V-600]; R3d PEH § 27; *Mitchell v. Gonzales* (1991) 54 Cal.3d 1041; *Rutherford v. Owens-Illinois, Inc.* (1997) 16 Cal.4th 953.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** Two negligently set fires merge and burn P's house; either alone would have done so. Each fire-setter is an actual cause.

### R-TRT-039 — Alternative liability, concert of action, market share
**Rule (write this):** Where two or more defendants acted negligently and the plaintiff cannot prove which one caused the harm, the burden shifts to each defendant to prove he did not cause it (alternative liability). Persons acting in concert are jointly liable for harm caused by any of them. California also allows market-share liability against makers of a fungible product when the plaintiff cannot identify the maker.

**Elements:** **Alternative liability:** all Δs negligent, all joined, one caused the harm. **Concert of action:** agreement or tacit understanding and substantial assistance (drag racing). **Market share:** fungible product, Π cannot identify the maker through no fault of her own, Δs representing a substantial share of the market are joined; each pays its market share.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A Δ who proves he could not have caused the harm escapes. In California, market-share liability is several only (*Brown v. Superior Court*). Many states reject market-share liability.

**Authority:** *Summers v. Tice* (1948) 33 Cal.2d 80; *Sindell v. Abbott Laboratories* (1980) 26 Cal.3d 588; *Brown v. Superior Court* (1988) 44 Cal.3d 1049; R2d §§ 433B(3), 876 [VERIFY V-600].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule (MBE: alternative liability and concert of action are the tested versions).

**Example:** Two hunters negligently fire toward P; one pellet hits his eye, and no one can tell whose. Each hunter must prove his shot did not hit P, or both are liable.

### R-TRT-040 — Loss of chance
**Rule (write this):** Some jurisdictions allow a patient whose chance of survival or recovery was reduced by medical negligence, but was already below 50%, to recover damages proportionate to the lost chance. Others apply the traditional all-or-nothing preponderance rule, under which the plaintiff must prove the negligence more likely than not caused the death or injury.

**Elements:** 1. Medical negligence. 2. Reduced chance of a better outcome. 3. Proportional damages (where allowed).

**Exceptions / defenses:** California appellate courts have generally not recognized loss of chance in medical cases [VERIFY V-610].

**Authority:** R3d PEH § 26 cmt. n [VERIFY V-600].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: use the traditional preponderance rule unless the question describes a loss-of-chance jurisdiction.

**Example:** A missed diagnosis cut P's survival chance from 40% to 25%. Traditional rule: no recovery (not more likely than not). Loss-of-chance rule: 15% of wrongful-death damages.

### R-TRT-041 — Joint and several liability; divisible harms
**Rule (write this):** When the tortious conduct of two or more defendants causes a single indivisible injury, each is jointly and severally liable for the entire harm, and the plaintiff may recover the whole judgment from any one of them, though only once. If the harm is divisible, so that a reasonable basis exists for apportioning it to each cause, each defendant is liable only for the part he caused.

**Elements:** 1. Two or more tortfeasors. 2. Indivisible injury (one death, one fire, one broken leg). 3. Each Δ liable for the whole; Π may collect from any. 4. Paying Δ seeks contribution (R-TRT-043).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Many states have abolished or limited joint and several liability (several only, or joint only above a fault threshold). **MBE default: joint and several liability with pure comparative negligence** unless the question says otherwise [S-040]. California: see R-TRT-042.

**Authority:** R2d §§ 433A, 875, 879 [VERIFY V-600]; Restatement (Third) of Torts: Apportionment of Liability § 10 [VERIFY V-600].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: joint and several. California essay: joint and several for economic damages, several for non-economic (Prop 51).

**Example:** A and B negligently collide, and the cars hit P, causing $100,000 of indivisible injury; the jury finds A 70% and B 30% at fault. P may collect all $100,000 from B; B may then seek $70,000 contribution from A.

### R-TRT-042 — California: several liability for non-economic damages (Proposition 51)
**Rule (write this):** In California, each defendant's liability for economic damages remains joint and several, but each defendant's liability for non-economic damages, such as pain and suffering, is several only, in direct proportion to that defendant's percentage of fault.

**Elements:** 1. Comparative-fault action. 2. Economic damages (medical costs, lost earnings): joint and several. 3. Non-economic damages: each Δ pays its own percentage.

**Exceptions / defenses:** An intentional tortfeasor is generally not allowed to use § 1431.2 to shift non-economic damages to negligent co-defendants [VERIFY V-610]. Vicariously liable defendants (employers) pay the share of the person for whom they are responsible.

**Authority:** Civ. Code § 1431.2 (Proposition 51, 1986).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: joint and several (default). California essay: apply § 1431.2 when the call asks how damages are apportioned (e.g., Jul 2017 Q5 [VERIFY V-071]).

**Example:** P has $50,000 economic and $100,000 non-economic damages; A is 80% and B 20% at fault, and A is insolvent. B pays all $50,000 of economic damages but only $20,000 of non-economic damages.

### R-TRT-043 — Contribution, indemnity and settlement
**Rule (write this):** A jointly liable defendant who pays more than his share may obtain contribution from the other tortfeasors, today usually in proportion to comparative fault. Indemnity shifts the entire loss to another, for example from a vicariously liable employer to the negligent employee, or from a retailer to the manufacturer of a defective product; California uses comparative equitable indemnity among concurrent tortfeasors.

**Elements:** **Contribution:** joint liability + payment exceeding share. **Indemnity:** express contract, vicarious liability, or a non-negligent seller against the manufacturer. **Settlement:** Π's claim against non-settling Δs is reduced by the settlement amount (or, in some states, the settling Δ's share).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Traditional rule: contribution in equal (pro rata) shares; no contribution for intentional tortfeasors. **California:** partial (comparative) equitable indemnity (*American Motorcycle*); a good-faith settlement reduces the claims against the others by the amount paid and bars contribution or indemnity claims against the settling Δ (CCP §§ 877, 877.6); good faith means within the ballpark of the settlor's proportionate liability (*Tech-Bilt*).

**Authority:** R2d § 886A [VERIFY V-600]; *American Motorcycle Assn. v. Superior Court* (1978) 20 Cal.3d 578; CCP §§ 875–877.6; *Tech-Bilt, Inc. v. Woodward-Clyde & Associates* (1985) 38 Cal.3d 488.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: contribution by comparative fault; indemnity for vicarious liability and the innocent retailer. California essay: name comparative indemnity and CCP § 877.

**Example:** An employer pays a judgment for a delivery driver's negligence. It may seek full indemnity from the driver (rarely pursued in practice).

## II.E.1 Proximate cause

### R-TRT-044 — Proximate cause: foreseeability and scope of the risk
**Rule (write this):** A defendant is liable only for harms that are within the scope of the risks that made his conduct negligent. The harm must be of a foreseeable type to a foreseeable plaintiff; the precise manner in which it occurs and its extent need not be foreseeable.

**Elements:** 1. What risks made Δ's conduct negligent? 2. Was Π's harm among them (type of harm)? 3. Manner and extent need not be foreseeable.

**Exceptions / defenses:** The older "direct consequences" test (*Polemis*) held Δ liable for all direct results; *Wagon Mound No. 1* adopted foreseeability of the type of harm, which is the modern majority.

**Authority:** R3d PEH § 29; *Overseas Tankship (U.K.) Ltd. v. Morts Dock & Engineering Co. (The Wagon Mound No. 1)*, [1961] A.C. 388 (P.C.); *In re Polemis*, [1921] 3 K.B. 560.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** D leaves a loaded gun on a table where a child knocks it to the floor, where it lands on and breaks P's toe without firing. The risk that made leaving a loaded gun negligent was a shooting, not a falling object, so the toe injury is outside the scope of the risk.

### R-TRT-045 — Eggshell plaintiff; manner and extent of harm
**Rule (write this):** A tortfeasor takes his plaintiff as he finds him and is liable for the full extent of the plaintiff's harm, even if a pre-existing condition makes it far greater than a normal person would have suffered. Once some harm of the foreseeable type occurs, its unforeseeable extent does not limit liability.

**Elements:** 1. Δ is liable for some foreseeable harm. 2. Π's susceptibility aggravated it. 3. Δ pays for all of it.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Damages may be reduced for a pre-existing condition that would have caused harm anyway (apportioned to the condition, not to Δ).

**Authority:** R2d § 461 [VERIFY V-600]; R3d PEH § 31.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** D negligently bumps P's car at low speed. P, who has a fragile skull from earlier surgery, suffers a brain injury. D is liable for the brain injury.

### R-TRT-046 — Intervening and superseding causes
**Rule (write this):** An intervening force that occurs after the defendant's negligence does not relieve him of liability if it was a foreseeable result of the risk he created. Only an unforeseeable, extraordinary intervening act, such as an independent intentional crime that the defendant's conduct did not make more likely, is a superseding cause that breaks the chain of proximate cause.

**Elements:** **Foreseeable (not superseding):** medical malpractice in treating the injury; rescue attempts; negligence of third persons (including foreseeable negligent responses); reactions and escape attempts; subsequent disease or a second accident caused by weakness; crimes that Δ's negligence made foreseeable (leaving keys in a car in a high-crime area, failing to provide security). **Superseding:** unforeseeable intentional torts or crimes; unforeseeable acts of God producing a different kind of harm.

**Exceptions / defenses:** If the intervening act is the very risk that made Δ negligent, it never supersedes.

**Authority:** R2d §§ 440, 442, 447, 448, 449, 457 [VERIFY V-600]; R3d PEH § 34.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** D negligently breaks P's leg; the ER doctor sets it badly, and P needs a second surgery. Medical negligence is foreseeable, so D is liable for the whole.

## II.E.2 Claims against owners and occupiers of land

### R-TRT-047 — Trespassers
**Rule (write this):** A possessor of land owes no duty to an undiscovered trespasser except to refrain from willful or wanton conduct. To a discovered or anticipated trespasser, including one who constantly intrudes on a limited area, the possessor must use reasonable care in active operations and must warn of or make safe known, man-made conditions that involve a risk of death or serious bodily harm and that the trespasser is unlikely to discover.

**Elements:** 1. Status: entered without privilege or consent. 2. Undiscovered → no duty beyond avoiding willful/wanton harm. 3. Discovered or anticipated → (a) reasonable care in activities; (b) warn of known, concealed, artificial, deadly conditions.

**Exceptions / defenses:** No duty for natural conditions or for obvious dangers. Child trespassers: R-TRT-048. Deadly force is never allowed against a trespasser (R-TRT-012).

**Authority:** R2d §§ 333, 334, 335, 337 [VERIFY V-600].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: these categories. California essay: categories are abolished; status is only a factor (R-TRT-051).

**Example:** A railroad knows people regularly cross its tracks at one spot. It must sound a warning before running trains through there (active operation toward anticipated trespassers).

### R-TRT-048 — Child trespassers: attractive nuisance
**Rule (write this):** A possessor is liable for physical harm to trespassing children caused by an artificial condition on the land if he knows or has reason to know children are likely to trespass where the condition exists, the condition poses an unreasonable risk of death or serious bodily harm to children, the children because of their youth do not discover or appreciate the risk, the burden of eliminating the danger is slight compared with the risk, and he fails to use reasonable care.

**Elements:** 1. Possessor knows or has reason to know of likely child trespass. 2. Artificial condition he knows of or should know of. 3. Unreasonable risk of death or serious bodily harm to children. 4. Children don't realize the risk because of youth. 5. Utility and burden of eliminating it slight compared with the risk. 6. Failure to use reasonable care.

**Exceptions / defenses:** The child need not have been lured by the condition. Natural conditions and common, obvious dangers (open water, fire, heights) usually fall outside the doctrine, unless something makes them unusually hidden.

**Authority:** R2d § 339.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule (California applies it as part of *Rowland*'s general duty).

**Example:** A builder leaves an unfenced, half-dug swimming pool with a rickety ladder next to a school; a 7-year-old climbs in and is hurt. All five factors are met.

### R-TRT-049 — Licensees
**Rule (write this):** A licensee is a person who enters with the possessor's consent for her own purposes, including a social guest. The possessor must warn of or make safe dangerous conditions that he knows of and that the licensee is unlikely to discover, and must use reasonable care in active operations, but has no duty to inspect for unknown dangers.

**Elements:** 1. Consent to enter. 2. Π's own purpose (social guests, salespeople at a home, persons taking a shortcut with permission). 3. Duty: known concealed dangers + active operations.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Obvious dangers; Π's comparative fault.

**Authority:** R2d §§ 330, 341, 342 [VERIFY V-600].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: these categories. California essay: R-TRT-051.

**Example:** A host knows a basement stair is loose and says nothing to a dinner guest who falls. Liable. Had the host not known, there would be no liability because he owed no duty to inspect.

### R-TRT-050 — Invitees
**Rule (write this):** An invitee enters land held open to the public, or for a purpose connected with the possessor's business. The possessor owes an invitee reasonable care, including a duty to inspect for and discover dangerous conditions and then to warn of or make them safe, and reasonable care in active operations.

**Elements:** 1. Public invitee (museum, church, public library) or business visitor (customer, delivery person). 2. Duty: inspect, discover, warn or repair; reasonable care in activities. 3. Status lasts only within the scope of the invitation (in an area not open to customers, the invitee becomes a licensee or trespasser).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Traditionally no duty for open and obvious dangers; many courts now treat obviousness as bearing on breach and comparative fault, especially where the possessor should anticipate harm despite obviousness. Firefighters and police are treated as licensees in many states (see R-TRT-024).

**Authority:** R2d §§ 332, 343, 343A [VERIFY V-600].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: categories. California essay: R-TRT-051.

**Example:** A supermarket customer wanders past an "Employees Only" sign into the storeroom and trips. She exceeded the scope of her invitation, so in a categories jurisdiction she was a licensee or trespasser there.

### R-TRT-051 — California premises liability
**Rule (write this):** California has abolished the trespasser, licensee and invitee categories: a possessor of land owes every entrant the duty of reasonable care in the management of the property under Civil Code § 1714, and the entrant's status is only one factor in deciding whether that care was exercised. Statutes limit liability to recreational users and to persons injured while committing specified felonies.

**Elements:** 1. § 1714 general duty. 2. *Rowland* factors if a categorical exception is claimed. 3. Breach in light of all circumstances, including the entrant's status and the foreseeability of the entry.

**Exceptions / defenses:** **Recreational use (Civ. Code § 846):** no duty to keep land safe for, or warn, persons entering for recreation, unless the owner willfully or maliciously failed to guard or warn, charged consideration, or expressly invited (rather than merely permitted) the entrant. **Felony trespassers (Civ. Code § 847):** limits liability to persons injured while committing listed felonies [VERIFY V-609]. Primary assumption of risk for sports facilities (R-TRT-073).

**Authority:** *Rowland v. Christian* (1968) 69 Cal.2d 108; Civ. Code §§ 846, 847, 1714.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: categories (R-TRT-047 to R-TRT-050). California essay: state the majority categories and then add "California rejects these categories (*Rowland*)". Essays with land-entrant facts (Feb 2019 Q2, Jul 2025 Q2) reward the categories plus *Rowland* [VERIFY V-071].

**Example:** A social guest is cut by a cracked faucet handle the host knew about. In California the question is simply whether the host acted reasonably; the guest's licensee status does not cap the duty.

### R-TRT-052 — Lessors, lessees and vendors of land
**Rule (write this):** A lessor who transfers possession is generally not liable for dangerous conditions arising afterward, but he is liable for conditions in common areas he controls, for latent dangers he knew of and did not disclose, for negligently performed repairs or repairs he promised to make, and for land leased for public admission with a known dangerous condition. The tenant, as possessor, owes entrants the duties of a possessor.

**Elements:** Lessor exceptions: 1. Concealed dangerous condition known at the lease (duty to disclose). 2. Common areas retained. 3. Covenant to repair or negligent voluntary repair. 4. Public-use lease. 5. Short-term furnished dwelling (some states).

**Exceptions / defenses:** A vendor of land must disclose concealed dangers he knows of, and remains liable until the buyer has had a reasonable time to discover them. **California:** landlords owe a duty of reasonable care under § 1714, but are not strictly liable for defects in rented premises (*Peterson*).

**Authority:** R2d §§ 355–362 [VERIFY V-600]; *Peterson v. Superior Court* (1995) 10 Cal.4th 1185.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: common-law lessor rules. California essay: § 1714 reasonable care; no strict liability.

**Example:** A landlord knows the back porch is rotted but says nothing when leasing; the tenant's guest falls through. The landlord is liable for a known latent defect.

### R-TRT-053 — Duty to protect entrants from third-party crime
**Rule (write this):** A business possessor owes invitees a duty to take reasonable steps to protect them from foreseeable criminal acts of third persons. In California, the scope of required precautions scales with foreseeability: costly measures such as hiring security guards require heightened foreseeability, usually shown by prior similar incidents, while minimal measures may be required on lesser foreseeability.

**Elements:** 1. Special relationship (business–invitee; landlord–tenant in common areas). 2. Foreseeability of the crime. 3. Burden of the precaution sought. 4. Breach, causation and damages.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Courts use different foreseeability tests: prior similar incidents, totality of circumstances, or balancing. An unforeseeable crime is a superseding cause.

**Authority:** *Ann M. v. Pacific Plaza Shopping Center* (1993) 6 Cal.4th 666; *Delgado v. Trax Bar & Grill* (2005) 36 Cal.4th 224; R2d § 344 [VERIFY V-600].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same core rule; California essay names the *Ann M.*/*Delgado* sliding scale.

**Example:** A bar's bouncer sees a patron threaten a customer and then follow him outside, and does nothing. Even without prior incidents, the immediate threat made minimal steps (separating the two) required.

## II.E.3 Mental distress and other intangible injuries

### R-TRT-054 — Negligent infliction of emotional distress: zone of danger and direct victims
**Rule (write this):** A plaintiff may recover for emotional distress caused by the defendant's negligence if the plaintiff was within the zone of physical danger created by the defendant and feared for his own safety. Many courts traditionally also required a physical injury or physical manifestation of the distress.

**Elements:** 1. Δ's negligence. 2. Π in the zone of danger (threatened with physical impact). 3. Serious emotional distress (traditionally with physical symptoms). 4. Causation.

**Exceptions / defenses:** **Special relationship / guaranteed-genuine cases:** liability without physical danger for negligently mishandling a corpse, negligently reporting a death, or a physician's negligent diagnosis communicated to the patient. A minority still applies an impact rule.

**Authority:** R2d §§ 313, 436, 436A [VERIFY V-600]; R3d PEH § 47.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: zone of danger, physical manifestation often required. California essay: no separate tort; it is negligence, with direct-victim and bystander paths (R-TRT-055, R-TRT-056).

**Example:** A truck jumps the curb and narrowly misses P, who suffers severe anxiety and stomach ulcers. P was in the zone of danger and has physical manifestations: recovery.

### R-TRT-055 — Bystander recovery (Dillon / Thing)
**Rule (write this):** A bystander may recover for serious emotional distress from witnessing negligently caused injury to another only if the plaintiff is closely related to the injury victim, is present at the scene of the injury-producing event when it occurs and is then aware that it is causing injury to the victim, and as a result suffers serious emotional distress beyond that which a disinterested witness would suffer.

**Elements:** 1. Close relationship (relatives living in the same household, or parents, siblings, children, grandparents). 2. Presence at the scene and contemporaneous awareness of the injury-causing event. 3. Serious emotional distress.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Arriving after the accident, or learning of it later, is not enough. Contemporaneous sensory awareness need not be visual (hearing the crash and knowing a child is involved can suffice). Awareness that the event is causing injury is required in medical cases (*Bird v. Saenz*). **Unmarried cohabitants** cannot recover (*Elden v. Sheldon*), but the California Supreme Court has granted review to reconsider that rule (*Sanchez v. Rojas*) [S-711] [VERIFY V-604]. Comparative fault of the injured victim may reduce recovery.

**Authority:** *Dillon v. Legg* (1968) 68 Cal.2d 728; *Thing v. La Chusa* (1989) 48 Cal.3d 644; *Elden v. Sheldon* (1988) 46 Cal.3d 267 [S-712]; *Bird v. Saenz* (2002) 28 Cal.4th 910; R3d PEH § 48.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Majority today: a close family member who perceives the event contemporaneously (R3d § 48), some still requiring zone of danger. California essay: the three *Thing* requirements, word for word.

**Example:** A mother hears brakes and a thud, turns and sees her son hit in the crosswalk. She recovers. Her sister, who learns of it by phone an hour later, does not.

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: PRC law compensates serious mental harm (精神损害赔偿) mainly through the victim's close relatives after death or personality-right violations; US bystander recovery is narrower and turns on presence at the scene (approx.).

### R-TRT-056 — California direct-victim recovery
**Rule (write this):** In California, negligent infliction of emotional distress is not an independent tort but a form of negligence. A direct victim may recover for serious emotional distress without physical injury where the defendant breached a duty owed directly to the plaintiff that was assumed by the defendant, imposed by law, or arose from a relationship between them.

**Elements:** 1. Duty owed directly to Π (assumed, imposed by law, or relational: physician–patient, therapist–patient, mortuary–family). 2. Breach. 3. Serious emotional distress. 4. Causation.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Without such a duty, Π must qualify as a bystander (R-TRT-055). Pure emotional distress from property damage is generally not recoverable in negligence.

**Authority:** *Burgess v. Superior Court* (1992) 2 Cal.4th 1064 (mother injured emotionally by negligent delivery of her child); *Molien v. Kaiser Foundation Hospitals* (1980) 27 Cal.3d 916 (husband of patient negligently misdiagnosed with syphilis); *Marlene F. v. Affiliated Psychiatric Medical Clinic, Inc.* (1989) 48 Cal.3d 583.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: special-relationship exceptions (R-TRT-054). California essay: *Burgess* direct-victim theory.

**Example:** An obstetrician's negligence during delivery causes a baby's brain injury. The mother is a direct victim because the physician's duty ran to her as a patient; she need not satisfy *Thing*.

### R-TRT-057 — Fear of future harm (toxic exposure)
**Rule (write this):** A plaintiff exposed to a toxic substance through the defendant's negligence, but not yet physically ill, may recover for fear of developing a disease in California only if the fear stems from knowledge, corroborated by reliable medical or scientific opinion, that it is more likely than not the disease will develop because of the exposure.

**Elements:** 1. Negligent exposure to a toxin. 2. Serious fear of disease. 3. Medical corroboration that the disease is more likely than not. 4. Fear is reasonable.

**Exceptions / defenses:** If Δ acted with oppression, fraud or malice, the more-likely-than-not requirement is relaxed to a significant and reasonable fear. Medical monitoring costs may be recoverable on a lesser showing.

**Authority:** *Potter v. Firestone Tire & Rubber Co.* (1993) 6 Cal.4th 965.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: rarely tested. California essay: cite *Potter*.

**Example:** A factory negligently leaks a chemical into a town's water; experts say it raises cancer risk by 3%. Residents cannot recover for fear of cancer (not more likely than not), but may seek medical monitoring.

### R-TRT-058 — Loss of consortium
**Rule (write this):** A spouse of a person tortiously injured may recover for loss of consortium: the loss of the injured spouse's companionship, affection, society and sexual relations. The claim is derivative, so defenses against the injured spouse affect it.

**Elements:** 1. Valid marriage (or registered domestic partnership) at the time of injury. 2. Tortious injury to the spouse. 3. Loss of consortium.

**Exceptions / defenses:** California does not allow children to recover for loss of a parent's consortium (*Borer*) or unmarried cohabitants (*Elden*, under review [VERIFY V-604]). Many states allow parents' or children's claims in limited forms.

**Authority:** *Rodriguez v. Bethlehem Steel Corp.* (1974) 12 Cal.3d 382; *Borer v. American Airlines, Inc.* (1977) 19 Cal.3d 441; *Elden v. Sheldon* (1988) 46 Cal.3d 267 [S-712].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** P's husband is paralyzed by D's negligent driving. P recovers for loss of consortium in addition to the husband's own damages.

### R-TRT-059 — Wrongful death
**Rule (write this):** A wrongful-death action allows the decedent's statutory beneficiaries to recover their own losses caused by a death that resulted from the defendant's tortious conduct. Recovery covers the financial support and services they would have received and, in California, the loss of the decedent's love, companionship, comfort, care, assistance, protection, affection, society and moral support, but not grief or sorrow, the decedent's own pain, or punitive damages.

**Elements:** 1. Death caused by Δ's wrongful act or neglect. 2. Π is a statutory beneficiary (CCP § 377.60: spouse, domestic partner, children, issue of deceased children; if none, those who would take by intestacy; also putative spouses, stepchildren and parents or minors who were financially dependent). 3. Damages to the beneficiaries.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Δ may raise any defense it would have had against the decedent (decedent's comparative fault reduces recovery). One action for all heirs. Limitations: two years from death (CCP § 335.1). Medical-negligence deaths: MICRA cap (R-TRT-115).

**Authority:** CCP §§ 377.60–377.62; CCP § 335.1.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: assume wrongful-death and survival actions are available [S-040]. California essay: list CCP § 377.60 beneficiaries and the damages categories.

**Example:** A negligent driver kills a 40-year-old engineer survived by a wife and two children. They recover his expected financial support and household services plus loss of his companionship; the estate separately brings a survival action for his pre-death losses.

### R-TRT-060 — Survival actions
**Rule (write this):** A survival action preserves the claims the decedent had at death, which pass to the decedent's personal representative or successor in interest. In California, damages are limited to the loss the decedent sustained before death, such as medical expenses and lost earnings, plus punitive damages the decedent could have recovered; damages for pain, suffering or disfigurement are not recoverable, except in actions filed from January 1, 2022 through December 31, 2025.

**Elements:** 1. Decedent had a cause of action when he died. 2. Brought by the personal representative or successor in interest (CCP § 377.30). 3. Damages: pre-death economic loss and punitive damages; no post-death lost earnings (those belong to the wrongful-death beneficiaries).

**Exceptions / defenses:** The temporary allowance of pain-and-suffering damages (CCP § 377.34(b), added by SB 447) expired for actions filed on or after Jan 1, 2026, and an extension bill (SB 29) was not enacted in 2025 [S-704] [S-705] [VERIFY V-602]. At common law, personal tort claims died with the person; survival statutes reverse that.

**Authority:** CCP §§ 377.20, 377.30, 377.34.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: assume survival actions are available [S-040]. California essay: distinguish survival (estate's claim) from wrongful death (heirs' claim).

**Example:** P is injured, lives three months, then dies. The survival action recovers his medical bills and lost wages for those three months, plus punitive damages if available; under current California law, his pain during those months is not recoverable in an action filed in 2027.

### R-TRT-061 — Pure economic loss
**Rule (write this):** There is generally no duty to avoid negligently causing purely economic loss, unconnected to personal injury or property damage, to a stranger. Recovery is allowed only where a special relationship makes the plaintiff's loss particularly foreseeable and closely connected to the defendant's conduct, such as negligent misrepresentation to an intended recipient or negligent performance of a service intended to benefit the plaintiff.

**Elements:** California special-relationship factors (*Biakanja*, *J'Aire*): 1. extent to which the transaction was intended to affect Π; 2. foreseeability of harm; 3. certainty of injury; 4. closeness of connection; 5. moral blame; 6. policy of preventing future harm.

**Exceptions / defenses:** No duty to businesses closed by a mass gas leak, absent a special relationship (*Southern California Gas Leak Cases*) [S-715]. Products that damage only themselves: warranty, not tort (*Seely*, R-TRT-086). Between contracting parties, the economic loss rule limits tort claims, but fraud claims survive (R-TRT-108; R-REM-032).

**Authority:** *Biakanja v. Irving* (1958) 49 Cal.2d 647; *J'Aire Corp. v. Gregory* (1979) 24 Cal.3d 799; *Southern California Gas Leak Cases* (2019) 7 Cal.5th 391 [S-715]; Restatement (Third) of Torts: Liability for Economic Harm § 1 [VERIFY V-600].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same core rule.

**Example:** A notary negligently fails to have a will properly witnessed, and the intended beneficiary loses her inheritance. The transaction was intended to benefit her: duty (*Biakanja*). A restaurant that loses business because a crash closes the street has no claim against the negligent driver.

### R-TRT-062 — Wrongful birth and wrongful life
**Rule (write this):** Parents may recover in negligence ("wrongful birth") when a physician's negligent genetic counseling, testing or sterilization leads to the birth of a child with impairments, generally for the extraordinary costs of care. Most states deny a child's own claim for "wrongful life", but California lets the child recover special damages for extraordinary medical and care expenses, though not general damages.

**Elements:** 1. Professional negligence in counseling, testing or procedure. 2. Birth that would otherwise have been avoided. 3. Damages: extraordinary expenses (not the ordinary costs of raising a healthy child, in most states).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Wrongful conception (failed sterilization, healthy child): most states limit damages to pregnancy-related costs.

**Authority:** *Turpin v. Sortini* (1982) 31 Cal.3d 220.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** rarely tested; know the labels.

**Example:** A lab misreads a genetic test; the parents, told the fetus is healthy, continue the pregnancy, and the child is born with a severe hereditary disorder. The parents recover the extraordinary costs of care; in California the child may also recover special damages.

## II.F Liability for acts of others

### R-TRT-063 — Respondeat superior
**Rule (write this):** An employer is vicariously liable for the torts of an employee committed within the scope of employment. Conduct is within the scope if it is of the kind the employee was hired to perform, occurs substantially within authorized time and space limits, and is motivated at least in part by a purpose to serve the employer; in California, the test is whether the tort was engendered by or a foreseeable outgrowth of the employment.

**Elements:** 1. Employer–employee relationship (right to control the manner and means of work). 2. Tort within the scope of employment. 3. Detours (minor deviations) stay within scope; frolics (substantial personal ventures) do not. 4. Going and coming: commuting is outside scope, except special errands, required use of a personal vehicle, or business travel.

**Exceptions / defenses:** **Intentional torts** are within scope if force is part of the job (bouncer, security guard), they arise from job-related friction, or they are meant to serve the employer. A sexual assault by a hospital technician was not engendered by the job (*Lisa M.*), but a rape by an on-duty police officer exercising authority was within scope (*Mary M.*). The employer is jointly liable with the employee and may seek indemnity. The employer may also be directly liable for negligent hiring or supervision (R-TRT-067).

**Authority:** Restatement (Third) of Agency § 7.07 [VERIFY V-600]; *Hinman v. Westinghouse Electric Co.* (1970) 2 Cal.3d 956; *Lisa M. v. Henry Mayo Newhall Memorial Hospital* (1995) 12 Cal.4th 291; *Mary M. v. City of Los Angeles* (1991) 54 Cal.3d 202.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same core rule. Crossovers with Business Associations (partnership liability, Jul 2023 Q1) and Agency use it [VERIFY V-071].

**Example:** A pizza driver runs a red light while delivering: within scope. If he drives 40 miles to visit his girlfriend first, he is on a frolic until he returns to the route.

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: like 用人单位责任 under the PRC Civil Code (employer liable for an employee's work-related torts, with recourse against an employee at serious fault) (approx.).

### R-TRT-064 — Independent contractors and nondelegable duties
**Rule (write this):** A person who hires an independent contractor is generally not vicariously liable for the contractor's negligence. Exceptions apply where the hirer was itself negligent in selecting, instructing or supervising the contractor, the work is inherently dangerous or involves a peculiar risk, or the hirer owes a nondelegable duty, such as a possessor's duty to keep premises open to the public reasonably safe or a duty imposed by statute.

**Elements:** 1. Independent contractor (hirer controls the result, not the manner and means). 2. General rule: no vicarious liability. 3. Exceptions: (a) hirer's own negligence; (b) inherently dangerous activity or peculiar risk; (c) nondelegable duty (public premises, common carriers, landlord's duties in common areas, statutory or regulatory safety duties); (d) apparent agency (a hospital that holds out an emergency-room doctor as its own).

**Exceptions / defenses:** **California:** a hirer is generally not liable to the contractor's **own employees** injured on the job (*Privette*), unless the hirer retained control and affirmatively contributed to the injury (*Hooker*) or failed to disclose a concealed hazard it knew of (*Kinsman*).

**Authority:** R2d §§ 409, 411, 414, 416, 425, 427 [VERIFY V-600]; *Privette v. Superior Court* (1993) 5 Cal.4th 689; *Hooker v. Department of Transportation* (2002) 27 Cal.4th 198; *Kinsman v. Unocal Corp.* (2005) 37 Cal.4th 659.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. A California essay asked whether a hospital was liable for its caterer's negligence (Jul 2021 Q3) and whether a caterer's owner was liable for a waiter (Jul 2014 Q6): run employee-vs-contractor, then the exceptions [VERIFY V-071].

**Example:** A hospital hires a caterer that serves peanuts to a patient flagged as allergic. The caterer is an independent contractor, but the hospital's duty to provide safe meals to patients in its care is arguably nondelegable, so it is liable.

### R-TRT-065 — Partners, joint enterprise, vehicle owners and bailors
**Rule (write this):** Partners are vicariously liable for torts committed by a partner in the ordinary course of partnership business, and members of a joint enterprise are liable for each other's torts within it. A vehicle owner or other bailor is not vicariously liable at common law for the bailee's negligence, but may be liable for his own negligent entrustment, and many states, including California, make vehicle owners liable by statute for permissive users within limits.

**Elements:** **Joint enterprise:** common purpose, community of pecuniary interest, equal right of control. **Owner statutes:** permissive use; capped amounts. **Family-purpose doctrine** (some states): head of household liable for family members using the family car.

**Exceptions / defenses:** California: Vehicle Code §§ 17150–17151 impose limited owner liability for permissive users [VERIFY V-609].

**Authority:** Revised Uniform Partnership Act § 305 [VERIFY V-600]; R2d § 491 [VERIFY V-600]; Veh. Code §§ 17150–17151 [VERIFY V-609].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule; BA crossovers test partnership liability.

**Example:** Two partners of a catering partnership drive to a client event; the driving partner causes a crash. The partnership and the other partner are liable because the trip was in the ordinary course of business.

### R-TRT-066 — Parents' liability for children's torts
**Rule (write this):** Parents are not vicariously liable for their children's torts at common law. A parent is liable for his own negligence in failing to exercise reasonable control over a child when he knows of the child's specific dangerous tendency and has the ability and opportunity to control it, and statutes impose limited liability for a child's willful misconduct.

**Elements:** 1. Parent knows of the child's specific dangerous propensity. 2. Ability and opportunity to control. 3. Failure to use reasonable care. 4. Causation.

**Exceptions / defenses:** California: a parent having custody and control is jointly and severally liable with the minor for the minor's willful misconduct, subject to a statutory cap (Civ. Code § 1714.1) [VERIFY V-609]; negligent entrustment of a car or gun is the parent's own tort.

**Authority:** R2d § 316; Civ. Code § 1714.1 [VERIFY V-609].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same common-law rule.

**Example:** Parents who know their 12-year-old has hit other children with a bat, and leave him unsupervised with one at a park, are liable for their own negligence when he strikes another child.

### R-TRT-067 — Negligent hiring, supervision, retention and entrustment
**Rule (write this):** An employer or other principal is directly liable for its own negligence in hiring, supervising or retaining a person it knew or should have known posed a particular risk of the harm that occurred. One who supplies a chattel to a person he knows or should know is likely to use it dangerously, such as an intoxicated, unlicensed or reckless driver, is liable for negligent entrustment.

**Elements:** 1. Δ knew or should have known of the person's unfitness. 2. The unfitness created the particular risk. 3. The harm resulted from that risk. 4. Causation.

**Exceptions / defenses:** These theories reach conduct outside the scope of employment, where respondeat superior fails.

**Authority:** R2d §§ 308, 390, 317 [VERIFY V-600].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. Pair it with respondeat superior whenever an employee commits an intentional tort.

**Example:** A moving company hires a driver with three recent assault convictions without a background check; he assaults a customer in her home. Even if the assault was outside the scope of employment, the company is liable for negligent hiring.

### R-TRT-068 — Dram shop and social host liability
**Rule (write this):** At common law, a seller or host who furnished alcohol was not liable for injuries caused by the drinker, because the drinking, not the furnishing, was treated as the proximate cause. Many states impose statutory liability on commercial vendors. California follows the common-law rule by statute, except that a licensee who sells alcohol to an obviously intoxicated minor, and a social host who knowingly furnishes alcohol at his residence to a person under 21, may be liable.

**Elements:** California: 1. General rule: furnishing alcohol is not the proximate cause of injuries from intoxication (Civ. Code § 1714(b)–(c); Bus. & Prof. Code § 25602). 2. Exceptions: licensee + obviously intoxicated minor (Bus. & Prof. Code § 25602.1); adult social host + knowingly furnished to person under 21 at the host's residence (Civ. Code § 1714(d)).

**Exceptions / defenses:** A bar may still be liable for its own negligence in other respects (failing to protect patrons from a known fight, R-TRT-053).

**Authority:** Civ. Code § 1714(b)–(d); Bus. & Prof. Code §§ 25602, 25602.1.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: follow the question's statute; absent one, common law. California essay: cite § 1714 and § 25602.

**Example:** A bar serves an obviously drunk 19-year-old who then drives and injures P. The bar is liable under § 25602.1. If the drinker were 25, the bar would not be liable to P in California.

## II.G Defenses to negligence

### R-TRT-069 — Contributory negligence and last clear chance
**Rule (write this):** Under the traditional rule, a plaintiff whose own negligence contributed to his injury is completely barred from recovery. The last-clear-chance doctrine softens the bar: a negligent plaintiff still recovers if the defendant had the last clear opportunity to avoid the harm and failed to use it.

**Elements:** 1. Π failed to use reasonable care for his own safety. 2. That failure was an actual and proximate cause of his harm. 3. Complete bar. **Last clear chance:** Π in helpless (or inattentive) peril; Δ knew or should have known; Δ could have avoided the harm with reasonable care.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Contributory negligence is no defense to intentional torts or to willful and wanton conduct, and (except knowing, unreasonable exposure) no defense to strict liability. Few jurisdictions retain it (e.g., Alabama, Maryland, North Carolina, Virginia, District of Columbia).

**Authority:** R2d §§ 463, 467, 479, 480 [VERIFY V-600].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: apply only if the question says contributory negligence governs. California: abolished (*Li*).

**Example:** P jaywalks; D, speeding and texting, sees P 200 feet away and could stop but does not. In a contributory-negligence state P is barred unless last clear chance applies, and here it does.

### R-TRT-070 — Comparative negligence
**Rule (write this):** Under comparative negligence, the plaintiff's own negligence does not bar recovery but reduces his damages in proportion to his percentage of fault. In a pure system, which California and the MBE default use, the plaintiff recovers even if he is more at fault than the defendant; in modified systems, recovery is barred once the plaintiff's fault equals (50% rule) or exceeds (51% rule) the defendant's.

**Elements:** 1. Trier of fact assigns percentages of fault to all parties. 2. Π's damages reduced by Π's percentage. 3. Pure: always recover (100 − Π%). Modified: barred at 50% or 51%. 4. Last clear chance disappears; secondary implied assumption of risk merges into comparative fault.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Most states do not reduce damages against an intentional tortfeasor for Π's negligence. Comparative fault applies to strict products liability in California (*Daly*).

**Authority:** *Li v. Yellow Cab Co.* (1975) 13 Cal.3d 804; *Daly v. General Motors Corp.* (1978) 20 Cal.3d 725; Restatement (Third) of Torts: Apportionment of Liability §§ 7, 8 [VERIFY V-600].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Both: **pure comparative negligence** (MBE default [S-040]; California under *Li*).

**Example:** P suffers $100,000 of damages; the jury finds P 60% and D 40% at fault. Under pure comparative fault P recovers $40,000; in a modified state P recovers nothing.

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: like 过失相抵 under the PRC Civil Code (the victim's fault may reduce the infringer's liability); PRC courts reduce by discretion, while US juries assign exact percentages (approx.).

### R-TRT-071 — Express assumption of risk (releases)
**Rule (write this):** A plaintiff who expressly agrees in advance, usually in a signed release, to relieve the defendant of liability for its negligence is barred from recovery, if the agreement is clear, unambiguous and covers the conduct. A release is unenforceable for intentional or reckless misconduct or gross negligence, and where it affects the public interest, as with essential services.

**Elements:** 1. Clear, explicit language covering negligence. 2. Π knowingly agreed. 3. Not against public policy (*Tunkl* factors: business suitable for public regulation; essential service; open to the public; decisive bargaining advantage; adhesion contract; Π placed under Δ's control). 4. Not gross negligence, recklessness or intentional harm.

**Exceptions / defenses:** California: contracts exempting a party from its own fraud, willful injury or violation of law are void (Civ. Code § 1668); a release of future gross negligence is unenforceable (*City of Santa Barbara*). Recreational releases are generally enforced for ordinary negligence.

**Authority:** *Tunkl v. Regents of University of California* (1963) 60 Cal.2d 92; *City of Santa Barbara v. Superior Court* (2007) 41 Cal.4th 747; Civ. Code § 1668; R2d § 496B [VERIFY V-600].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A gym member signs a release of "all claims arising from ordinary negligence." She is hurt by a machine staff had failed to inspect for months despite complaints. The release bars ordinary negligence but not gross negligence, so the question is how bad the failure was.

### R-TRT-072 — Implied assumption of risk (traditional)
**Rule (write this):** A plaintiff who knows of a specific risk created by the defendant and voluntarily chooses to encounter it impliedly assumes that risk. Traditionally this was a complete bar; in most comparative-fault jurisdictions, unreasonable assumption of risk is now merged into comparative negligence, while risks inherent in an activity remain outside the defendant's duty.

**Elements:** 1. Actual, subjective knowledge of the specific risk. 2. Appreciation of its nature. 3. Voluntary choice to encounter it (a choice is not voluntary if Δ left no reasonable alternative).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Not a defense to intentional torts. Rescuers usually do not assume the risk. A defense to strict liability for abnormally dangerous activities and products if Π knowingly encountered the danger.

**Authority:** R2d §§ 496C, 496D, 496E [VERIFY V-600].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: knowledge + voluntary encounter, then treat as comparative fault unless the question says it is a complete bar. California essay: use *Knight* (R-TRT-073).

**Example:** P knows a friend's car has no working brakes but rides with him anyway to save bus fare. P assumed the risk; in a comparative state his recovery is reduced.

### R-TRT-073 — California: primary and secondary assumption of risk; coaches and sports
**Rule (write this):** In California, primary assumption of risk applies where, by the nature of the activity and the parties' relationship to it, the defendant owes no duty to protect the plaintiff from the risks inherent in the activity; it is a complete bar. Secondary assumption of risk applies where the defendant owes a duty but the plaintiff knowingly encounters the risk, and it is merged into comparative fault. Co-participants in a sport are liable only if they intentionally injure another or act so recklessly as to be totally outside the range of ordinary activity in the sport.

**Elements:** 1. Is the activity a sport or recreational activity with inherent risks (*Knight*; *Nalwa*)? 2. Was the risk inherent? 3. Δ's role: **co-participant** → intent or recklessness required; **coach or instructor** → liable for intentionally injuring the student or for conduct totally outside the range of ordinary coaching, such as recklessly pushing a student beyond her ability (*Kahn*) [S-714]; **operator or host** → no duty for inherent risks, but a duty not to increase them beyond those inherent and to minimize extrinsic risks (*Avila*). 4. Otherwise: secondary AOR → comparative fault.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Being hit by a pitch is inherent in baseball (*Avila*). A facility's negligently maintained field or equipment can increase risks beyond those inherent.

**Authority:** *Knight v. Jewett* (1992) 3 Cal.4th 296; *Kahn v. East Side Union High School Dist.* (2003) 31 Cal.4th 990 [S-714]; *Avila v. Citrus Community College Dist.* (2006) 38 Cal.4th 148; *Nalwa v. Cedar Fair, L.P.* (2012) 55 Cal.4th 1148.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: traditional implied AOR (R-TRT-072). California essay: *Knight*'s primary/secondary framework is high-yield (sports injuries, Jul 2025 Q2: field owner, coach, players) [VERIFY V-071].

**Example:** In a pickup soccer game P is kicked while both players chase the ball: inherent risk, no liability. If the coach, angry at a loss, orders a novice to play goalie with no gear against adult shooters, the coach may be liable for reckless conduct outside the range of ordinary coaching.

### R-TRT-074 — Immunities: intrafamily and charitable
**Rule (write this):** Most states, including California, have abolished interspousal and charitable immunity. Parent–child immunity has been abolished or narrowed: California asks what an ordinarily reasonable and prudent parent would have done in similar circumstances, while some states keep immunity for the exercise of parental authority and discretion.

**Elements:** 1. Interspousal: abolished. 2. Parent–child: California reasonable-parent standard; elsewhere varies (often retained for discipline and supervision decisions, not for driving). 3. Charitable: abolished in most states (often replaced by damages caps or insurance limits).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Immunity is a personal defense; it does not protect a third party (an employer remains vicariously liable even if the employee is immune).

**Authority:** *Self v. Self* (1962) 58 Cal.2d 683; *Klein v. Klein* (1962) 58 Cal.2d 692; *Gibson v. Gibson* (1971) 3 Cal.3d 914; *Malloy v. Fong* (1951) 37 Cal.2d 356.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A child injured when her father negligently drives the family car can sue him in California; insurance usually pays.

### R-TRT-075 — Governmental and official immunity
**Rule (write this):** A government is immune from tort liability except to the extent it has waived immunity by statute. The Federal Tort Claims Act waives federal immunity for negligent acts of employees within the scope of employment, but not for discretionary functions or most intentional torts; California public entities are liable only as provided by statute, notably vicariously for employees' torts and for dangerous conditions of public property, and public employees are immune for discretionary policy decisions.

**Elements:** **Federal (FTCA):** negligence of a federal employee within scope; exceptions: discretionary function, most intentional torts (except listed torts by law-enforcement officers). **California:** 1. No common-law liability (Gov. Code § 815). 2. Vicarious liability for employee acts within scope, if the employee would be liable (§ 815.2). 3. Employee immunity for discretionary acts (§ 820.2): basic policy decisions, not operational ones (*Johnson v. State*). 4. **Dangerous condition of public property** (§ 835): dangerous condition at the time of injury, proximate cause, reasonably foreseeable risk of the kind of injury, and either an employee's negligent act created it or the entity had actual or constructive notice in time to fix it [S-719]. 5. No punitive damages against public entities (§ 818) [VERIFY V-607]. 6. Claim presentation within six months for personal injury (§ 911.2) [VERIFY V-607].

**Exceptions / defenses:** **Officials:** judges, legislators and prosecutors have absolute immunity for official acts; other officials have qualified immunity for discretionary acts in good faith (in § 1983 actions, unless they violate clearly established law). Traditional municipal law distinguished governmental (immune) from proprietary (liable) functions. California public entities are not liable for failure to provide police protection (Gov. Code § 845) [VERIFY V-607].

**Authority:** 28 U.S.C. §§ 1346(b), 2680(a), (h); Gov. Code §§ 815, 815.2, 818, 820.2, 835, 845, 911.2 [VERIFY V-607]; *Johnson v. State of California* (1968) 69 Cal.2d 782; *Harlow v. Fitzgerald*, 457 U.S. 800 (1982).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: FTCA and discretionary immunity basics. California essay: Gov. Code framework, briefly, when Δ is a public entity or employee (Jul 2021 Q3, a state hospital and its CEO) [VERIFY V-071].

**Example:** A state hospital's CEO decides, as a budget policy, to cut night security on every ward: discretionary, immune. A guard who falls asleep on duty while a patient is attacked acts operationally, and the hospital is vicariously liable under § 815.2.

### R-TRT-076 — Statutes of limitations and repose (California)
**Rule (write this):** A tort claim must be filed within the statutory limitations period, which usually begins when the cause of action accrues; under the discovery rule, accrual is delayed until the plaintiff discovers, or reasonably should discover, the injury and its wrongful cause. Statutes of repose cut off claims a fixed time after an event regardless of discovery.

**Elements:** California periods: personal injury, assault, battery and wrongful death — 2 years (CCP § 335.1); libel, slander and false imprisonment — 1 year (CCP § 340(c)); trespass, injury to property and fraud — 3 years, fraud from discovery (CCP § 338); medical negligence — 3 years from injury or 1 year from discovery, whichever is first (CCP § 340.5); latent construction defects — 10-year repose (CCP § 337.15).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Tolling for minority and incapacity; claims against public entities require a timely administrative claim first (R-TRT-075).

**Authority:** CCP §§ 335.1, 337.15, 338, 340, 340.5.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** rarely tested; mention only if the facts give dates.

**Example:** P is defamed in a newspaper on March 1, 2026, and sues on April 1, 2027. The one-year period under CCP § 340(c) has run.

## III Strict liability

### R-TRT-077 — Abnormally dangerous activities
**Rule (write this):** One who carries on an abnormally dangerous activity is strictly liable for harm to the person or property of another resulting from it, although he exercised the utmost care. The liability is limited to the kind of harm whose possibility makes the activity abnormally dangerous, suffered by a foreseeable plaintiff.

**Elements:** 1. Abnormally dangerous activity, judged by R2d § 520 factors: (a) high risk of harm; (b) likelihood that the harm will be great; (c) inability to eliminate the risk by reasonable care; (d) not a matter of common usage; (e) inappropriateness to the place; (f) value to the community outweighed by its dangerous attributes. R3d PEH § 20: a foreseeable and highly significant risk of physical harm even with reasonable care, and not common usage. 2. Harm of the type that made it dangerous. 3. Actual and proximate cause. 4. Damages.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Typical examples: blasting, storing or transporting explosives, fumigation with poison gas, toxic waste, crop dusting in some states. Driving and ordinary utilities are common usage. Liability attaches even where an unforeseeable third person or force of nature triggers the harm (R2d § 522) [VERIFY V-600]. Defenses: R-TRT-080.

**Authority:** *Rylands v. Fletcher*, L.R. 3 H.L. 330 (1868); R2d §§ 519, 520; R3d PEH § 20; *Luthringer v. Moore* (1948) 31 Cal.2d 489 (fumigation).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A contractor blasting rock with every precaution showers a neighbor's house with debris. Strict liability. If the noise instead frightens a neighbor's mink into killing their young, many courts deny recovery because that is not the kind of harm (debris, concussion) that makes blasting dangerous.

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: like 高度危险责任 (no-fault liability for ultra-hazardous activities) in the PRC Civil Code; the US test is a factor-based court judgment, not a list (approx.).

### R-TRT-078 — Wild animals
**Rule (write this):** A possessor of a wild animal is strictly liable for harm caused by a dangerous propensity characteristic of wild animals of its class, even if the animal was tamed and the possessor used the utmost care.

**Elements:** 1. Δ possesses (owns or harbors) a wild animal (not by nature domesticated in the community). 2. Harm results from a dangerous propensity typical of the species, or one Δ knows of. 3. Π is a foreseeable plaintiff (licensees and invitees; many courts deny strict liability to trespassers, who must prove negligence).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Harm not from the animal's dangerous propensity (tripping over a sleeping tiger) requires negligence. Zoos and public licensees are sometimes held to a negligence standard. Defenses: R-TRT-080.

**Authority:** R2d §§ 506, 507 [VERIFY V-600].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. Tested on a California essay with a chimpanzee (Feb 2019 Q2) [VERIFY V-071].

**Example:** A breeder keeps a "tame" chimpanzee; it bites a visitor who wanders into its room. Strict liability, subject to the visitor's own fault for entering a room he was told to avoid.

### R-TRT-079 — Domestic animals and livestock; California dog-bite statute
**Rule (write this):** A possessor of a domestic animal is strictly liable only if he knew or had reason to know of the animal's abnormally dangerous propensity, and the harm resulted from that propensity; otherwise liability requires negligence. A possessor of livestock is strictly liable for reasonably foreseeable damage done by the livestock trespassing on another's land. In California, a dog owner is strictly liable when the dog bites a person in a public place or lawfully in a private place, regardless of the dog's prior viciousness or the owner's knowledge.

**Elements:** **Common law:** 1. Domestic animal. 2. Δ knew or had reason to know of a dangerous propensity abnormal to its class (prior bites, attacks). 3. Harm from that propensity. **California § 3342:** 1. Δ owns the dog. 2. Bite. 3. Π in a public place or lawfully in a private place (including the owner's property). 4. Damages.

**Exceptions / defenses:** § 3342 covers bites only; other dog-caused injuries (knocking someone down) require the common-law knowledge rule or negligence. Exceptions apply to police and military dogs on duty. Comparative fault and assumption of risk apply; professionals who accept dogs for care (veterinarians, kennel workers) may be barred by primary assumption of risk (*Priebe v. Nelson*) [VERIFY V-611]. Livestock: no strict liability on highways or in fence-out jurisdictions.

**Authority:** R2d §§ 504, 509 [VERIFY V-600]; Civ. Code § 3342.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: the common-law knowledge rule ("one free bite"). California essay: state the common law, then § 3342 (Feb 2019 Q2: a puppy bite) [VERIFY V-071].

**Example:** A puppy with no history of aggression bites a visitor's hand during a home viewing. At common law there is no strict liability (no known propensity), so the visitor must prove negligence; under § 3342 the owner is strictly liable.

### R-TRT-080 — Defenses to strict liability
**Rule (write this):** Ordinary contributory negligence, such as failing to discover or guard against the danger, is not a defense to strict liability. A plaintiff who knowingly and voluntarily encounters the danger assumes the risk, and in comparative-fault jurisdictions the plaintiff's fault reduces recovery.

**Elements:** 1. Contributory negligence in failing to discover: no defense (contributory states). 2. Knowing, unreasonable encounter of the risk: defense (R2d § 524). 3. Assumption of risk: defense (R2d § 523). 4. Comparative fault: reduces damages (most states; California). 5. Abnormally sensitive activity of Π: no strict liability (R2d § 524A).

**Exceptions / defenses:** An unforeseeable intervening act of a third person or force of nature does not, by itself, defeat strict liability for an abnormally dangerous activity (R-TRT-077). Products cases have their own defenses (R-TRT-087).

**Authority:** R2d §§ 523, 524, 524A [VERIFY V-600]; *Daly v. General Motors Corp.* (1978) 20 Cal.3d 725.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: pure comparative fault reduces strict-liability recovery by default. California essay: comparative fault (*Daly*); primary AOR (*Knight*) where inherent.

**Example:** A trespasser who climbs into a fenced blasting zone to watch, despite warning signs, assumed the risk.

## III Products liability

### R-TRT-081 — Strict products liability: the prima facie case
**Rule (write this):** A commercial seller is strictly liable for physical harm caused by a product that was defective when it left the seller's control, if the product reached the user or consumer without substantial change. Liability applies although the seller exercised all possible care and although the plaintiff did not buy from it.

**Elements:** 1. Δ is a commercial supplier (in the business of selling such products) (R-TRT-085). 2. Product defective (manufacturing, design or warning) when it left Δ (R-TRT-082 to R-TRT-084). 3. Product reached Π without substantial change. 4. Defect actually and proximately caused 5. physical injury or damage to other property (R-TRT-086).

**Exceptions / defenses:** R-TRT-087. Majority (R2d § 402A): defect must make the product "unreasonably dangerous"; **California** rejects that requirement (*Cronin*).

**Authority:** *Greenman v. Yuba Power Products, Inc.* (1963) 59 Cal.2d 57; R2d § 402A; R3d PL §§ 1, 2; *Cronin v. J.B.E. Olson Corp.* (1972) 8 Cal.3d 121; *Escola v. Coca Cola Bottling Co.* (1944) 24 Cal.2d 453 (Traynor, J., concurring).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same core. On a California essay, organize theory by theory: strict liability, negligence, warranty, misrepresentation; then defenses (Jul 2023 Q2 selected answers used this order) [VERIFY V-071].

**Example:** A power tool's set screws are too weak, so the wood flies out and strikes the user. The manufacturer is strictly liable even if it used great care, because the tool was defective when sold (*Greenman*).

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: like 产品责任 under the PRC Civil Code (producer strictly liable for a defective product; seller liable to the victim with recourse) (approx.).

### R-TRT-082 — Manufacturing defect
**Rule (write this):** A product has a manufacturing defect when it departs from its intended design, so that it differs from the manufacturer's other units of the same product line, even though all possible care was exercised. A defect may be inferred from circumstances when the incident is of a kind that ordinarily results from a product defect and was not solely the result of other causes.

**Elements:** 1. Unit differs from the intended design or the other units. 2. The deviation existed when the product left Δ. 3. Causation.

**Exceptions / defenses:** State-of-the-art evidence is irrelevant to manufacturing defects. The malfunction doctrine lets Π prove defect without identifying it (R3d PL § 3).

**Authority:** R3d PL §§ 2(a), 3.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** One soda bottle in a batch has a hairline crack from a faulty mold and explodes in a waitress's hand. Manufacturing defect.

### R-TRT-083 — Design defect
**Rule (write this):** A product is defectively designed if it failed to perform as safely as an ordinary consumer would expect when used in an intended or reasonably foreseeable manner, or if the risks of the design outweigh its benefits, as shown by a feasible safer alternative design. In California, under the risk-benefit test, once the plaintiff proves the design was a proximate cause of the injury, the burden shifts to the defendant to prove that the design's benefits outweigh its risks.

**Elements:** **Consumer-expectations test:** ordinary consumer's minimum safety expectations, based on everyday experience. **Risk-utility test:** gravity and likelihood of the danger; feasibility and cost of a safer alternative design; adverse effects of the alternative on the product and the consumer. R3d PL § 2(b): Π must show a **reasonable alternative design** that would have reduced the foreseeable risks.

**Exceptions / defenses:** **California (*Soule*):** the consumer-expectations test applies only where the product's everyday use allows ordinary consumers to form safety expectations; complex technical failures go to risk-benefit. **Prescription drugs:** no strict liability for design defects in California (*Brown v. Superior Court*). **No duty to innovate:** a maker of a nondefective drug has no negligence duty to commercialize a safer alternative sooner (*Gilead Tenofovir Cases*, 2026) [S-709] [VERIFY V-603]. State of the art is relevant to feasibility.

**Authority:** *Barker v. Lull Engineering Co.* (1978) 20 Cal.3d 413; *Soule v. General Motors Corp.* (1994) 8 Cal.4th 548; R3d PL § 2(b); *Brown v. Superior Court* (1988) 44 Cal.3d 1049.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: risk-utility with a feasible alternative design is the usual frame; consumer expectations also appear. California essay: write **both** *Barker* tests, then the burden shift.

**Example:** A loader has no rollbar and tips on a slope, injuring the operator. Consumer expectations are hard to apply to a complex machine (*Soule*), so use risk-benefit: a rollbar was cheap and feasible, and under *Barker* the maker must prove the design's benefits outweigh its risks.

### R-TRT-084 — Warning defect (failure to warn)
**Rule (write this):** A product is defective because of inadequate instructions or warnings when its foreseeable risks of harm, known or reasonably knowable at the time of distribution, could have been reduced or avoided by reasonable warnings, and the omission makes the product not reasonably safe. There is no duty to warn of obvious or generally known risks.

**Elements:** 1. Risk known or reasonably knowable in light of scientific knowledge at distribution (California requires knowability even under strict liability, *Anderson*). 2. Warning absent or inadequate (content, conspicuousness, language). 3. Causation: an adequate warning would have been heeded and prevented the harm (some courts presume heeding). 4. Damages.

**Exceptions / defenses:** **Learned intermediary:** a prescription drug or device maker warns the prescribing physician, not the patient. In California, a plaintiff can show causation by proving that a stronger warning would have changed the physician's conduct, including by being communicated to the patient who then would have declined treatment (*Himes v. Somatics*) [S-708] [VERIFY V-605]. Sophisticated users need no warning of risks they know or should know (*Johnson v. American Standard*). Strict-liability failure to warn applies to prescription drugs in California (*Carlin*).

**Authority:** R3d PL § 2(c); *Anderson v. Owens-Corning Fiberglas Corp.* (1991) 53 Cal.3d 987; *Carlin v. Superior Court* (1996) 13 Cal.4th 1104; *Johnson v. American Standard, Inc.* (2008) 43 Cal.4th 56.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same core rule.

**Example:** A dishwasher detergent leaves a residue that reacts with aluminum cookware, which the maker's own tests revealed; the label says nothing. The risk was knowable, a warning was easy, and a user would have heeded it: warning defect.

### R-TRT-085 — Who is liable: commercial sellers and others
**Rule (write this):** Strict products liability extends to every commercial seller in the chain of distribution, including manufacturers, makers of defective components, distributors, wholesalers and retailers, and to commercial lessors. It does not extend to occasional sellers, providers of services, or, generally, sellers of used goods.

**Elements:** 1. Δ in the business of selling or leasing products of that type. 2. Product, not service (in mixed transactions, the product part). 3. Δ placed the product in the stream of commerce.

**Exceptions / defenses:** **Retailers** are strictly liable (*Vandermark*) but may seek indemnity from the manufacturer. **Lessors:** *Price v. Shell Oil*. **Service providers** (a pharmacist filling a prescription, *Murphy*) and **landlords** (*Peterson*) are not strictly liable. **Component makers** are liable if the component itself is defective or they substantially participated in integrating it; a manufacturer is not liable for harm caused by another company's replacement parts used with its product (*O'Neil*). **Successor corporations:** not liable unless they expressly or impliedly assume liability, there is a de facto merger or mere continuation, or the transfer was fraudulent; California adds the product-line exception (*Ray v. Alad*).

**Authority:** *Vandermark v. Ford Motor Co.* (1964) 61 Cal.2d 256; *Price v. Shell Oil Co.* (1970) 2 Cal.3d 245; *Murphy v. E.R. Squibb & Sons, Inc.* (1985) 40 Cal.3d 672; *Peterson v. Superior Court* (1995) 10 Cal.4th 1185; *O'Neil v. Crane Co.* (2012) 53 Cal.4th 335; *Ray v. Alad Corp.* (1977) 19 Cal.3d 22; R3d PL §§ 5, 8, 20.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A car dealer sells a new car with defective brakes made by the manufacturer. The dealer is strictly liable to the injured buyer even though it could not have found the defect, and it can seek indemnity from the manufacturer.

### R-TRT-086 — Who may recover and for what: plaintiffs, harms, economic loss
**Rule (write this):** Any user, consumer or foreseeable bystander injured by a defective product may recover in strict liability, without privity. Recovery covers personal injury and damage to property other than the product itself; purely economic loss, including damage to the product itself, is recoverable only in warranty or contract.

**Elements:** 1. Π is a user, consumer or foreseeable bystander (*Elmore*). 2. Harm: personal injury or other property. 3. Economic loss rule: no tort recovery for the product's own failure (*Seely*).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Damage to "other property" includes property damaged when a component fails and harms the rest of the system in some courts, but not in others.

**Authority:** *Elmore v. American Motors Corp.* (1969) 70 Cal.2d 578; *Seely v. White Motor Co.* (1965) 63 Cal.2d 9; R3d PL § 21.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A truck's defective brakes fail, and the truck hits a pedestrian and then a parked car. The pedestrian (bystander) and the car owner (other property) recover in strict liability; the truck owner's lost profits from the idle truck lie in warranty.

### R-TRT-087 — Defenses in products cases
**Rule (write this):** A product seller may defend by showing the plaintiff's comparative fault, assumption of the risk, unforeseeable misuse, or a substantial alteration of the product after it left the seller's control. Compliance with government safety standards is evidence that the product was not defective but is not conclusive, unless federal law preempts the state claim.

**Elements:** 1. **Comparative fault** reduces damages (*Daly*), but ordinary failure to discover the defect is not fault in contributory states. 2. **Assumption of risk** (knowing, voluntary encounter of the specific defect). 3. **Misuse:** a defense only if unforeseeable; foreseeable misuse must be designed or warned against. 4. **Alteration:** no liability if a substantial, unforeseeable change caused the harm. 5. **State of the art:** relevant to design and warning (knowability), not manufacturing. 6. **Regulatory compliance:** evidence only (R3d PL § 4(b)). 7. **Federal preemption:** premarket-approved medical devices (*Riegel*); not ordinarily FDA-approved drug labels (*Wyeth*). 8. Sophisticated user or intermediary (*Johnson*; *Webb*). 9. Limitations periods.

**Exceptions / defenses:** In California, subsequent remedial measures are admissible to prove defect in strict liability (*Ault*; Evid. Code § 1151), unlike FRE 407.

**Authority:** *Daly v. General Motors Corp.* (1978) 20 Cal.3d 725; *Webb v. Special Electric Co.* (2016) 63 Cal.4th 167; *Ault v. International Harvester Co.* (1974) 13 Cal.3d 113; *Riegel v. Medtronic, Inc.*, 552 U.S. 312 (2008); *Wyeth v. Levine*, 555 U.S. 555 (2009); R3d PL § 4.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: comparative fault reduces; foreseeable misuse is no defense. California essay: *Daly*; mention *Ault* if evidence of a later redesign appears.

**Example:** A user stands on a folding chair to change a light bulb and it collapses. Standing on chairs is foreseeable misuse, so it is no defense, though the user's comparative fault may reduce recovery.

### R-TRT-088 — Products: negligence
**Rule (write this):** A manufacturer or other supplier who fails to use reasonable care in the manufacture, design, inspection or labeling of a product is liable in negligence to anyone foreseeably injured by it, regardless of privity. Unlike strict liability, the plaintiff must prove the defendant's fault, though res ipsa loquitur and proof of a defect that reasonable care would have prevented often suffice.

**Elements:** 1. Duty to all foreseeable users and bystanders (*MacPherson*). 2. Breach: unreasonable conduct in design, manufacture, inspection or warning. 3. Causation. 4. Damages (physical harm).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Retailers and wholesalers usually have no duty to inspect, unless they have reason to know of the danger. Comparative fault, assumption of risk. Negligence is the theory for non-sellers (a repairer, a used-goods dealer).

**Authority:** *MacPherson v. Buick Motor Co.*, 217 N.Y. 382 (1916); R2d § 395 [VERIFY V-600].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. On an essay, after strict liability, write "Negligence: same defect facts + Δ's failure to use reasonable care".

**Example:** A detergent maker that tested its product on stainless steel but never on aluminum, though aluminum cookware is common, breached the duty of reasonable care in testing.

### R-TRT-089 — Products: warranty
**Rule (write this):** A seller may be liable for breach of an express warranty, of the implied warranty of merchantability, or of the implied warranty of fitness for a particular purpose under UCC Article 2. Liability is strict, but warranty claims are subject to contract defenses such as disclaimer, notice of breach and, in some states, privity limits.

**Elements:** **Express (§ 2-313):** affirmation of fact, promise, description or sample that becomes part of the basis of the bargain (not puffery). **Merchantability (§ 2-314):** merchant seller of goods of that kind; goods not fit for their ordinary purpose. **Fitness (§ 2-315):** seller knows the buyer's particular purpose and that the buyer relies on the seller's skill or judgment. Then: breach, causation, damages, notice within a reasonable time (§ 2-607(3)(a)).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Disclaimers (§ 2-316: "merchantability" mentioned and, if written, conspicuous; fitness disclaimed in a conspicuous writing; "as is"); limiting consequential damages for personal injury from consumer goods is prima facie unconscionable (§ 2-719(3)); horizontal privity (§ 2-318 alternatives); four-year limitations period (§ 2-725).

**Authority:** UCC §§ 2-313, 2-314, 2-315, 2-316, 2-318, 2-607, 2-719, 2-725; see R-CTR-057 to R-CTR-061.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. On a products essay, warranty is its own heading (Jul 2023 Q2) [VERIFY V-071].

**Example:** A detergent labeled "safe on all cookware" damages aluminum pans and causes illness. The label is an express warranty; the product also fails the ordinary-purpose test for merchantability.

### R-TRT-090 — Products: misrepresentation
**Rule (write this):** A seller engaged in the business of selling products who, by advertising, labels or otherwise, makes to the public a misrepresentation of a material fact about the product's character or quality is strictly liable for physical harm to a consumer caused by justifiable reliance on it, even if the misrepresentation was not fraudulent or negligent and there is no privity.

**Elements:** 1. Commercial seller. 2. Public misrepresentation of a material fact (not opinion or puffery) about the product. 3. Justifiable reliance by the consumer (or someone acting for him). 4. Physical harm caused by the reliance.

**Exceptions / defenses:** If the representation was knowing or reckless, intentional misrepresentation (fraud) also applies (R-TRT-107), with punitive damages possible; if careless, negligent misrepresentation.

**Authority:** R2d § 402B.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A helmet maker advertises its bicycle helmet as "crash-tested at 30 mph." It was never tested and cracks at 15 mph. A rider who bought it in reliance recovers even if the maker honestly believed the claim.

### R-TRT-091 — Products: intentional-tort theory
**Rule (write this):** A product seller is liable for battery or another intentional tort only if it acted with the purpose of causing harmful contact or knew to a substantial certainty that its product would cause such contact to the plaintiff. Knowing that a product carries a statistical risk of injury to some users is usually not enough.

**Elements:** 1. Purpose or substantial-certainty knowledge (R-TRT-001). 2. Harmful contact or other intentional-tort elements. 3. Causation.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Where the seller's knowledge is shown, the facts usually also support punitive damages on the strict-liability and negligence claims (R-TRT-114).

**Authority:** R2d § 8A; *Garratt v. Dailey*, 46 Wash.2d 197 (1955).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** On a California essay, mention the theory briefly and conclude it likely fails unless Δ knew of the specific danger (Jul 2023 Q2 selected answer) [VERIFY V-071].

**Example:** A maker knows that 1 in 10,000 of its space heaters overheats. That statistical knowledge is not substantial certainty as to any given buyer, so battery fails; the claim lies in strict liability and negligence.

## IV.A Nuisance

### R-TRT-092 — Private nuisance
**Rule (write this):** A private nuisance is a nontrespassory invasion of another's interest in the private use and enjoyment of land that is substantial and unreasonable. The interference is unreasonable if the gravity of the harm outweighs the utility of the defendant's conduct, or if the harm is serious and the defendant could compensate for it and still carry on.

**Elements:** 1. Π has a possessory interest in land (owner, tenant). 2. Interference with use and enjoyment (noise, odor, smoke, vibration, light, pollution). 3. **Substantial:** offensive, inconvenient or annoying to a normal person in the community (not a hypersensitive one). 4. **Unreasonable:** gravity of harm (extent, character, social value of Π's use, suitability to the locality, burden on Π to avoid) vs. utility of Δ's conduct (social value, suitability to locality, impracticability of preventing harm). 5. Δ's conduct intentional (knows the interference is occurring), negligent, reckless or abnormally dangerous.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Trespass protects exclusive possession; nuisance protects use and enjoyment. Defenses: R-TRT-094. California defines a nuisance broadly as anything injurious to health, indecent or offensive to the senses, or an obstruction to the free use of property that interferes with comfortable enjoyment of life or property (Civ. Code § 3479); a nuisance not public is private (§ 3481).

**Authority:** R2d §§ 821D, 822, 826, 827, 828; Civ. Code §§ 3479, 3481; *San Diego Gas & Electric Co. v. Superior Court* (1996) 13 Cal.4th 893.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. California essays pair nuisance with injunction remedies (Feb 2022 Q3, Feb 2018 Q3) [VERIFY V-071].

**Example:** An outdoor theater's amplified concerts until midnight shake windows in a nearby subdivision. A normal person would find this substantial; whether it is unreasonable turns on the theater's value, its location and the cost of sound barriers.

### R-TRT-093 — Public nuisance
**Rule (write this):** A public nuisance is an unreasonable interference with a right common to the general public, such as public health, safety, peace, comfort or convenience. A public official may sue to abate it; a private person may recover only if he suffers harm different in kind, not merely in degree, from that suffered by the public generally.

**Elements:** 1. Interference with a public right (blocking a highway, polluting a river, a drug house, an illegal dump). 2. Unreasonable (often shown by violation of a statute or ordinance). 3. Private plaintiff: special injury different in kind (a business whose only access is blocked; a commercial fisherman whose catch is destroyed).

**Exceptions / defenses:** California: a public nuisance affects an entire community or neighborhood, or any considerable number of persons, at the same time (Civ. Code § 3480); a private person may sue if it is specially injurious to himself (§ 3493); no lapse of time legalizes a public nuisance (§ 3490).

**Authority:** R2d §§ 821B, 821C; Civ. Code §§ 3480, 3490, 3493.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A factory dumps waste into a public lake. Swimmers who can no longer swim have no private claim; a resort whose business collapses has special injury.

### R-TRT-094 — Nuisance defenses
**Rule (write this):** Coming to the nuisance, by moving next to an existing activity, does not bar a nuisance claim but is a factor in deciding whether the interference is unreasonable. Compliance with zoning is likewise relevant but not conclusive, and statutory authorization is a defense only if the statute expressly authorizes the specific act complained of.

**Elements:** 1. Coming to the nuisance: factor (R2d § 840D) [VERIFY V-600]. 2. Zoning and permits: evidence of reasonableness, not a defense. 3. Statutory authorization: nothing done under the express authority of a statute is a nuisance (Civ. Code § 3482), construed narrowly. 4. Contributory or comparative negligence: a defense only to a negligence-based nuisance. 5. Right-to-farm statutes protect established agricultural operations (Civ. Code § 3482.5) [VERIFY V-609].

**Exceptions / defenses:** Courts may deny an injunction but award damages where Π came to the nuisance.

**Authority:** R2d § 840D [VERIFY V-600]; Civ. Code §§ 3482, 3482.5 [VERIFY V-609].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. A California essay tested a homeowner who bought next to an existing theater (Feb 2022 Q3) [VERIFY V-071].

**Example:** P buys a house next to a 30-year-old pig farm and sues over the smell. Coming to the nuisance weighs against P, and a right-to-farm statute may bar the claim.

### R-TRT-095 — Nuisance remedies
**Rule (write this):** A nuisance plaintiff may recover damages for past harm and, if the nuisance is permanent, for the diminished value of the land; may obtain an injunction if damages are inadequate and the balance of hardships favors relief; and may abate a private nuisance by reasonable self-help after notice.

**Elements:** 1. Damages: permanent nuisance (one action for past and future loss) vs. continuing nuisance (successive actions for each period). 2. Injunction: inadequate legal remedy, balance of hardships, public interest; courts may award permanent damages instead (*Boomer*). 3. Self-help: reasonable force, after notice, no breach of the peace.

**Exceptions / defenses:** California distinguishes permanent and continuing nuisances for limitations and damages (R-REM-024).

**Authority:** *Boomer v. Atlantic Cement Co.*, 26 N.Y.2d 219 (1970); Civ. Code § 3501 [VERIFY V-609]; R-REM-024, R-REM-055, R-REM-060, R-REM-062.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. On a Torts/Remedies crossover, finish the nuisance analysis and then hand off to the Remedies template.

**Example:** A cement plant's dust damages neighbors' homes. An injunction would close a plant employing 300 workers, so the court may award permanent damages instead.

## IV.B Defamation and privacy

### R-TRT-096 — Defamation: common-law elements
**Rule (write this):** A defendant is liable for defamation if he publishes to a third person a false and defamatory statement of fact of and concerning the plaintiff, causing damage. A statement is defamatory if it tends to harm the plaintiff's reputation, lowering him in the estimation of the community or deterring others from associating or dealing with him.

**Elements:** 1. Defamatory language (judged by its natural meaning to a reasonable recipient; innuendo with extrinsic facts allowed). 2. Of and concerning Π (colloquium; a small group can sue, members of a large group cannot). 3. Publication to at least one third person, intentional or negligent; each repetition is a new publication, and repeaters are liable. 4. Damages (presumed or special, R-TRT-097). 5. Where the speech involves a public figure or a matter of public concern, falsity and fault (R-TRT-098, R-TRT-099).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Only living persons, and entities, can be defamed. Online platforms are not treated as the publisher of content posted by users (47 U.S.C. § 230(c)(1)). Defenses: R-TRT-100.

**Authority:** R2d §§ 558, 559, 564, 577 [VERIFY V-600]; Civ. Code §§ 44, 45, 46.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule, plus the constitutional overlay. Tested on California essays (Feb 2015 Q5 libel, Feb 2020 Q1 professor and student) [VERIFY V-071].

**Example:** A professor tells a hiring committee that a former student "cheated on every exam." The statement is factual, defamatory, about the student and published to third persons.

### R-TRT-097 — Libel, slander and per se categories; damages
**Rule (write this):** Libel is defamation in written, printed or similarly permanent form, and general damages are presumed. Slander is spoken defamation and requires proof of special (pecuniary) damages unless it is slander per se: a statement imputing a serious crime, a loathsome disease, misconduct in the plaintiff's business or profession, or serious sexual misconduct.

**Elements:** 1. Classify the form (libel vs. slander; broadcasts are usually treated as libel). 2. Libel: general damages presumed (majority). 3. Slander: special damages required unless per se. 4. Once special damages are shown, general damages are also recoverable.

**Exceptions / defenses:** **Libel per quod** (defamatory meaning appears only from extrinsic facts): some states require special damages unless the statement fits a slander-per-se category. **California:** libel defamatory on its face needs no special damages; libel not defamatory on its face requires proof of special damages (Civ. Code § 45a). California classifies communications by radio and similar mechanical means as slander (Civ. Code § 46) [VERIFY V-609], and its slander-per-se categories include imputing a crime, an infectious, contagious or loathsome disease, matters injuring the plaintiff in his office, profession, trade or business, impotence or want of chastity (Civ. Code § 46). Constitutional limits on presumed damages: R-TRT-098, R-TRT-099.

**Authority:** R2d §§ 568, 569, 570, 575 [VERIFY V-600]; Civ. Code §§ 45, 45a, 46.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule; on a California essay cite §§ 45a and 46.

**Example:** At a dinner party D says P, a surgeon, "operates drunk." That is slander per se (profession), so P recovers general damages without proving lost income.

### R-TRT-098 — Public officials and public figures: actual malice
**Rule (write this):** A public official or public figure suing for defamation must prove by clear and convincing evidence that the statement was false and that the defendant made it with actual malice, meaning knowledge of its falsity or reckless disregard for whether it was true. Reckless disregard means the defendant in fact entertained serious doubts about the truth.

**Elements:** 1. Π is a public official (government employee with substantial responsibility for public affairs) or public figure: **all-purpose** (pervasive fame or notoriety) or **limited-purpose** (voluntarily injected himself into a particular public controversy, for statements related to it). 2. Falsity (Π's burden). 3. Actual malice (subjective; failure to investigate alone is not enough). 4. Clear and convincing evidence.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Ill will or hostility is not "actual malice". The rule extends to IIED claims by public figures over publications (*Hustler*) and to false-light claims involving public matters.

**Authority:** *New York Times Co. v. Sullivan*, 376 U.S. 254 (1964); *Curtis Publishing Co. v. Butts*, 388 U.S. 130 (1967); *St. Amant v. Thompson*, 390 U.S. 727 (1968); *Gertz v. Robert Welch, Inc.*, 418 U.S. 323 (1974).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule (federal constitutional law binds both).

**Example:** A newspaper reports that a city council member took bribes, relying on a single anonymous tip it privately doubted. Publishing despite serious doubts is reckless disregard: actual malice.

### R-TRT-099 — Private figures (Gertz; Dun & Bradstreet)
**Rule (write this):** A private-figure plaintiff suing over a statement on a matter of public concern must prove the statement false and the defendant at least negligent as to its falsity, and may recover presumed or punitive damages only by proving actual malice; otherwise recovery is limited to actual injury. Where the statement involves a purely private matter, the Constitution does not bar presumed and punitive damages without actual malice.

**Elements:** **Private figure + public concern:** falsity (Π's burden, *Hepps*), fault at least negligence (state choice), actual injury (including reputational harm and emotional distress), presumed/punitive only with actual malice. **Private figure + private concern:** common-law rules apply; presumed and punitive damages available without actual malice (*Dun & Bradstreet*).

**Exceptions / defenses:** California requires a private-figure plaintiff to prove negligence (*Brown v. Kelly Broadcasting*) [S-717].

**Authority:** *Gertz v. Robert Welch, Inc.*, 418 U.S. 323 (1974); *Dun & Bradstreet, Inc. v. Greenmoss Builders, Inc.*, 472 U.S. 749 (1985); *Philadelphia Newspapers, Inc. v. Hepps*, 475 U.S. 767 (1986); *Brown v. Kelly Broadcasting Co.* (1989) 48 Cal.3d 711 [S-717].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. Build the 2 × 2 grid (public/private plaintiff × public/private concern) on every defamation essay.

**Example:** A TV station negligently misidentifies P, a private contractor, as the subject of a consumer-fraud investigation. P must show negligence and actual damages; presumed damages require actual malice.

### R-TRT-100 — Defamation defenses: truth, opinion and privileges
**Rule (write this):** Truth, including substantial truth, is a complete defense, and a statement of pure opinion that does not imply false facts is not actionable. Absolute privileges protect statements in judicial, legislative and certain executive proceedings and between spouses; qualified privileges protect statements made in good faith to protect a legitimate interest of the speaker, the recipient or the public, and fair reports of official proceedings, but are lost by abuse.

**Elements:** 1. **Truth** (substantial truth; burden on Π where falsity is constitutionally required). 2. **Opinion:** no separate privilege; the question is whether the statement asserts or implies a provably false fact (*Milkovich*); rhetorical hyperbole is protected. 3. **Absolute:** judicial proceedings (judges, lawyers, parties, witnesses, if related to the proceeding), legislative proceedings, high executive officials, spouses, compelled publications. 4. **Qualified:** common interest, protection of the publisher's or recipient's interest (employment references, credit reports), reports to police, fair and accurate reports of public proceedings. 5. **Abuse** defeats a qualified privilege: knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard (or, in some states, ill will), excessive publication, or an improper purpose. 6. Consent.

**Exceptions / defenses:** **California (Civ. Code § 47):** (a) official duty; (b) legislative, judicial and other official proceedings (the absolute litigation privilege, which bars all torts based on the communication except malicious prosecution, *Silberg*); (c) communications without malice to an interested person; (d) fair and true reports of public proceedings; (e) fair and true reports of public meetings. **Retraction (Civ. Code § 48a):** for statements in a daily or weekly news publication or broadcast, Π recovers only special damages unless he demanded a correction within 20 days of learning of the publication and it was not published [S-713] [S-706] [VERIFY V-606]. **Anti-SLAPP (CCP § 425.16):** a procedural motion to strike claims arising from protected speech unless Π shows a probability of prevailing.

**Authority:** *Milkovich v. Lorain Journal Co.*, 497 U.S. 1 (1990); R2d §§ 581A, 585–592A, 594–598, 611 [VERIFY V-600]; Civ. Code §§ 47, 48a; *Silberg v. Anderson* (1990) 50 Cal.3d 205; CCP § 425.16.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same core rules; on a California essay cite § 47 and mention anti-SLAPP in one line.

**Example:** A former employer tells a prospective employer, in good faith, that P was fired for "unexplained cash shortages." That is a qualified common-interest privilege; it is lost only if the employer knew the statement was false or recklessly disregarded the truth.

### R-TRT-101 — Privacy: appropriation of name or likeness
**Rule (write this):** A defendant who appropriates the plaintiff's name or likeness for his own use or benefit, usually commercial, without consent is liable for invasion of privacy. In California, a statute also gives a remedy against anyone who knowingly uses another's name, voice, signature, photograph or likeness in products or advertising without consent.

**Elements:** **Common law:** 1. Use of Π's identity. 2. For Δ's advantage (commercial or otherwise). 3. Lack of consent. 4. Injury. **Civ. Code § 3344:** knowing use for advertising or selling, without consent; damages of the greater of a statutory minimum or actual damages, plus profits attributable to the use.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Newsworthiness: use in news, public affairs, sports broadcasts or political campaigns is exempt; incidental use is not actionable. First Amendment: an artistic work that is transformative is protected (*Comedy III*). Deceased personalities: Civ. Code § 3344.1.

**Authority:** R2d § 652C; Civ. Code §§ 3344, 3344.1; *Comedy III Productions, Inc. v. Gary Saderup, Inc.* (2001) 25 Cal.4th 387.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A sports-drink company puts a well-known surfer's photo on its billboard without permission. Appropriation, and a § 3344 claim.

### R-TRT-102 — Privacy: intrusion upon seclusion
**Rule (write this):** A defendant who intentionally intrudes, physically or otherwise, upon the solitude or seclusion of another or his private affairs is liable if the intrusion would be highly offensive to a reasonable person. No publication is required.

**Elements:** 1. Intentional intrusion (physical entry, eavesdropping, wiretapping, peering through windows, hacking). 2. Into a place, conversation or matter in which Π has a reasonable expectation of privacy. 3. Highly offensive to a reasonable person (degree, context, motive).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Observing a person in a public place is not intrusion. Consent. California also recognizes a constitutional privacy right enforceable against private parties (Cal. Const. art. I, § 1): legally protected privacy interest, reasonable expectation, serious invasion, balanced against countervailing interests (*Hill v. NCAA*).

**Authority:** R2d § 652B; *Shulman v. Group W Productions, Inc.* (1998) 18 Cal.4th 200; *Hill v. National Collegiate Athletic Assn.* (1994) 7 Cal.4th 1.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A landlord installs a hidden camera in a tenant's bathroom. Intrusion, even if he never shows the footage to anyone.

### R-TRT-103 — Privacy: public disclosure of private facts
**Rule (write this):** A defendant who gives publicity to a matter concerning the private life of another is liable if the matter is not of legitimate public concern and its publication would be highly offensive to a reasonable person. Truth is not a defense.

**Elements:** 1. Publicity (communication to the public at large or to so many persons that it is substantially certain to become public). 2. Private facts (not already public). 3. Highly offensive to a reasonable person. 4. Not newsworthy (not of legitimate public concern).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Newsworthiness; matters of public record (truthful publication of lawfully obtained public-record information is constitutionally protected); consent.

**Authority:** R2d § 652D; *Shulman v. Group W Productions, Inc.* (1998) 18 Cal.4th 200.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A hospital clerk posts on social media that a named neighbor is being treated for a sexually transmitted disease. Public disclosure of private facts.

### R-TRT-104 — Privacy: false light
**Rule (write this):** A defendant who gives publicity to a matter that places the plaintiff before the public in a false light is liable if the false light would be highly offensive to a reasonable person and the defendant knew of or acted in reckless disregard of the falsity and the false light in which the plaintiff would be placed.

**Elements:** 1. Publicity. 2. False or misleading portrayal (including by juxtaposition). 3. Highly offensive to a reasonable person. 4. Fault: actual malice where the matter is of public interest (*Time, Inc. v. Hill*); some states accept negligence for private matters.

**Exceptions / defenses:** The defamation privileges and constitutional limits apply. Some states do not recognize false light. In California, a false-light claim that duplicates a defamation claim is subject to the same requirements.

**Authority:** R2d § 652E; *Time, Inc. v. Hill*, 385 U.S. 374 (1967).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A magazine illustrates an article on deadbeat parents with a stock photo of P and her child. P is placed in a highly offensive false light.

## Misuse of legal process (intentional injuries to reputation or similar interests)

### R-TRT-105 — Malicious prosecution and wrongful civil proceedings
**Rule (write this):** A defendant is liable for malicious prosecution if he instituted or continued criminal proceedings against the plaintiff without probable cause and for an improper purpose, and the proceedings terminated in the plaintiff's favor, causing damage. Most states, including California, apply the same tort to civil proceedings brought without probable cause and with malice.

**Elements:** 1. Institution or continuation of proceedings by Δ (a person who knowingly gives false information to police "institutes"). 2. Favorable termination reflecting on the merits (not a settlement or procedural dismissal). 3. No probable cause (California: an objective question for the court, whether any reasonable attorney would have thought the claim tenable, *Sheldon Appel*). 4. Malice (any purpose other than bringing an offender to justice or adjudicating a claim). 5. Damages.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Continuing a suit after discovering it lacks probable cause is actionable (*Zamos*). Prosecutors have absolute immunity. Some states require "special injury" for civil-proceeding claims; California does not [VERIFY V-610]. The § 47(b) litigation privilege does not bar malicious prosecution.

**Authority:** R2d §§ 653, 674 [VERIFY V-600]; *Sheldon Appel Co. v. Albert & Oliker* (1989) 47 Cal.3d 863; *Zamos v. Stroud* (2004) 32 Cal.4th 958.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same core rule.

**Example:** A business rival files a groundless fraud suit to scare off P's investors, and loses on summary judgment. P may sue for malicious prosecution of a civil action.

### R-TRT-106 — Abuse of process
**Rule (write this):** A defendant is liable for abuse of process if he willfully uses legal process, properly issued, primarily to accomplish an ulterior purpose for which it was not designed, through an act not proper in the regular conduct of the proceeding.

**Elements:** 1. Ulterior purpose. 2. Willful act in the use of process not proper in the regular conduct of the proceeding (a subpoena or attachment used as leverage to extort payment unrelated to the suit). 3. Damages.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Filing a suit with bad motives is not enough; there must be a misuse of process after issuance. In California the litigation privilege bars abuse-of-process claims based on communicative acts in litigation [VERIFY V-610].

**Authority:** R2d § 682 [VERIFY V-600].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A creditor attaches P's business bank account in a $500 dispute and offers to release it only if P signs an unrelated non-compete. Abuse of process.

## IV.C Misrepresentation

### R-TRT-107 — Intentional misrepresentation (fraud, deceit)
**Rule (write this):** A defendant is liable for intentional misrepresentation if he makes a false representation of material fact, knowing it is false or with reckless disregard for its truth, intending to induce the plaintiff's reliance, and the plaintiff justifiably relies on it and suffers pecuniary damage.

**Elements:** 1. Misrepresentation of fact (words, conduct, active concealment, a half-truth; opinions count if Δ has superior knowledge, is a fiduciary, or the opinion implies facts; a promise made without intent to perform is promissory fraud). 2. Scienter: knowledge or recklessness. 3. Intent to induce reliance. 4. Actual reliance. 5. Justifiable reliance (no duty to investigate unless the falsity is obvious). 6. Pecuniary damage (out-of-pocket or benefit of the bargain).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Π's contributory negligence is not a defense; unjustifiable reliance is. Puffery is not fact. California defines deceit to include suggestion of a false fact, an assertion without reasonable ground (negligent misrepresentation), suppression of a fact by one bound to disclose, and a promise made without intent to perform (Civ. Code §§ 1709, 1710). Damages: Civ. Code § 3343 (property transactions) and R-REM-023.

**Authority:** R2d §§ 525, 526, 537, 538, 540, 541 [VERIFY V-600]; Civ. Code §§ 1709, 1710; *Lazar v. Superior Court* (1996) 12 Cal.4th 631; *Alliance Mortgage Co. v. Rothwell* (1995) 10 Cal.4th 1226 [S-453].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. Fraud has appeared on California essays with remedies calls (Feb 2014 Q6, Jul 2024 Q5) [VERIFY V-071].

**Example:** A seller tells a buyer a house "has never flooded," knowing the basement flooded twice. The buyer, who had no reason to doubt it, buys and suffers flood damage. Fraud.

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: compare 欺诈 as a ground to rescind a contract under the PRC Civil Code; US fraud is also an independent tort with tort damages and possibly punitive damages (approx.).

### R-TRT-108 — Fraudulent concealment and nondisclosure; Rattagan
**Rule (write this):** Active concealment of a material fact is treated as a misrepresentation. Mere silence is generally not actionable unless the defendant has a duty to disclose, as where he is a fiduciary, made a misleading half-truth, learned that an earlier statement has become false, or knows material facts the plaintiff cannot reasonably discover. In California, a contracting party may sue in tort for fraudulent concealment during contract performance if the tort's elements exist independently of the contract and the conduct exposes the plaintiff to a risk of harm beyond what the parties reasonably contemplated when they contracted.

**Elements:** 1. Concealment or suppression of a material fact. 2. Duty to disclose (or active concealment). 3. Intent to defraud. 4. Π unaware of the fact and would have acted differently. 5. Damage.

**Exceptions / defenses:** **Economic loss rule:** generally bars tort claims for purely economic loss from a contracting party's failure to perform, but not fraud claims meeting the *Rattagan* test [S-132]; see R-CTR-117 and R-REM-032. California sellers of residential property must disclose known facts materially affecting value that the buyer cannot reasonably observe (*Lingsch v. Savage*) [VERIFY V-611].

**Authority:** R2d §§ 550, 551 [VERIFY V-600]; Civ. Code § 1710(3); *Rattagan v. Uber Technologies, Inc.* (2024) 17 Cal.5th 1 [S-132]; *Robinson Helicopter Co., Inc. v. Dana Corp.* (2004) 34 Cal.4th 979.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: the common-law duty-to-disclose categories. California essay: add *Rattagan* whenever a contracting party hid information during performance.

**Example:** A software vendor under a support contract learns its product has exposed the client's customer data, hides it for six months and keeps billing. Under *Rattagan* the client may sue in fraudulent concealment, beyond contract, because the concealment created risks (regulatory fines, customer claims) outside what the parties contemplated.

### R-TRT-109 — Negligent misrepresentation
**Rule (write this):** One who, in the course of his business or profession or a transaction in which he has a pecuniary interest, supplies false information for the guidance of others in their business transactions is liable for pecuniary loss caused by their justifiable reliance if he failed to exercise reasonable care in obtaining or communicating the information. Liability extends only to the persons for whose guidance he intends to supply the information, or knows the recipient intends to supply it, in the kind of transaction he intends to influence.

**Elements:** 1. Δ supplies information in a business or professional capacity. 2. False information. 3. Lack of reasonable care. 4. Π within the limited class of intended recipients. 5. Justifiable reliance. 6. Pecuniary loss (out-of-pocket).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Comparative negligence **is** a defense (unlike fraud). **California:** auditors are liable in negligence only to their client; third parties may sue for negligent misrepresentation only if they were specifically intended beneficiaries of the audit report (*Bily*). California requires a positive assertion, not an omission or implied assertion; an honest but unreasonable belief is enough (Civ. Code § 1710(2)).

**Authority:** R2d § 552; *Bily v. Arthur Young & Co.* (1992) 3 Cal.4th 370; Civ. Code § 1710(2).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A surveyor hired by a seller carelessly misstates the lot size in a report he knows will be shown to buyers. A buyer who relied on it recovers the overpayment.

## IV.D Intentional interference with business relations

### R-TRT-110 — Intentional interference with contractual relations
**Rule (write this):** A defendant who intentionally and improperly interferes with a valid contract between the plaintiff and a third person, by inducing or causing the third person not to perform or by making performance more costly, is liable for the plaintiff's resulting loss.

**Elements:** 1. Valid contract between Π and a third party. 2. Δ's knowledge of the contract. 3. Intentional acts designed to induce breach or disruption. 4. Actual breach or disruption (California: disruption suffices, *PG&E v. Bear Stearns*). 5. Damages. 6. Improper interference (R2d § 767 factors: nature of the conduct, motive, interests interfered with and advanced, social interests, proximity, relations).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Privileges: legitimate competition for **at-will** contracts by proper means (R2d § 768); protecting a financial interest or a third person's welfare; giving truthful information or honest advice. **California:** interference with an **at-will** contract requires an independently wrongful act (*Ixchel*) [S-716]; a party to the contract cannot be liable in tort for interfering with its own contract (*Applied Equipment*). Damages include contract value, consequential loss, emotional distress and punitive damages.

**Authority:** R2d §§ 766, 767, 768; *Pacific Gas & Electric Co. v. Bear Stearns & Co.* (1990) 50 Cal.3d 1118; *Ixchel Pharma, LLC v. Biogen, Inc.* (2020) 9 Cal.5th 1130 [S-716]; *Applied Equipment Corp. v. Litton Saudi Arabia Ltd.* (1994) 7 Cal.4th 503; *Reeves v. Hanlon* (2004) 33 Cal.4th 1140.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same core rule; NCBE inclusion of IV.D on the MBE is unconfirmed [VERIFY V-023].

**Example:** A rival studio, knowing an actor has a two-year exclusive contract with P, offers him double pay to break it. Interference with contract; the studio's competitive motive does not privilege inducing breach of a fixed-term contract.

### R-TRT-111 — Intentional interference with prospective economic advantage
**Rule (write this):** A defendant is liable for intentionally and improperly interfering with the plaintiff's reasonably probable future economic relationship with a third person, causing its loss. In California, the plaintiff must also prove the defendant's conduct was independently wrongful, meaning proscribed by some constitutional, statutory, regulatory, common-law or other determinable legal standard apart from the interference itself.

**Elements:** 1. Economic relationship between Π and a third party with the probability of future economic benefit. 2. Δ's knowledge. 3. Intentional (or, in California, negligent where a special relationship exists, *J'Aire*) acts designed to disrupt. 4. Independently wrongful act (California: *Della Penna*; *Korea Supply*). 5. Actual disruption. 6. Damages.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Lawful competition by proper means is privileged. Wrongful means include fraud, defamation, threats, violence, unfair competition and antitrust violations.

**Authority:** R2d § 766B; *Della Penna v. Toyota Motor Sales, U.S.A., Inc.* (1995) 11 Cal.4th 376; *Korea Supply Co. v. Lockheed Martin Corp.* (2003) 29 Cal.4th 1134; *J'Aire Corp. v. Gregory* (1979) 24 Cal.3d 799.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same core; the independently-wrongful requirement is California's distinctive point.

**Example:** D spreads false rumors that P's bakery failed a health inspection, and a hotel that was about to sign a supply deal with P backs out. The false rumors are independently wrongful (defamation, trade libel): liability.

### R-TRT-112 — Injurious falsehood (trade libel, slander of title)
**Rule (write this):** A defendant is liable for injurious falsehood if he publishes a false statement disparaging the quality of the plaintiff's goods, services or business, or the plaintiff's title to property, knowing it is false or with reckless disregard for its truth, and the statement causes the plaintiff pecuniary loss.

**Elements:** 1. False statement of fact disparaging Π's product, business or title. 2. Publication to a third person. 3. Knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard (R2d § 623A). 4. Special damages (specific lost sales or deals) must be proved.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Statements about Π's personal character are defamation instead. Competitors may make general comparative claims (puffery). The defamation privileges apply.

**Authority:** R2d §§ 623A, 624, 626 [VERIFY V-600].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule; rarely tested.

**Example:** A competitor tells retailers that P's car seats "failed federal crash tests," knowing they passed. Retailers cancel orders worth $200,000. Trade libel.

## Damages in tort (cross-cutting)

### R-TRT-113 — Compensatory damages in tort
**Rule (write this):** A tort plaintiff recovers compensatory damages for all detriment proximately caused by the tort: economic damages such as medical expenses and lost earnings, and non-economic damages such as pain and suffering and emotional distress. Nominal damages are available for intentional torts without actual harm, but not for negligence.

**Elements:** 1. Economic: past and future medical costs, lost earnings and earning capacity, property damage (repair cost or diminution in value). 2. Non-economic: pain, suffering, disfigurement, emotional distress, loss of enjoyment. 3. Future losses reduced to present value. 4. Reasonable certainty.

**Exceptions / defenses:** **Collateral source rule:** payments from independent sources (insurance) do not reduce recovery (*Helfend*); in California, past medical damages are limited to amounts actually paid or incurred (*Howell*) [S-472]. **Avoidable consequences:** Π must mitigate. **Eggshell plaintiff:** R-TRT-045. **California limits:** Prop 51 (R-TRT-042); MICRA (R-TRT-115); uninsured motorists and convicted drunk drivers cannot recover non-economic damages in motor-vehicle cases (Civ. Code § 3333.4) [S-720] [VERIFY V-608].

**Authority:** Civ. Code § 3333 [S-469]; *Helfend v. Southern Cal. Rapid Transit Dist.* (1970) 2 Cal.3d 1; *Howell v. Hamilton Meats & Provisions, Inc.* (2011) 52 Cal.4th 541 [S-472]; see R-REM-007 to R-REM-025.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule; a California Torts essay ends each claim with a short damages paragraph.

**Example:** P, injured by D's negligence, has $40,000 in medical bills (paid by insurance), $20,000 lost wages and severe pain. P recovers all three categories; the insurance payment does not reduce the award.

### R-TRT-114 — Punitive damages in tort
**Rule (write this):** Punitive damages may be awarded to punish and deter where the defendant acted with malice, oppression or fraud, or with conscious disregard of the rights or safety of others. In California, the plaintiff must prove oppression, fraud or malice by clear and convincing evidence, and an employer is liable for punitive damages based on an employee's conduct only if an officer, director or managing agent had advance knowledge and consciously disregarded it, authorized or ratified it, or was personally guilty of it.

**Elements:** 1. Underlying tort with actual damages. 2. **Malice** (intent to injure, or despicable conduct with willful and conscious disregard of rights or safety), **oppression** (despicable conduct subjecting Π to cruel and unjust hardship in conscious disregard of rights) or **fraud**. 3. Clear and convincing evidence (Civ. Code § 3294). 4. Evidence of Δ's financial condition (*Adams v. Murakami*) [S-464]. 5. Due process: ratio and reprehensibility limits (*Gore*; *State Farm*).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Not available for breach of contract alone, in wrongful-death actions (beneficiaries' claim), or against public entities (Gov. Code § 818) [VERIFY V-607]. Available in a survival action (CCP § 377.34).

**Authority:** Civ. Code § 3294 [S-451]; *BMW of North America, Inc. v. Gore*, 517 U.S. 559 (1996); *State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co. v. Campbell*, 538 U.S. 408 (2003); R-REM-026 to R-REM-030.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same core; cite § 3294 on a California essay.

**Example:** A manufacturer learns its heater overheats, decides a recall costs more than expected lawsuits, and keeps selling it. That is conscious disregard of safety: punitive damages.

### R-TRT-115 — California MICRA: medical negligence damages limits
**Rule (write this):** In a California action against a health care provider based on professional negligence, non-economic damages are capped by Civil Code § 3333.2 as amended by AB 35. From January 1, 2023, the caps were $350,000 for non-death cases and $500,000 for wrongful-death cases, rising each January 1 by $40,000 and $50,000 respectively until they reach $750,000 and $1,000,000, then adjusting 2% a year; on that schedule, the caps for 2027 are $510,000 and $700,000.

**Elements:** 1. Δ is a health care provider. 2. Claim is based on professional negligence. 3. Non-economic damages only (economic damages uncapped). 4. Separate caps may apply to health care providers, to health care institutions, and to unaffiliated providers or institutions, so up to three caps per case [VERIFY V-601]. 5. The applicable amount is reported to depend on when the judgment, award or settlement is entered [VERIFY V-601].

**Exceptions / defenses:** Claims that are not "professional negligence" (e.g., certain elder-abuse claims based on custodial neglect) fall outside the cap. Related MICRA rules: limitations period (CCP § 340.5); collateral-source evidence (Civ. Code § 3333.1); periodic payments (CCP § 667.7); contingency-fee limits (Bus. & Prof. Code § 6146).

**Authority:** Civ. Code § 3333.2 [S-701]; AB 35 (Stats. 2022) [S-702]; [S-703].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Not MBE material. California essay: mention in one line if a doctor or hospital is sued in negligence.

**Example:** A surgeon's negligence in 2026 leaves P with chronic pain; judgment is entered in 2027 with $2 million in non-economic damages. P's non-economic recovery against the surgeon is reduced to the applicable cap (on the AB 35 schedule, $510,000 in 2027) [VERIFY V-601].
