# Torts — comprehensive outline

**What this file is.** The complete reference for Torts. Headings follow the NCBE MBE Subject Matter Outline numbering exactly [S-040] (some sub-item wording is approximate [VERIFY V-023]). The State Bar's essay outline topics [S-092] are placed where their content lives; their numbering is provisional [VERIFY V-024]. Each section gives the rule with its authority, the splits, a `CA note:` where California differs, and how the topic is tested. Essay-ready wording is in [`rule-statements.md`](rule-statements.md) (cited as R-TRT-###); California contrasts are in [`ca-distinctions.md`](ca-distinctions.md).

**Abbreviations.** R2d = Restatement (Second) of Torts; R3d PEH = Restatement (Third) of Torts: Liability for Physical and Emotional Harm; R3d PL = Restatement (Third) of Torts: Products Liability; Π = plaintiff; Δ = defendant. Less common Restatement section numbers carry [VERIFY V-600].

**Which law to use.**
- **MBE:** generally accepted principles. NCBE tells you to assume that survival and wrongful-death actions are available and that **joint and several liability with pure comparative negligence** applies unless the question says otherwise [S-040].
- **California essay:** general principles unless the call asks for California law [S-077]. Where California differs on a point the facts raise, add one sentence giving the California rule.

**Weight.** About half of the 25 scored Torts MBE questions come from II Negligence; the other half from I, III and IV together [S-040]. Torts was a California essay subject on 12 of 31 administrations, 2012–2026 ([`../../06-past-exams/frequency-analysis.md`](../../06-past-exams/frequency-analysis.md)).

## I Intentional torts

**Common features.**
- **Intent** means purpose or knowledge to a substantial certainty (R2d § 8A; *Garratt v. Dailey*, 46 Wash.2d 197 (1955)). Motive is irrelevant. Children and mentally ill persons can intend (R-TRT-001).
- **Transferred intent** applies among the five "trespass" torts: assault, battery, false imprisonment, trespass to land, trespass to chattels. It works across victims and across those torts. It does not apply to IIED or conversion.
- **Damages.** Nominal damages are available without actual harm for assault, battery, false imprisonment and trespass to land. Trespass to chattels, IIED and conversion need actual damage. Punitive damages are available for malice (R-TRT-114).
- **Defenses.** Consent and the privileges in I.B. Contributory or comparative negligence is **not** a defense to intentional torts (majority).
- **Vicarious liability.** An employer is liable for an employee's intentional tort only if it falls within the scope of employment (II.F.1).

### I.A Harms to the person, such as assault, battery, false imprisonment, and infliction of mental distress; and harms to property interests, such as trespass to land and chattels, and conversion

#### Intentional torts to the person (battery, assault, false imprisonment, IIED)

##### Battery (R-TRT-002)
- **Rule:** an act intended to cause a harmful or offensive contact with Π's person, or an imminent apprehension of one, that directly or indirectly results in such a contact (R2d §§ 13, 18).
- **Harmful** means bodily injury, pain or illness. **Offensive** means it offends a reasonable sense of personal dignity. Δ's knowledge of Π's special sensitivity can make a normally acceptable touch offensive.
- **Π's person** includes objects closely connected to the body: clothes, a plate in Π's hand (*Fisher v. Carrousel Motor Hotel*, 424 S.W.2d 627 (Tex. 1967)), a horse Π is riding, a car Π is driving.
- **Indirect contact** counts: poisoning food, setting a trap, a thrown object, causing a third person to touch Π.
- **No awareness needed.** A kiss on an unconscious person is a battery. No harm is needed; nominal damages are available.
- **Single vs. dual intent.** Courts split on whether Δ must intend the contact to be harmful or offensive, or only intend the contact, which then turns out to be harmful or offensive. *Vosburg v. Putney*, 80 Wis. 523 (1891): a schoolboy's light kick caused serious injury and he was liable for all the harm. On essays, state the R2d wording and note the eggshell rule.
- **Tested:** battery between sports players, with consent and self-defense (Jul 2025 Q2); battery as part of a broader fact pattern (Feb 2016 Q2, Feb 2020 Q1) [VERIFY V-071].

##### Assault (R-TRT-003)
- **Rule:** an act intended to cause harmful or offensive contact or imminent apprehension of it, which puts Π in **reasonable apprehension of an imminent** harmful or offensive contact (R2d § 21).
- **Apprehension** means awareness, not fear. A boxer who is certain he can block a punch is still apprehensive of it. Π must be aware at the time.
- **Imminence:** threats of future harm ("I'll get you next week") are not assault. Words alone are usually not enough, but words plus conduct (reaching for a pocket while threatening) may be. Words can negate apprehension ("If you weren't a lady…").
- **Apparent ability** is enough: an unloaded gun Π reasonably thinks is loaded.
- **Conditional threats** are assault when Δ has no right to impose the condition.
- **Overlap:** an attempted battery that Π sees coming is also an assault. A completed battery from behind is not.
- **Criminal vs. tort:** criminal law includes attempted battery without apprehension. Tort assault requires Π's apprehension.

##### False imprisonment (R-TRT-004)
- **Rule:** an act intended to confine Π within boundaries fixed by Δ, which directly or indirectly results in confinement, where Π is conscious of it or harmed by it (R2d § 35).
- **Means of confinement:** physical barriers; physical force; threats of immediate force against Π, Π's family or Π's property; asserted legal authority (a false arrest); duress (keeping Π's purse); or refusal to release Π when Δ has a duty to do so (refusing to let a passenger off a ship at the agreed port).
- **Escape:** no confinement if a reasonable means of escape exists that Π knows of. A means that is dangerous, humiliating or hidden does not count.
- **Not enough:** moral pressure, shame, a threat of future action, or blocking Π's path in one direction (*Bird v. Jones*, 7 Q.B. 742 (1845)).
- **Time:** any appreciable time; the length goes to damages.
- **Defenses:** shopkeeper's privilege (I.B.2.b), lawful arrest (I.B.2.d), consent.
- **CA note:** one-year limitations period (CCP § 340(c)). False arrest is false imprisonment by an asserted legal authority.

##### Intentional infliction of emotional distress (R-TRT-005)
- **Rule:** extreme and outrageous conduct by which Δ intentionally or recklessly causes severe emotional distress (R2d § 46).
- **Outrageous** means beyond all possible bounds of decency, atrocious and utterly intolerable. Factors:
  - abuse of power or position (employers, landlords, collection agencies, police, school officials);
  - Δ's knowledge of Π's special vulnerability (a child, a pregnant woman, a person in grief);
  - repetition;
  - Δ is a common carrier or innkeeper insulting a patron (liability even for gross insults);
  - racial or sexual slurs in an employment relationship (*Alcorn v. Anbro Engineering, Inc.* (1970) 2 Cal.3d 493).
- **Not enough:** mere insults, indignities, threats, annoyances and petty oppressions (*Hughes v. Pair* (2009) 46 Cal.4th 1035 [S-721]).
- **Severe distress:** distress so severe that no reasonable person could be expected to endure it. Physical symptoms are not required but help.
- **Third-party Π (R2d § 46(2)):** when Δ's outrageous conduct is directed at another person, Δ is liable for distress to (a) a member of that person's immediate family who is present, if Δ knows of the presence, and (b) anyone else present, if bodily harm results.
- **Constitutional limits:** *Hustler Magazine v. Falwell*, 485 U.S. 46 (1988) (a public figure suing over a parody must show a false statement of fact made with actual malice); *Snyder v. Phelps*, 562 U.S. 443 (2011) (picketing speech on public issues near a funeral was protected).
- **CA note:** California recognized IIED early (*State Rubbish Collectors Assn. v. Siliznoff* (1952) 38 Cal.2d 330). It requires the conduct to be directed at Π or to occur in the presence of a Π whom Δ knew was present (*Christensen v. Superior Court* (1991) 54 Cal.3d 868).
- **Tested:** IIED paired with defamation in a professor–student dispute (Feb 2020 Q1) [VERIFY V-071].

#### Intentional torts to property (trespass to land, trespass to chattels, conversion)

##### Trespass to land (R-TRT-006)
- **Rule:** liability for intentionally entering land in another's possession, or causing a thing or third person to enter it; for remaining after permission ends; or for failing to remove a thing Δ has a duty to remove (R2d § 158).
- **Intent** is intent to enter **that place**. A mistake about ownership or permission is no defense.
- **Land** includes the surface, the subsurface, and the airspace within the immediate reaches of the land. Overflights at ordinary altitudes are not trespass.
- **Plaintiff:** the person in possession (a tenant, not the landlord, for a trespass during the lease; the landlord sues for permanent damage to the reversion).
- **Damages:** nominal damages without harm; actual damages; punitive damages for willful trespass. A trespasser is liable for harm to persons or things on the land even if it was unforeseeable (R2d § 162) [VERIFY V-600].
- **Unintentional entries:** liability only if the entry was negligent, reckless or abnormally dangerous **and** caused harm (R2d § 165) [VERIFY V-600].
- **Continuing trespass:** a structure left on Π's land is a continuing trespass.
- **CA note:** noise, odor and light intrusions are trespass only if they cause physical damage or deposit particulate matter (*Wilson v. Interlake Steel Co.* (1982) 32 Cal.3d 229 [S-718]); otherwise use nuisance. Damages for injury to trees are doubled or trebled (Civ. Code § 3346 [S-460]) (R-REM-020).
- **Tested:** entry onto a neighbor's land (to take measurements; to retrieve a dog), combined with nuisance and remedies (Feb 2022 Q3, Feb 2018 Q3); intentional property torts with remedies calls (Feb 2013 Q3, Jul 2013 Q6) [VERIFY V-071].

##### Trespass to chattels (R-TRT-007)
- **Rule:** intentional dispossession of, or use of or intermeddling with, a chattel in another's possession, causing actual harm (R2d §§ 217, 218).
- **Actual harm** means damage to the chattel, loss of its use for a substantial time, or other harm. There are no nominal damages for mere intermeddling.
- **Damages:** cost of repair or loss of use (rental value) (R-REM-022).
- **CA note:** unsolicited e-mails that did not impair a computer system were not a trespass to chattels (*Intel Corp. v. Hamidi* (2003) 30 Cal.4th 1342).

##### Conversion (R-TRT-008)
- **Rule:** intentional exercise of dominion or control over a chattel that so seriously interferes with another's right to control it that Δ may justly be required to pay its full value (R2d § 222A).
- **Seriousness factors (§ 222A(2)):** the extent and duration of Δ's control; Δ's intent to assert a right inconsistent with Π's right; Δ's good faith; the harm done; and the inconvenience and expense to Π.
- **Typical acts:** stealing; embezzling; buying from a thief; selling another's goods; refusing to return them after demand; destroying or materially altering them; serious misuse (driving a borrowed car across the country).
- **Bona fide purchasers:** a good-faith buyer from a thief is a converter. A buyer from a person with voidable title, or from a merchant entrusted with the goods, may take good title (UCC § 2-403).
- **What can be converted:** tangible chattels, plus intangible rights merged into a document (stock certificates, checks, promissory notes).
- **Remedies:** damages equal to market value at the time of conversion plus interest (a forced sale), or replevin to recover the chattel itself (R-REM-021, R-REM-041).
- **CA note:** Civ. Code § 3336 adds compensation for the time and money spent pursuing the property [S-468].

##### Comparison: trespass to chattels vs. conversion

| Feature | Trespass to chattels | Conversion |
|---|---|---|
| Interference | Minor: brief use, dispossession, slight damage | Serious: theft, destruction, major alteration, long or adverse use |
| Damages | Actual loss: repair, loss of use | Full market value at conversion (forced sale), or replevin |
| Nominal damages | No (actual harm required) | Not applicable (value is recovered) |
| Mistake or good faith | No defense | No defense (but relevant to seriousness) |

**How I.A is tested.** On the MBE, the traps are: lack of awareness (assault needs it, battery does not); a reasonable means of escape; transferred intent; mistake is no defense to trespass; and outrageousness. On essays, run each tort element by element for each Π–Δ pair, then defenses, then damages. Property torts often come with a Remedies call (replevin, ejectment, injunction).

### I.B Defenses to claims for physical harms

The burden is on Δ to plead and prove consent and the privileges. A privilege protects a **reasonable mistake** in self-defense, defense of others (majority), the shopkeeper's privilege and arrest by officers, but **not** in defense of property, recapture of chattels, or a private citizen's mistake about whether a felony occurred.

#### I.B.1 Consent
- **Express** consent: words or writing.
- **Implied** consent:
  - **Apparent consent:** Π's conduct reasonably understood as consent. An immigrant who held out her arm for vaccination consented (*O'Brien v. Cunard S.S. Co.*, 154 Mass. 272 (1891)).
  - **Custom:** ordinary contacts in crowded places; sports contacts within the rules and customs of the game.
  - **Implied by law:** emergency treatment of an unconscious person when a reasonable person would consent and no contrary wish is known.
- **Capacity:** young children, incompetents and severely intoxicated persons cannot consent. A parent or guardian may consent for them.
- **Scope:** exceeding the consent is a battery. Consent to an operation on the right ear is not consent to an operation on the left ear (*Mohr v. Williams*, 95 Minn. 261 (1905)). In sport, a deliberate blow after the play is outside the scope (*Hackbart v. Cincinnati Bengals, Inc.*, 601 F.2d 516 (10th Cir. 1979)).
- **Fraud or duress:** fraud about the **essential nature** of the act vitiates consent (a "medical exam" that is a sexual touching). Fraud about a **collateral** matter does not (a payment in counterfeit money for a consensual act). Duress vitiates consent; economic pressure usually does not.
- **Consent to a crime:**
  - Majority (R2d § 892C): consent to an illegal act is effective, so mutual combatants cannot sue each other for battery.
  - Exception: where a statute protects a class against its own consent (statutory rape, child labor).
  - Minority: consent to a crime is ineffective, so each combatant can sue.
- **Medical consent and informed consent:** see II.B.1 and R-TRT-028.
- **CA note:** California analyzes sports injuries mainly through primary assumption of risk (*Knight v. Jewett* (1992) 3 Cal.4th 296), asking whether Δ's conduct was intentional or totally outside the range of ordinary activity in the sport (R-TRT-073).
- Rule: R-TRT-009.

#### I.B.2 Privileges and immunities

##### I.B.2.a Protection of self and others
- **Self-defense** (R-TRT-010; R2d §§ 63–65):
  - reasonable belief in an imminent attack (a reasonable mistake is protected);
  - force proportional to the threat;
  - deadly force only against a reasonably perceived threat of death or serious bodily harm;
  - no retaliation after the threat ends.
- **Retreat:** no duty to retreat before using nondeadly force. R2d § 65 requires retreat before deadly force if retreat is completely safe, except in one's dwelling. Many jurisdictions ("stand your ground") reject any retreat duty. MBE questions rarely turn on this.
- **Initial aggressor:** no privilege unless he withdraws and communicates the withdrawal, or the victim responds with excessive (deadly) force.
- **Injury to bystanders:** the actor is liable only if negligent toward the bystander (R2d § 75) [VERIFY V-600]. Transferred intent does not make a privileged defender liable.
- **Defense of others** (R-TRT-011; R2d § 76) [VERIFY V-600]:
  - Majority: privileged if the actor reasonably believes the third person is privileged to defend himself, so a reasonable mistake is protected.
  - Minority: the actor "steps into the shoes" of the person defended and loses the privilege if that person was not in fact privileged.
- **CA note:** Civ. Code § 50 permits any necessary force to protect oneself and listed family members, wards, servants, masters and guests from wrongful injury [VERIFY V-609].
- **Tested:** self-defense raised by a player who struck another (Jul 2025 Q2) [VERIFY V-071].

##### I.B.2.b Protection of property interests
- **Defense of property** (R-TRT-012; R2d § 77):
  - reasonable force to prevent or end an intrusion;
  - a request to desist first, unless futile or dangerous;
  - **never deadly force** to protect property alone.
- **Mistake:** the privilege is lost if the "intruder" was privileged (e.g., by necessity), unless the intruder caused the mistake.
- **Mechanical devices:** permitted only to the extent the possessor could have used the same force in person, with warning where reasonable. A spring gun in an unoccupied house is not permitted (*Katko v. Briney*, 183 N.W.2d 657 (Iowa 1971)). Barbed wire and guard dogs with warning signs are usually reasonable.
- **Dwelling:** if the intruder threatens the occupants, self-defense rules apply (deadly force may be privileged).
- **Recapture of chattels** (R-TRT-013):
  - only after a **wrongful taking**, in **fresh pursuit**, after a demand, with reasonable nondeadly force;
  - mistake is not protected;
  - entry onto the wrongdoer's land to recapture is privileged;
  - entry onto an innocent third person's land is privileged at a reasonable time and in a reasonable manner, with liability for actual damage.
  - Where the original possession was lawful (a lessee who keeps goods after the lease ends), use legal process, not force.
- **Shopkeeper's privilege** (R2d § 120A): detention for a reasonable time, in a reasonable manner, on or near the premises, on a reasonable belief of theft. A reasonable mistake is protected. **CA note:** Pen. Code § 490.5(f) (merchant may detain with probable cause, for a reasonable time) [VERIFY V-609].

##### I.B.2.c Parental discipline
- Parents may use reasonable force or confinement that they reasonably believe necessary for the child's control, training or education (R2d § 147) [VERIFY V-600] (R-TRT-014).
- Factors: the child's age, sex, physical and mental condition; the nature of the misconduct; whether the force was necessary; and whether it was disproportionate, degrading or likely to cause serious harm.
- Teachers and others in loco parentis have a similar but narrower privilege, subject to statutes and school rules.
- **CA note:** corporal punishment of public-school pupils is prohibited (Ed. Code § 49001) [VERIFY V-609]. Parent–child immunity is abolished in California; a "reasonable and prudent parent" standard applies (*Gibson v. Gibson* (1971) 3 Cal.3d 914) (R-TRT-074).

##### I.B.2.d Protection of public interests
- **Arrest** (R-TRT-015):
  - **With a warrant:** an officer is privileged if the warrant is valid on its face.
  - **Without a warrant, officers:** a felony on reasonable belief that a felony occurred and the arrestee committed it; a misdemeanor only for a breach of the peace in the officer's presence (some states: any misdemeanor in presence).
  - **Without a warrant, private citizens:** a felony only if the felony **was in fact committed** and the citizen reasonably believed the arrestee committed it; a misdemeanor breach of the peace in the citizen's presence.
  - **Force:** reasonable force; deadly force only where the suspect threatens death or serious bodily harm (*Tennessee v. Garner*, 471 U.S. 1 (1985), under the Fourth Amendment).
- **Prevention of crime:** reasonable force to prevent a felony or a breach of the peace.
- **Discipline and official authority:** officials exercising discretionary authority may be immune (see II.G Immunities, R-TRT-075).
- **CA note:** a private person may arrest for a public offense committed or attempted in his presence, or when a felony has in fact been committed and he has reasonable cause to believe the arrestee committed it (Pen. Code § 837).
- **Tested:** a hospital CEO's defenses for reassigning a security guard (Jul 2021 Q3; probably official or governmental immunity) [VERIFY V-071].

##### I.B.2.e Necessity
- **Public necessity** (R-TRT-016; R2d § 196): a complete privilege to enter or destroy property when reasonably necessary to avert an imminent public disaster. There is no compensation under tort law. Example: destroying a house to stop a city fire (*Surocco v. Geary* (1853) 3 Cal. 69).
- **Private necessity** (R-TRT-017; R2d § 197) [VERIFY V-600]: privilege to enter or use another's property to prevent serious harm to oneself, one's property or a few persons. It is incomplete (see I.B.2.f).
- The owner may not resist or expel a person privileged by necessity (*Ploof v. Putnam*, 81 Vt. 471 (1908)). If he does, he is liable for the resulting harm.

##### I.B.2.f Incomplete privilege
- An "incomplete" privilege excuses the entry, so the actor is not a trespasser and owes no nominal or punitive damages, but the actor must still pay for **actual harm** caused.
- The classic case: a ship kept tied to a dock during a storm damaged the dock, and the shipowner paid for it (*Vincent v. Lake Erie Transp. Co.*, 109 Minn. 456 (1910)).
- Private necessity is the main incomplete privilege. Some authorities also treat entry to recapture chattels from an innocent third person's land as incomplete (liability for actual damage).

**How I.B is tested.** The MBE tests the mistake rules (which privileges protect a reasonable mistake), the proportionality of force (deadly force for property is never allowed), and the private/public necessity split. Essays test consent and self-defense in a fight or sports setting, and the shopkeeper's privilege.

## II Negligence

**Map.** Duty (II.A) → breach (II.B, II.C) → actual cause (II.D) → proximate cause (II.E.1) → damages; special duty rules (II.E.2–II.E.4); vicarious liability (II.F); defenses (II.G). Negligence is about **half** of the Torts MBE [S-040] and the core of most California Torts essays (Jul 2014 Q6, Jul 2017 Q5, Feb 2019 Q2, Jul 2021 Q3, Jul 2023 Q1, Jul 2025 Q2) [VERIFY V-071]. Prima facie case: R-TRT-018 (R3d PEH § 6; Civ. Code § 1714(a)).

### II.A The duty question, including failure to act, unforeseeable plaintiffs, and obligations to control the conduct of third parties

**General duty.**
- Everyone owes a duty of reasonable care to avoid creating unreasonable risks of physical harm to others (R3d PEH § 7).
- Duty is a **question of law** for the court. Breach is usually a question of fact.
- **CA note:** California's general duty is statutory: "Everyone is responsible … for an injury occasioned to another by his or her want of ordinary care or skill" (Civ. Code § 1714(a)). Exceptions must be clearly supported by public policy under the *Rowland* factors (*Rowland v. Christian* (1968) 69 Cal.2d 108), applied categorically (*Cabral v. Ralphs Grocery Co.* (2011) 51 Cal.4th 764) (R-TRT-020).

**Unforeseeable plaintiffs** (R-TRT-019).
- *Palsgraf v. Long Island R.R. Co.*, 248 N.Y. 339 (1928):
  - **Cardozo (majority):** a duty is owed only to plaintiffs within the zone of foreseeable danger.
  - **Andrews (minority):** if Δ owes a duty to anyone, he owes it to all who are injured; the limits come through proximate cause.
- **Rescuers** are foreseeable ("danger invites rescue," *Wagner v. International Ry. Co.*, 232 N.Y. 176 (1921)), including when Δ imperils himself (R-TRT-024).
- **Firefighter's rule:** professional rescuers cannot recover for risks inherent in responding to the emergency Δ negligently created.
  - **CA note:** the rule does not apply to private safety employees (*Neighbarger v. Irwin Industries, Inc.* (1994) 8 Cal.4th 532 [S-707] [VERIFY V-611]).
  - **CA note:** Civ. Code § 1714.9 [S-722] allows claims for conduct after Δ knew the responder was present, statutory violations that did not prompt the call, intentional injury and arson.
- **Prenatal injuries:** a child born alive may recover for prenatal injuries negligently caused.

**California duty: recent cases.**

| Case | Holding |
|---|---|
| *Kesner v. Superior Court* (2016) 1 Cal.5th 1132 | Employers and premises owners who use asbestos owe a duty to members of a worker's household exposed to asbestos carried home on the worker's clothes. |
| *Kuciemba v. Victory Woodworks, Inc.* (2023) 14 Cal.5th 993 [S-700] | **No duty** to prevent take-home COVID-19 infection of employees' household members: the *Rowland* policy factors (an intolerable burden on employers and society) outweigh foreseeability. The derivative-injury rule of workers' compensation exclusivity did **not** bar the spouse's claim. (Apply.) |
| *Gilead Tenofovir Cases* (Cal. Aug. 3, 2026, S283862) [S-709] [S-710] [VERIFY V-603] | **No duty** of care, by a maker of a nondefective drug, in deciding whether and when to develop or commercialize an allegedly safer alternative. This reaffirms a defect-centered framework for product negligence. |

**No duty to act** (R-TRT-021; R2d § 314).
- No general duty to rescue or aid (*Yania v. Bigan*, 397 Pa. 316 (1959)). The line is misfeasance vs. nonfeasance.
- **Exceptions:**
  1. **Special relationship with Π** (R2d § 314A): common carrier–passenger; innkeeper–guest; possessor of land open to the public–member of the public; one who takes custody of another (jailer, hospital, school); employer–employee injured at work; and, in California, college–student for foreseeable violence during curricular activities (*Regents of University of California v. Superior Court* (2018) 4 Cal.5th 607).
  2. **Δ's conduct created the peril**, even without fault (R2d §§ 321, 322) [VERIFY V-600]. Example: a driver who innocently hits a pedestrian must summon help.
  3. **Voluntary undertaking** (R2d §§ 323, 324, 324A) [VERIFY V-600]. One who begins to help must act reasonably. Δ is liable if his failure increases the risk, if Π or others rely on the undertaking, or if Δ leaves Π worse off (e.g., by stopping others from helping).
  4. **Statute** (e.g., a duty to report or to stop at an accident) or **contract**.
- **Good Samaritan statutes** protect volunteers from ordinary negligence. **CA note:** Health & Saf. Code § 1799.102 (good-faith emergency care at the scene, not for compensation; no protection for gross negligence or willful misconduct) [VERIFY V-609].

**Obligations to control the conduct of third parties** (R-TRT-022; R2d § 315).
- No general duty to control others or protect Π from them, unless:
  - **Δ–third person relationship:**
    - parent–child (R2d § 316): the parent knows of the child's specific dangerous tendency and has the ability and opportunity to control it;
    - employer–employee acting outside scope on the employer's premises or with its chattels (R2d § 317) [VERIFY V-600];
    - one who takes charge of a person with dangerous propensities (R2d § 319) [VERIFY V-600]: psychiatric hospitals, jailers;
    - possessor of land or chattels allowing a third person to use them.
  - **Δ–Π relationship:** carriers, innkeepers, landowners, schools and custodians must protect those in their care from foreseeable third-party harm.
- **California two-step** (*Brown v. USA Taekwondo* (2021) 11 Cal.5th 204):
  1. Is there a special relationship (or other basis) that creates a duty to protect?
  2. If so, do the *Rowland* factors justify limiting it?

  The *Rowland* factors cannot create a duty where no special relationship exists. They can only limit one.
- **Psychotherapists** (R-TRT-023). *Tarasoff v. Regents of University of California* (1976) 17 Cal.3d 425 requires reasonable care to protect a foreseeable victim of a dangerous patient. **CA note:** Civ. Code § 43.92 limits liability to a serious threat of physical violence **communicated** to the therapist against a reasonably identifiable victim, and the duty is discharged by reasonable efforts to communicate the threat to the victim and to a law-enforcement agency.
- **Alcohol providers:** see II.F (R-TRT-068).
- **Tested:** a state hospital's duty to protect a patient from a third party (Jul 2021 Q3); the duty owed by a field owner and coach to an opposing player (Jul 2025 Q2) [VERIFY V-071].

### II.B The standard of care

**Baseline.** The reasonably prudent person under the same or similar circumstances (R3d PEH § 3). Breach is found by comparing Δ's conduct with that standard, often through the risk-utility (Hand) formula, B < P × L (*United States v. Carroll Towing Co.*, 159 F.2d 169 (2d Cir. 1947)) (R-TRT-031).

#### II.B.1 The reasonably prudent person, including children, physically and mentally impaired individuals, professional people, and other special classes

| Class | Standard | Authority |
|---|---|---|
| Ordinary adult | Objective reasonable person; Δ's best judgment is not enough | *Vaughan v. Menlove*, 132 Eng. Rep. 490 (C.P. 1837) (R-TRT-025) |
| Superior knowledge or skill | Must use it (a race-car driver on the road) | R3d PEH § 12 |
| Physical disability | Reasonable person with the same disability | R2d § 283C [VERIFY V-600]; R3d PEH § 11(a) |
| Mental illness or low intelligence | No allowance: the normal adult standard | R2d § 283B [VERIFY V-600]; R3d PEH § 11(c) |
| Sudden incapacity | Not negligent if unforeseeable (heart attack, first seizure); negligent if the risk was known | R3d PEH § 11(b) |
| Voluntary intoxication | No allowance | — |
| Children | Reasonable child of like age, intelligence and experience; **adult standard for adult activities** (driving, boating, flying); very young children may be incapable of negligence | R2d § 283A; R3d PEH § 10 (R-TRT-026) |
| Professionals | Knowledge and skill of a member of the profession in good standing; specialists, the specialty standard; expert testimony usually required; locality rule largely replaced by a national standard | R2d § 299A [VERIFY V-600] (R-TRT-027) |
| Physicians: informed consent | Disclose material risks a reasonable patient would want; objective causation | *Cobbs v. Grant* (1972) 8 Cal.3d 229; *Canterbury v. Spence*, 464 F.2d 772 (D.C. Cir. 1972) (R-TRT-028) |
| Common carriers and innkeepers | Highest degree of care consistent with practical operation (traditional); many courts now use reasonable care heightened by the circumstances | Civ. Code § 2100 (R-TRT-029) |
| Emergency | Reasonable person in an emergency not of Δ's making | R3d PEH § 9 (R-TRT-030) |
| Bailors and bailees | Traditional sliding standards (bailment for the sole benefit of the bailor: slight care; sole benefit of the bailee: great care); modern trend, ordinary care | — |

- **Informed consent, more detail.**
  - The majority patient-centered standard asks what a reasonable patient would consider material. The minority physician standard asks what reasonable physicians disclose.
  - Causation is objective: would a reasonable patient have refused?
  - Exceptions: emergency; risks that are commonly known or remote; patient waiver; therapeutic privilege.
  - **CA note:** a physician must disclose the risks of **declining** a recommended test (*Truman v. Thomas* (1980) 27 Cal.3d 285).
- **CA note:** medical negligence actions have a limitations period of three years from injury or one year from discovery, whichever is first (CCP § 340.5), and a cap on non-economic damages (MICRA, Civ. Code § 3333.2) (R-TRT-115).
- **Tested (MBE):** the adult-activity exception for children; sudden incapacity; the professional standard and expert testimony; whether Δ's subjective good faith matters (it does not).

#### II.B.2 Rules of conduct derived from statutes and custom

**Custom** (R-TRT-032).
- Compliance with industry custom is evidence of due care but not conclusive (*The T.J. Hooper*, 60 F.2d 737 (2d Cir. 1932)).
- Departure from custom is evidence of negligence.
- For professionals, the custom of the profession **is** the standard.

**Negligence per se** (R-TRT-033; R2d §§ 286, 288B [VERIFY V-600]; R3d PEH § 14).
1. The statute or regulation sets a clear standard of conduct.
2. Π is within the class of persons it protects.
3. The harm is the type it was designed to prevent.
4. The violation is unexcused.

Effect:
- **Majority:** negligence per se, which conclusively establishes duty and breach. Π must still prove causation and damages (*Martin v. Herzog*, 228 N.Y. 164 (1920)).
- **Minority:** the violation is merely evidence of negligence.
- **Licensing statutes:** violation is usually not negligence per se (unlicensed driving, unlicensed practice) unless the lack of skill caused the harm.
- **Excuse** (R-TRT-034; R2d § 288A [VERIFY V-600]): incapacity; Δ neither knew nor should have known of the occasion for compliance; inability after reasonable diligence; an emergency not of Δ's making; compliance would involve greater risk (*Tedla v. Ellman*, 280 N.Y. 124 (1939)).
- **Compliance** with a statute is evidence of due care, but not conclusive (R2d § 288C) [VERIFY V-600].
- **CA note:** Evid. Code § 669 creates a **presumption** of negligence when the four conditions are met. Δ rebuts it by proving he did what might reasonably be expected of a person of ordinary prudence, acting under similar circumstances, who desired to comply with the law. In California, negligence per se is an evidentiary doctrine within a negligence claim, not a separate cause of action.
- **Tested:** a texting-while-driving statute (Jul 2017 Q5) [VERIFY V-071]. MBE favorites: Π outside the protected class (a statute protecting workers invoked by a trespasser); harm of a different type (a fencing statute aimed at disease, not falls); excused violations.

### II.C Problems relating to proof of fault, including res ipsa loquitur

**Direct and circumstantial evidence** (R-TRT-036).
- Π may prove breach by direct evidence or by inference from circumstances.
- **Slip-and-fall:** where Δ did not create the hazard, Π must show actual or constructive notice: the hazard existed long enough that a reasonable inspection would have revealed it. A minority "mode of operation" rule dispenses with notice for self-service hazards.
- **CA note:** *Ortega v. Kmart Corp.* (2001) 26 Cal.4th 1200 (constructive notice may be inferred if Δ failed to inspect within a reasonable time) [VERIFY V-611].

**Res ipsa loquitur** (R-TRT-035; R2d § 328D; R3d PEH § 17).
- Elements:
  1. The event is of a kind that ordinarily does not occur without negligence (a barrel falling from a warehouse window, *Byrne v. Boadle*, 159 Eng. Rep. 299 (Ex. 1863); a sponge left in a patient; an elevator that drops).
  2. Other responsible causes, including Π and third persons, are sufficiently eliminated. Traditionally this is phrased as Δ's "exclusive control" of the instrumentality.
  3. The negligence is within the scope of Δ's duty to Π.
- **Effect (majority):** a permissible inference. Π survives a directed verdict; the jury may, but need not, find negligence.
- **Effect (minority):** a presumption that shifts the burden of production or of proof.
- **CA note:** res ipsa is a presumption affecting the burden of producing evidence (Evid. Code § 646). If Δ produces evidence of due care, the presumption disappears, but the jury may still draw the inference.
- **Multiple defendants:**
  - An unconscious patient injured during surgery can invoke res ipsa against everyone who controlled his body (*Ybarra v. Spangard* (1944) 25 Cal.2d 486).
  - A bottler is responsible for a bottle that exploded after it left its control, if Π shows it was handled carefully afterward (*Escola v. Coca Cola Bottling Co.* (1944) 24 Cal.2d 453).
- **Specific evidence:** Π who offers specific evidence of negligence may still rely on res ipsa (majority).
- **Tested (MBE):** whether the accident ordinarily implies negligence; control at the relevant time; the effect (inference, not a directed verdict for Π).

### II.D Problems relating to causation

#### II.D.1 But for and substantial causes
- **But-for test** (R-TRT-037; R3d PEH § 26): Δ's negligence is an actual cause if the harm would not have occurred without it.
- Focus on the **negligent aspect** of Δ's conduct. If Δ was speeding but the child darted out so suddenly that no driver could have stopped, the speed is not a but-for cause.
- **Substantial-factor test** (R-TRT-038; R2d § 431 [VERIFY V-600]): used where multiple causes make the but-for test misleading.
- **CA note:** California uses the substantial-factor test as its general causation instruction; it subsumes but-for causation (*Mitchell v. Gonzales* (1991) 54 Cal.3d 1041). In asbestos cases, Π must show exposure to Δ's product was a substantial factor contributing to the risk of developing cancer (*Rutherford v. Owens-Illinois, Inc.* (1997) 16 Cal.4th 953).
- **Loss of chance** (R-TRT-040): some states award proportional damages where malpractice reduced an already-below-50% chance of recovery. Others require more-likely-than-not causation. California appellate courts have generally not recognized it [VERIFY V-610].
- **Warning cases:** Π must show an adequate warning would have been read and heeded (some states presume heeding). **CA note:** learned-intermediary causation under *Himes v. Somatics, LLC* (Cal. 2024) [S-708] [VERIFY V-605] (see III).

#### II.D.2 Harms traceable to multiple causes
- **Multiple sufficient causes:** two fires merge, each enough to burn Π's house. Each is an actual cause (R2d § 432(2) [VERIFY V-600]; R3d PEH § 27) (R-TRT-038).
- **Alternative liability:** two negligent hunters, one unidentified shot. The burden shifts to each Δ to prove he did not cause the harm (*Summers v. Tice* (1948) 33 Cal.2d 80; R2d § 433B(3)) (R-TRT-039). All potentially responsible Δs must be before the court, and all must have acted negligently.
- **Concert of action:** Δs acting under a common plan, or one substantially assisting the other's tortious conduct (drag racers), are each liable for the whole (R2d § 876) [VERIFY V-600].
- **Market share** (California): for a fungible product (DES) where Π cannot identify the maker, Δs who made a substantial share of the market are liable in proportion to their market share unless they prove they could not have made the product (*Sindell v. Abbott Laboratories* (1980) 26 Cal.3d 588). Liability is several only (*Brown v. Superior Court* (1988) 44 Cal.3d 1049). Many states reject it.
- **Indivisible vs. divisible harm:** if the harm can be apportioned (two dogs kill Π's sheep, and each dog's kills are known), each Δ pays only for his part (R2d § 433A). The burden of proving divisibility is on Δs.

#### II.D.3 Questions of apportionment of responsibility among multiple tortfeasors, including joint and several liability
- **Joint and several liability** (R-TRT-041): each Δ whose tortious conduct was a legal cause of an indivisible injury is liable for the entire amount. Π may recover the whole from any Δ but may collect only once (the one-satisfaction rule).
- **MBE default:** joint and several liability with pure comparative negligence, unless the question says otherwise [S-040].
- **Modern variations** (Restatement (Third) of Torts: Apportionment of Liability § 10 [VERIFY V-600]): several liability only; hybrid systems (joint liability only for Δs above a fault threshold, or only for economic damages); reallocation of an insolvent Δ's share.
- **CA note:** Proposition 51 (Civ. Code § 1431.2) makes liability for **non-economic** damages several only, in proportion to fault. Economic damages remain joint and several (R-TRT-042).
- **Contribution** (R-TRT-043): a Δ who pays more than his share may recover from the others.
  - Traditional: equal (pro rata) shares.
  - Modern: shares in proportion to comparative fault.
  - No contribution in favor of intentional tortfeasors.
- **Indemnity:** shifts the entire loss to another:
  - from a vicariously liable employer to the employee;
  - from a retailer to the manufacturer of a defective product;
  - by express contract;
  - formerly, from a passively negligent party to an actively negligent one.
- **CA note:** comparative equitable indemnity apportions loss among concurrent tortfeasors by fault (*American Motorcycle Assn. v. Superior Court* (1978) 20 Cal.3d 578).
- **Settlements:**
  - **Common law:** a release of one joint tortfeasor released all.
  - **Modern law:** a release discharges only the settling Δ. Π's claim against the others is reduced pro tanto (by the settlement amount) or, under some statutes, by the settling Δ's percentage share.
  - **CA note:** a settlement made in good faith reduces the claims against non-settling Δs by the amount paid and bars their contribution or indemnity claims against the settling Δ (CCP §§ 877, 877.6). Good faith means the amount is within the reasonable range of the settlor's proportionate share (*Tech-Bilt, Inc. v. Woodward-Clyde & Associates* (1985) 38 Cal.3d 488).
- **Tested:** "how should damages be apportioned?" between a negligent driver and a utility (Jul 2017 Q5) [VERIFY V-071]. MBE: computing recovery under pure comparative fault plus joint and several liability.

**Worked numbers.** Π's damages are $100,000. Π is 20% at fault, A is 50% and B is 30%.
- **MBE default:** Π recovers $80,000 and may collect all of it from either A or B. A paying Δ seeks contribution so that A bears $50,000 and B $30,000.
- **California:** suppose the $100,000 is $40,000 economic and $60,000 non-economic. Π's recoverable economic damages are $32,000, owed jointly by A and B. Π's recoverable non-economic damages are $48,000, owed severally: A pays $30,000 (50% of $60,000) and B pays $18,000 (30%).

### II.E Limitations on liability and special rules of liability

#### II.E.1 Problems relating to "remote" or "unforeseeable" causes, "legal" or "proximate" cause, and "superseding" causes
- **Scope of the risk** (R-TRT-044; R3d PEH § 29): Δ is liable only for harms that result from the risks that made the conduct negligent.
  - The modern test is foreseeability of the **type** of harm (*The Wagon Mound No. 1*, [1961] A.C. 388 (P.C.)).
  - The older "direct consequences" test (*In re Polemis*, [1921] 3 K.B. 560) made Δ liable for all direct results.
- **Manner and extent** need not be foreseeable. The **eggshell plaintiff** rule makes Δ liable for the full extent of the harm (R-TRT-045).
- **Intervening causes** (R-TRT-046; R2d §§ 440–453 [VERIFY V-600]): a force that comes into play after Δ's negligence.
  - **Foreseeable intervening causes do not relieve Δ:**
    - subsequent medical negligence in treating the injury (R2d § 457 [VERIFY V-600]);
    - efforts of rescuers;
    - foreseeable negligence of third persons, including other drivers;
    - Π's reaction to the danger, or his attempt to escape it;
    - later disease or a second injury caused by the weakened condition;
    - a crime or intentional tort that Δ's negligence made more likely (keys left in a car in a high-crime area; a landlord's broken lock).
  - **Superseding causes break the chain:**
    - unforeseeable, extraordinary acts;
    - independent intentional crimes that Δ's conduct did not make more likely;
    - acts of God that produce an unforeseeable kind of harm.
  - **Key principle:** if the intervening act is the very risk that made Δ negligent, it cannot be superseding.
- **CA note:** California applies the same foreseeability approach, often within the substantial-factor instruction. Civ. Code § 3333 measures tort damages as all detriment proximately caused "whether it could have been anticipated or not" [S-469].
- **Tested (MBE):** classic chains: a negligent driver, then a negligent doctor (liable); a negligent maintenance company, then an arsonist (usually superseding unless the risk was foreseeable); a gas leak lit by a careless match (foreseeable).

#### II.E.2 Claims against owners and occupiers of land

**Traditional (majority/MBE) categories.**

| Entrant | Who | Duty of the possessor |
|---|---|---|
| Undiscovered trespasser | Enters without privilege, unknown | Refrain from willful or wanton harm (R-TRT-047) |
| Discovered or anticipated trespasser | Known, or one who constantly enters a limited area | Reasonable care in active operations; warn of or make safe **known, artificial, concealed, deadly** conditions (R2d §§ 334–337 [VERIFY V-600]) |
| Child trespasser | Attractive nuisance (R2d § 339) | Reasonable care regarding artificial conditions when the five § 339 factors are met (R-TRT-048) |
| Licensee | Enters with consent for own purpose (social guest) | Warn of or make safe **known** concealed dangers; reasonable care in active operations; **no duty to inspect** (R-TRT-049) |
| Invitee | Public invitee or business visitor | Reasonable care: **inspect**, discover, warn or repair; reasonable care in active operations (R2d §§ 332, 343, 343A) (R-TRT-050) |

- **Scope of invitation:** an invitee who goes outside the area of the invitation loses invitee status.
- **Open and obvious dangers:** traditionally no duty to warn. Under R2d § 343A, the possessor is still liable if he should anticipate harm despite obviousness (e.g., Π distracted by merchandise). Many courts treat obviousness as going to breach or comparative fault.
- **Police and firefighters:** often treated as licensees, plus the firefighter's rule.
- **Recreational-use statutes** in most states limit duties to those who use land for recreation without charge.
- **Off the premises:** for natural conditions, traditionally no duty (except trees near highways in urban areas, in many states); for artificial conditions, reasonable care toward users of adjacent public ways.
- **Lessors and vendors** (R-TRT-052; R2d §§ 355–362 [VERIFY V-600]): no duty after transfer, except (1) known latent dangers not disclosed; (2) common areas; (3) a covenant to repair or a negligent repair; (4) a public-use lease; (5) a short-term furnished dwelling. Vendors must disclose known concealed dangers.
- **Crime on premises** (R-TRT-053): a business possessor must take reasonable precautions against foreseeable crime. Tests vary: prior similar incidents, totality of circumstances, or balancing.

**CA note: California premises liability** (R-TRT-051).
- *Rowland v. Christian* (1968) 69 Cal.2d 108 abolished the categories. A possessor owes all entrants reasonable care in managing the property (Civ. Code § 1714); status is only a factor in breach.
- **Recreational use** (Civ. Code § 846): no duty to keep land safe for recreational entrants or to warn them, unless (1) willful or malicious failure to guard or warn, (2) consideration was paid, or (3) the entrant was expressly invited rather than merely permitted.
- **Felony trespassers:** Civ. Code § 847 limits liability for injuries suffered while committing listed felonies [VERIFY V-609].
- **Crime:**
  - Hiring security guards requires heightened foreseeability, usually prior similar incidents (*Ann M. v. Pacific Plaza Shopping Center* (1993) 6 Cal.4th 666).
  - A sliding scale applies: minimal, inexpensive precautions may be required on lesser foreseeability, and immediate, obvious threats require reasonable responses (*Delgado v. Trax Bar & Grill* (2005) 36 Cal.4th 224).
- **Landlords:** reasonable care under § 1714; no strict liability for defects in rented premises (*Peterson v. Superior Court* (1995) 10 Cal.4th 1185).
- **Public property:** the dangerous-condition statute (Gov. Code § 835) (R-TRT-075).

**Tested:** a dog-breeder's visitors (Feb 2019 Q2: invitee/licensee duties, plus animals); a field owner's duty to a visiting player (Jul 2025 Q2) [VERIFY V-071]. On an essay, analyze the category first (majority), then add "In California, *Rowland* abolished these categories…".

#### II.E.3 Claims for mental distress not arising from physical harm; other intangible injuries

**Negligent infliction of emotional distress (NIED).**
- **Impact rule** (minority, historical): recovery only if Π suffered a physical impact.
- **Zone of danger** (R-TRT-054; R2d §§ 313, 436 [VERIFY V-600]): Π was within the zone of physical danger and feared for his own safety. Many courts traditionally require physical manifestations (ulcers, miscarriage, heart attack).
- **Bystanders** (R-TRT-055):
  - *Dillon v. Legg* (1968) 68 Cal.2d 728 used foreseeability guidelines.
  - **California:** *Thing v. La Chusa* (1989) 48 Cal.3d 644 sets firm requirements: Π (1) is closely related to the injury victim; (2) is present at the scene of the injury-producing event when it occurs and is then aware that it is causing injury to the victim; and (3) as a result suffers serious emotional distress, beyond that of a disinterested witness.
  - **Closely related:** relatives living in the same household, or parents, siblings, children and grandparents. Unmarried cohabitants are excluded (*Elden v. Sheldon* (1988) 46 Cal.3d 267 [S-712]). That rule is under review (*Sanchez v. Rojas*, S294671) [S-711] [VERIFY V-604].
  - **Awareness:** in a medical setting, Π must be aware of the injury-producing event as it happens, not merely of its results (*Bird v. Saenz* (2002) 28 Cal.4th 910).
  - **R3d PEH § 48:** a close family member who perceives the event contemporaneously.
- **Direct victims** (R-TRT-056):
  - Majority: special situations, such as mishandling a corpse, a negligent death notice, or a negligent diagnosis communicated to the patient.
  - **California:** NIED is not an independent tort; it is negligence (*Burgess v. Superior Court* (1992) 2 Cal.4th 1064). A direct victim recovers when Δ breached a duty assumed by Δ, imposed by law, or arising from a relationship with Π. Examples: a mother injured emotionally in a negligent delivery (*Burgess*); a husband whose wife was negligently diagnosed with syphilis (*Molien v. Kaiser Foundation Hospitals* (1980) 27 Cal.3d 916); parents of children molested by the parents' therapist (*Marlene F. v. Affiliated Psychiatric Medical Clinic, Inc.* (1989) 48 Cal.3d 583).
- **Fear of future disease** (R-TRT-057): in California, recoverable only if Π's fear stems from knowledge, corroborated by reliable medical or scientific opinion, that the disease is more likely than not to develop. The rule is relaxed for oppression, fraud or malice (*Potter v. Firestone Tire & Rubber Co.* (1993) 6 Cal.4th 965).
- **Emotional distress from property damage:** generally not recoverable in negligence.

**Other intangible injuries.**
- **Loss of consortium** (R-TRT-058):
  - Spouses, and registered domestic partners in California (*Rodriguez v. Bethlehem Steel Corp.* (1974) 12 Cal.3d 382).
  - **CA:** no claim for a child's loss of parental consortium (*Borer v. American Airlines, Inc.* (1977) 19 Cal.3d 441), and none for unmarried cohabitants (*Elden*, under review).
  - The claim is derivative.
- **Wrongful death and survival:** see the cross-cutting section below (R-TRT-059, R-TRT-060).
- **Wrongful birth and wrongful life** (R-TRT-062): parents recover the extraordinary costs. In California, the child may recover special damages for extraordinary expenses, but not general damages (*Turpin v. Sortini* (1982) 31 Cal.3d 220).
- **Prenatal injury:** a child born alive may recover.
- **Tested:** NIED (Feb 2013 Q4) [VERIFY V-071]. MBE favorites: a bystander who arrives after the accident (no recovery); a zone-of-danger Π without physical symptoms in a jurisdiction that requires them.

#### II.E.4 Claims for pure economic loss
- **General rule** (R-TRT-061): there is no general duty to avoid unintentionally causing purely economic loss to strangers (Restatement (Third) of Torts: Liability for Economic Harm § 1 [VERIFY V-600]). The concern is liability to an indeterminate class.
- **Recognized exceptions:**
  1. Negligent misrepresentation to the limited class of intended recipients (R2d § 552) (R-TRT-109).
  2. Negligent performance of services meant to benefit Π: a lawyer or notary drafting a will for the intended beneficiaries (*Biakanja v. Irving* (1958) 49 Cal.2d 647; *Lucas v. Hamm* (1961) 56 Cal.2d 583).
  3. Special injury from a public nuisance (commercial fishermen).
  4. **CA note:** negligent interference with prospective economic advantage where a special relationship exists. In *J'Aire Corp. v. Gregory* (1979) 24 Cal.3d 799, a contractor's delay cost a restaurant tenant its business. The factors: (1) the extent the transaction was intended to affect Π; (2) foreseeability of harm; (3) degree of certainty of injury; (4) closeness of connection; (5) moral blame; (6) policy of preventing future harm.
- **CA note:** no duty to nearby businesses for purely economic losses from a mass gas leak, absent a special relationship (*Southern California Gas Leak Cases* (2019) 7 Cal.5th 391 [S-715]).
- **Products:** no tort recovery for a product that damages only itself; that loss belongs to warranty (*Seely v. White Motor Co.* (1965) 63 Cal.2d 9) (R-TRT-086).
- **Contracting parties: the economic loss rule.** Contract governs purely economic losses from a failure to perform. Torts independent of the contract survive: fraud in the inducement (*Lazar v. Superior Court* (1996) 12 Cal.4th 631); affirmative misrepresentations during performance (*Robinson Helicopter Co., Inc. v. Dana Corp.* (2004) 34 Cal.4th 979); and, under *Rattagan v. Uber Technologies, Inc.* (2024) 17 Cal.5th 1 [S-132], fraudulent **concealment** during performance where the tort's elements exist independently of the contract and the conduct exposes Π to a risk of harm beyond the parties' reasonable contemplation. (Apply; R-TRT-108; see R-CTR-117 and R-REM-032.)
- **Tested:** MBE: a business owner's lost profits from a road closure (no recovery). California: *Rattagan* in a Contracts or Torts crossover.

### II.F Liability for acts of others

#### II.F.1 Employees and other agents
- **Respondeat superior** (R-TRT-063): the employer is strictly (vicariously) liable for torts committed by an employee acting within the scope of employment (Restatement (Third) of Agency § 7.07 [VERIFY V-600]).
- **Employee or independent contractor?** The test is the right to control the manner and means of the work. Factors: skill required; who supplies tools; method of payment; duration; whether the work is part of the employer's regular business; and the parties' belief.
- **Scope of employment:**
  - conduct of the kind the employee was hired to perform;
  - substantially within authorized time and space;
  - motivated at least in part to serve the employer.
  - A forbidden **manner** of doing the job (speeding on a delivery) stays within scope.
- **Detour vs. frolic:** a minor deviation stays within scope; a substantial personal venture does not. The employee re-enters scope on resuming the employer's business.
- **Going and coming rule:** ordinary commuting is outside scope. Exceptions: special errands; required use of a personal vehicle for work; travel that is part of the job; employer-provided transport (*Hinman v. Westinghouse Electric Co.* (1970) 2 Cal.3d 956: travel time as part of the job).
- **Intentional torts** are within scope when:
  - force is inherent in the job (bouncers, security guards, repossession agents);
  - the tort arises from a job-related dispute;
  - or the employee intends to serve the employer.
- **CA note:** the test is whether the tort was engendered by, or a foreseeable outgrowth of, the employment, i.e., "typical of or broadly incidental to" the enterprise.
  - A hospital technician's sexual assault on a patient was not within scope (*Lisa M. v. Henry Mayo Newhall Memorial Hospital* (1995) 12 Cal.4th 291).
  - An on-duty police officer's rape of a detainee, committed by misusing official authority, was within scope (*Mary M. v. City of Los Angeles* (1991) 54 Cal.3d 202).
- **Other agents:**
  - A principal is vicariously liable for a non-employee agent's torts only in limited situations: apparent authority, misrepresentations within authority, or nondelegable duties.
  - **Apparent (ostensible) agency:** a hospital that holds out emergency physicians as its staff is liable for them.
- **Borrowed servants:** the employer with the right to control the specific act is liable.
- **Employer's own negligence:** negligent hiring, supervision, retention and entrustment are direct liability (R-TRT-067; R2d §§ 308, 317, 390 [VERIFY V-600]).
- **Punitive damages against employers (CA):** only where an officer, director or managing agent had advance knowledge of the employee's unfitness and employed him in conscious disregard of others' safety, authorized or ratified the conduct, or was personally guilty of oppression, fraud or malice (Civ. Code § 3294(b) [S-451]).
- **Tested:** a waiter's negligence attributed to the caterer and the owner (Jul 2014 Q6); a lawyer's car accident and the law-firm partnership (Jul 2023 Q1, a BA crossover) [VERIFY V-071].

#### II.F.2 Independent contractors and nondelegable duties
- **General rule** (R-TRT-064; R2d § 409 [VERIFY V-600]): one who hires an independent contractor is not liable for the contractor's negligence.
- **Exceptions:**
  1. **The hirer's own negligence:** in selecting an incompetent contractor (R2d § 411), in giving instructions, or in supervising work over which it retained control (R2d § 414) [VERIFY V-600].
  2. **Inherently dangerous work or peculiar risk** (R2d §§ 416, 427) [VERIFY V-600]: blasting, excavation next to a public way, demolition.
  3. **Nondelegable duties:**
     - the possessor's duty to keep premises open to the public reasonably safe for invitees;
     - duties imposed by statute or regulation (vehicle-safety rules; building codes);
     - common carriers' duties to passengers;
     - a landlord's duty to maintain common areas;
     - work done in a public place;
     - the duty of lateral support.
  4. **Apparent agency:** the hirer held the contractor out as its own employee.
- **CA note:** under the *Privette* doctrine, the hirer of a contractor is generally not liable to the contractor's **own employees** for injuries from the contracted work (*Privette v. Superior Court* (1993) 5 Cal.4th 689). The reason is that workers' compensation covers them. Exceptions:
  - the hirer retained control and **affirmatively contributed** to the injury (*Hooker v. Department of Transportation* (2002) 27 Cal.4th 198);
  - the hirer knew of a concealed hazard on its premises that the contractor did not know of and could not reasonably discover, and failed to warn (*Kinsman v. Unocal Corp.* (2005) 37 Cal.4th 659).
- **Tested:** a state hospital's liability for its caterer's peanut-contaminated meal (Jul 2021 Q3); a caterer, a waiter and a venue owner (Jul 2014 Q6) [VERIFY V-071]. The essay structure: employee or contractor → general rule → exceptions (nondelegable duty; the hirer's own negligence; apparent agency).

#### Other vicarious and related liability (partners, joint enterprise, vehicle owners, parents, alcohol providers)
- **Partners:** a partnership is liable for a partner's torts in the ordinary course of its business or with its authority, and partners in a general partnership are jointly and severally liable for partnership obligations (Revised Uniform Partnership Act §§ 305, 306 [VERIFY V-600]) (R-TRT-065; see Business Associations).
- **Joint enterprise:** members are vicariously liable for each other's torts within the enterprise (R2d § 491 [VERIFY V-600]). Requirements: an agreement, a common purpose, a community of pecuniary interest, and an equal right of control.
- **Vehicle owners:**
  - Not vicariously liable at common law. Exceptions: the family-purpose doctrine (some states) and owner-liability statutes.
  - **CA note:** Veh. Code §§ 17150–17151 make an owner liable for a permissive user's negligence up to statutory limits [VERIFY V-609].
  - Negligent entrustment is the owner's own tort.
- **Bailors:** not vicariously liable for bailees.
- **Parents** (R-TRT-066):
  - No vicarious liability at common law.
  - A parent is liable for his own negligent supervision of a child with a known dangerous propensity (R2d § 316), and for negligent entrustment.
  - **CA note:** Civ. Code § 1714.1 makes a parent or guardian with custody jointly and severally liable with a minor for the minor's willful misconduct, up to a statutory cap [VERIFY V-609].
- **Alcohol providers** (R-TRT-068):
  - **Common law:** no liability, because the drinking, not the furnishing, is the proximate cause.
  - **Modern law:** dram-shop statutes make commercial vendors liable for serving visibly intoxicated persons or minors. Some states impose social-host liability.
  - **CA note:** Civ. Code § 1714(b)–(c) and Bus. & Prof. Code § 25602 codify non-liability. Exceptions:
    - a licensee who sells to an **obviously intoxicated minor** (Bus. & Prof. Code § 25602.1);
    - an adult social host who **knowingly** furnishes alcohol at the host's residence to a person **under 21** (Civ. Code § 1714(d)).

### II.G Defenses

#### II.G.1 Contributory fault, including common law contributory negligence and last clear chance, and the various forms of comparative negligence

| System | Effect of Π's negligence | Where |
|---|---|---|
| Contributory negligence | Complete bar, subject to last clear chance | A handful of jurisdictions (Alabama, Maryland, North Carolina, Virginia, D.C.) |
| Pure comparative | Damages reduced by Π's %; Π recovers even at 99% | **California** (*Li v. Yellow Cab Co.* (1975) 13 Cal.3d 804); **MBE default** [S-040] |
| Modified (50%) | Π barred if Π's fault ≥ 50% (equal to Δ) | Some states |
| Modified (51%) | Π barred if Π's fault > 50% (greater than Δ) | Most comparative-fault states |

- **Contributory negligence** (R-TRT-069; R2d § 463 [VERIFY V-600]) is Π's failure to use reasonable care for his own safety that contributes to his harm. The same standards apply as for Δ (children, emergencies). Π's violation of a safety statute meant to protect Π is contributory negligence per se.
- **Not a defense** to intentional torts, or to willful, wanton or reckless conduct, in contributory jurisdictions.
- **Last clear chance** (R2d §§ 479–480 [VERIFY V-600]):
  - **Helpless Π:** Δ who knew or should have known of the peril and could have avoided it is liable.
  - **Inattentive Π:** Δ must actually have known of the peril.
  - Comparative-fault states abolish last clear chance, because fault is simply apportioned.
- **Comparative negligence** (R-TRT-070):
  - The jury assigns percentages to every party, and in some states to non-parties.
  - It applies to strict products liability in California (*Daly v. General Motors Corp.* (1978) 20 Cal.3d 725).
  - Most states do not reduce damages for Π's negligence against an intentional tortfeasor.
- **Imputed contributory negligence:**
  - Negligence is imputed to Π where Π would be vicariously liable for the actor (employer–employee; partners; joint enterprise).
  - It is not imputed between spouses, from parent to child, or from driver to owner-passenger merely because of ownership (modern rule).
  - In wrongful-death and loss-of-consortium claims, the decedent's or injured spouse's fault reduces the derivative recovery.
- **Avoidable consequences and seat belts:** failing to mitigate (refusing reasonable treatment) reduces damages. Failure to wear a seat belt may reduce recovery in some states, including California through comparative fault.
- **Tested (MBE):** computing recovery under pure vs. modified systems; whether last clear chance survives (not in comparative states); whether comparative fault reduces intentional-tort damages (generally not).

#### II.G.2 Assumption of risk
- **Express** (R-TRT-071): a valid release bars ordinary negligence claims. Unenforceable:
  - for intentional, reckless or grossly negligent conduct (*City of Santa Barbara v. Superior Court* (2007) 41 Cal.4th 747);
  - where the release affects the public interest (*Tunkl v. Regents of University of California* (1963) 60 Cal.2d 92: hospital admission release void);
  - if ambiguous;
  - **CA note:** for fraud, willful injury or violation of law (Civ. Code § 1668).
- **Implied, traditional** (R-TRT-072; R2d § 496C [VERIFY V-600]): Π knew and appreciated a specific risk and voluntarily encountered it. A complete bar at common law.
  - Not voluntary if Δ left no reasonable alternative (a tenant forced to use the only exit).
  - Not applied to rescuers.
- **Implied, modern:** most comparative-fault states merge **secondary** (unreasonable) implied assumption into comparative fault and treat **primary** assumption (inherent risks) as a no-duty rule.
- **CA note: *Knight v. Jewett* (1992) 3 Cal.4th 296** (R-TRT-073):
  - **Primary** assumption of risk: Δ owes no duty to protect Π from risks inherent in a sport or recreational activity; a complete bar.
  - **Secondary** assumption of risk: Δ owes a duty, but Π knowingly encountered a risk Δ created; it becomes comparative fault.
  - **Co-participants** are liable only for intentional injury or conduct so reckless as to be totally outside the range of ordinary activity in the sport.
  - **Coaches and instructors** are liable if they intentionally injure the student or engage in conduct totally outside the range of ordinary coaching, such as recklessly pushing a student beyond her ability without training (*Kahn v. East Side Union High School Dist.* (2003) 31 Cal.4th 990 [S-714]).
  - **Facility operators and hosts:** no duty to eliminate inherent risks, but a duty not to **increase** them beyond those inherent (*Avila v. Citrus Community College Dist.* (2006) 38 Cal.4th 148: being hit by a pitch is inherent in baseball).
  - The doctrine reaches non-sport recreation (*Nalwa v. Cedar Fair, L.P.* (2012) 55 Cal.4th 1148: bumper cars).
- **Tested:** a sports injury with claims against a field owner, a coach and other players (Jul 2025 Q2); visitors who entered an off-limits room (Feb 2019 Q2) [VERIFY V-071].

#### Immunities (intrafamily, charitable, governmental, official)
- **Intrafamily** (R-TRT-074):
  - Interspousal immunity is abolished almost everywhere, including California (*Self v. Self* (1962) 58 Cal.2d 683; *Klein v. Klein* (1962) 58 Cal.2d 692).
  - Parent–child immunity is abolished in California, which uses a reasonable-parent standard (*Gibson v. Gibson* (1971) 3 Cal.3d 914). Other states retain immunity for parental authority and discretion.
- **Charitable immunity** is abolished in most states, including California (*Malloy v. Fong* (1951) 37 Cal.2d 356).
- **Governmental immunity** (R-TRT-075):
  - **Federal:** the FTCA (28 U.S.C. §§ 1346(b), 2671–2680) waives immunity for negligence of federal employees within the scope of employment. Excluded: discretionary functions (§ 2680(a)) and most intentional torts (§ 2680(h)). Law-enforcement officers' assault, battery, false imprisonment, false arrest, abuse of process and malicious prosecution are covered.
  - **States and municipalities:** waivers vary. The traditional municipal split is governmental (immune) vs. proprietary (liable) functions.
  - **CA note (Government Claims Act):**
    - no common-law public-entity liability (Gov. Code § 815);
    - vicarious liability for employees acting within scope (§ 815.2);
    - discretionary-act immunity for employees (§ 820.2), limited to basic policy decisions, not operational acts (*Johnson v. State of California* (1968) 69 Cal.2d 782);
    - dangerous condition of public property (§ 835) [S-719];
    - no punitive damages (§ 818);
    - no liability for failing to provide police protection (§ 845);
    - a six-month claim requirement for personal-injury claims (§ 911.2) [VERIFY V-607].
- **Official immunity:**
  - Judges, legislators and prosecutors have absolute immunity for acts within their functions. High executive officials have absolute immunity in some contexts.
  - Other officials have qualified immunity for discretionary acts in good faith. In federal civil-rights suits, an official is immune unless he violated clearly established law (*Harlow v. Fitzgerald*, 457 U.S. 800 (1982)).

#### Other defenses: limitations periods and workers' compensation exclusivity
- **Statutes of limitations** (R-TRT-076), California:
  - two years for personal injury and wrongful death (CCP § 335.1);
  - one year for libel, slander and false imprisonment (CCP § 340(c));
  - three years for injury to property, trespass and fraud, with fraud running from discovery (CCP § 338);
  - medical negligence under CCP § 340.5;
  - a 10-year repose period for latent construction defects (CCP § 337.15).
  - The discovery rule and tolling for minority apply.
- **Workers' compensation exclusivity:** compensation is the employee's exclusive remedy against the employer for job injuries (Lab. Code §§ 3600, 3602), with narrow exceptions. Claims against third parties remain, e.g., against a product manufacturer. Under *Kuciemba*, a spouse's own claim for her own infection was not barred as "derivative", though it failed for lack of duty [S-700].

## III Strict liability and products liability: common law strict liability, including claims arising from abnormally dangerous activities, and defenses to such claims; claims against manufacturers and other defendants arising out of the manufacture and distribution of products, and defenses to such claims

**Map.** Strict liability means liability without proof of fault. Three common-law categories: abnormally dangerous activities, wild animals, and domestic animals with known dangerous propensities (plus trespassing livestock). Then strict products liability, with negligence, warranty and misrepresentation as parallel product theories. Torts I, III and IV together are about half of the Torts MBE [S-040].

### Common law strict liability: abnormally dangerous activities
- **Origin:** *Rylands v. Fletcher*, L.R. 3 H.L. 330 (1868) (a "non-natural" use of land: a reservoir that flooded a mine).
- **R2d § 519:** one who carries on an abnormally dangerous activity is strictly liable for harm to the person, land or chattels of another resulting from the activity, although he exercised the utmost care. Liability is limited to harm of the kind whose possibility makes the activity abnormally dangerous (R-TRT-077).
- **R2d § 520 factors:**
  - (a) high degree of risk of some harm;
  - (b) likelihood that the harm will be great;
  - (c) inability to eliminate the risk by reasonable care;
  - (d) the activity is not a matter of common usage;
  - (e) inappropriateness to the place where it is carried on;
  - (f) its value to the community is outweighed by its dangerous attributes.
- **R3d PEH § 20:** an activity is abnormally dangerous if it creates a foreseeable and highly significant risk of physical harm even when reasonable care is exercised, and it is not a matter of common usage.
- **Examples:**
  - Usually strict: blasting; storing or transporting large quantities of explosives or highly flammable liquids; fumigation with lethal gas (*Luthringer v. Moore* (1948) 31 Cal.2d 489); toxic-waste disposal; nuclear materials; some crop dusting.
  - Usually not strict: driving cars, ordinary gas or electricity lines, and household activities (common usage).
- **Who may recover:** foreseeable plaintiffs, for the kind of harm that makes the activity dangerous. Example: mink killing their young because of blasting noise is outside the risk in many courts.
- **Intervening causes:** strict liability applies even though the harm is triggered by an unexpected act of a third person, an animal or a force of nature (R2d § 522) [VERIFY V-600].
- **Tested (MBE):** whether an activity is abnormally dangerous (a question of law for the court); the "kind of harm" limit; defenses.

### Animals (wild animals, domestic animals, livestock, California dog-bite statute)
- **Wild animals** (R-TRT-078; R2d §§ 506–507 [VERIFY V-600]):
  - The possessor is strictly liable for harm from dangerous propensities characteristic of the species, even if the animal was tamed.
  - Trespassers usually must prove negligence.
  - Harm not arising from the dangerous propensity (tripping over a sleeping tiger) needs negligence.
- **Domestic animals** (R-TRT-079; R2d § 509 [VERIFY V-600]):
  - Strict liability only if the possessor knew or had reason to know of the animal's abnormally dangerous propensity, and the harm resulted from it.
  - Otherwise negligence: leash laws are often the basis for negligence per se.
- **Livestock** (R2d § 504 [VERIFY V-600]): strict liability for foreseeable harm done by trespassing livestock, except on highways and in "fence-out" jurisdictions.
- **CA note:** a dog owner is strictly liable for damages to any person bitten in a public place or lawfully in a private place, including the owner's property, regardless of the dog's former viciousness or the owner's knowledge (Civ. Code § 3342).
  - Bites only: other dog injuries follow the common-law rule or negligence.
  - Exceptions for police and military dogs on duty.
  - Comparative fault applies, and professionals who take dogs into their care may be barred by primary assumption of risk (*Priebe v. Nelson* (2006) 39 Cal.4th 1112) [VERIFY V-611].
- **Tested:** a chimpanzee bite and a puppy bite at a breeder's home (Feb 2019 Q2) [VERIFY V-071].

### Defenses to strict liability claims
(R-TRT-080; R2d §§ 523–524A [VERIFY V-600])

- **Contributory negligence** in failing to discover or guard against the danger: no defense (contributory states).
- **Knowing and unreasonable exposure:** a defense (§ 524(2)).
- **Assumption of risk:** a defense (§ 523).
- **Comparative fault:** in most states, Π's fault reduces recovery. California applies comparative principles to strict liability (*Daly*).
- **Abnormally sensitive activity of Π:** no strict liability for harm that would not have occurred but for Π's abnormal sensitivity (§ 524A).
- **Statutory authorization:** may defeat strict liability for public-duty activities in some jurisdictions.

### Products liability (strict liability, negligence, warranty, misrepresentation, intent, defenses)

**Essay order for a products call:**
1. Strict products liability: proper Δ, defect by type, causation, damages.
2. Negligence.
3. Warranty: express, merchantability, fitness.
4. Misrepresentation (R2d § 402B; fraud; negligent).
5. Intentional tort (briefly).
6. Defenses.

The July 2023 Q2 selected answers followed this order [VERIFY V-071].

#### Strict products liability: the prima facie case
- **Elements** (R-TRT-081):
  1. Δ is a commercial supplier.
  2. The product was defective when it left Δ's control.
  3. The product reached Π without substantial change.
  4. The defect actually and proximately caused
  5. physical harm to person or other property.
- **Authority:** *Greenman v. Yuba Power Products, Inc.* (1963) 59 Cal.2d 57; R2d § 402A ("defective condition unreasonably dangerous"); R3d PL §§ 1–2.
- **CA note:** Π need not prove the product was "unreasonably dangerous" (*Cronin v. J.B.E. Olson Corp.* (1972) 8 Cal.3d 121).
- **Policy:** loss spreading, deterrence, and consumers' inability to prove fault (*Escola v. Coca Cola Bottling Co.* (1944) 24 Cal.2d 453, Traynor, J., concurring).

#### Types of defect
| Defect | Test | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Manufacturing** (R-TRT-082) | Departs from the intended design; differs from other units (R3d PL § 2(a)) | Liability even with all possible care; the malfunction doctrine allows circumstantial proof (R3d PL § 3) |
| **Design** (R-TRT-083) | Consumer expectations: failed to perform as safely as an ordinary consumer would expect in intended or reasonably foreseeable use; **or** risk-utility: risks outweigh benefits, usually shown by a reasonable alternative design (R3d PL § 2(b)) | **CA:** *Barker v. Lull Engineering Co.* (1978) 20 Cal.3d 413: either test; under risk-benefit, once Π shows the design caused the injury, **Δ bears the burden** of proving benefits outweigh risks. *Soule v. General Motors Corp.* (1994) 8 Cal.4th 548: consumer expectations only where everyday experience lets consumers form safety expectations |
| **Warning** (R-TRT-084) | Foreseeable risks, known or reasonably knowable at distribution, that reasonable warnings or instructions would have reduced (R3d PL § 2(c)) | **CA:** knowability required even in strict liability (*Anderson v. Owens-Corning Fiberglas Corp.* (1991) 53 Cal.3d 987). No duty to warn of obvious or generally known dangers. Learned intermediary for prescription products. Sophisticated user (*Johnson v. American Standard, Inc.* (2008) 43 Cal.4th 56) |

**Risk-benefit factors (*Barker*).**
- The gravity of the danger posed by the design.
- The likelihood that the danger would occur.
- The mechanical feasibility of a safer alternative design.
- The financial cost of an improved design.
- The adverse consequences to the product and to the consumer of an alternative design.

**Prescription drugs and devices.**
- **CA note:** no strict liability for design defects in prescription drugs (*Brown v. Superior Court* (1988) 44 Cal.3d 1049, following R2d § 402A cmt. k). Strict liability for failure to warn does apply (*Carlin v. Superior Court* (1996) 13 Cal.4th 1104).
- **Learned intermediary:** warnings go to the physician. *Himes v. Somatics, LLC* (Cal. June 20, 2024) [S-708] [VERIFY V-605] held that Π may establish causation by showing a stronger warning would have altered the physician's conduct, e.g., the physician would have conveyed the stronger warning and an objectively prudent patient would have declined the treatment.
- **No "duty to innovate":** a maker of a nondefective drug owes no negligence duty regarding when to bring a safer alternative to market (*Gilead Tenofovir Cases*, Cal. Aug. 3, 2026) [S-709] [VERIFY V-603].

**Post-sale duties.** Some courts recognize a duty to warn of defects discovered after sale (R3d PL § 10). A duty to recall exists only if a statute requires it or Δ voluntarily undertakes one (R3d PL § 11).

#### Proper defendants and plaintiffs
- **Defendants** (R-TRT-085):
  - manufacturers;
  - component makers (if the component was defective, or the maker substantially participated in integrating it);
  - distributors, wholesalers and retailers (*Vandermark v. Ford Motor Co.* (1964) 61 Cal.2d 256);
  - commercial lessors and bailors (*Price v. Shell Oil Co.* (1970) 2 Cal.3d 245).
- **Not strictly liable:** casual sellers; service providers (*Murphy v. E.R. Squibb & Sons, Inc.* (1985) 40 Cal.3d 672: a pharmacist); landlords (*Peterson*); sellers of used goods, usually (R3d PL § 8).
- **Others' replacement parts:** no liability for harm caused by another manufacturer's replacement parts or products used with Δ's product, unless Δ's own product contributed substantially to the harm or Δ participated in the system (*O'Neil v. Crane Co.* (2012) 53 Cal.4th 335).
- **Successors:** liable only on express or implied assumption, de facto merger, mere continuation, or fraud. **CA note:** a successor that continues the predecessor's product line may also be liable (*Ray v. Alad Corp.* (1977) 19 Cal.3d 22).
- **Plaintiffs** (R-TRT-086): users, consumers and foreseeable bystanders (*Elmore v. American Motors Corp.* (1969) 70 Cal.2d 578). Privity is irrelevant.
- **Harm:** personal injury and damage to other property. Pure economic loss is excluded (*Seely*).

#### Products negligence
(R-TRT-088)

- *MacPherson v. Buick Motor Co.*, 217 N.Y. 382 (1916) abolished the privity requirement. A manufacturer owes reasonable care in design, manufacture, inspection, testing and warning to all foreseeably endangered.
- Breach may be shown by res ipsa or by proving a defect that reasonable care would have prevented.
- Retailers have no duty to inspect unless they have reason to know of a danger. They are liable for their own negligence (e.g., improper assembly).
- Negligence is the main theory against non-commercial or service Δs, and it carries punitive-damages facts more naturally.

#### Products warranty
(R-TRT-089; see R-CTR-057 to R-CTR-061)

- **Express warranty** (UCC § 2-313): an affirmation of fact or promise, a description, or a sample or model that is part of the basis of the bargain. Puffery does not count. Labels and advertising can create express warranties.
- **Implied warranty of merchantability** (§ 2-314): a merchant seller of goods of that kind; the goods must be fit for their ordinary purposes and adequately labeled.
- **Implied warranty of fitness for a particular purpose** (§ 2-315): the seller has reason to know the particular purpose and that the buyer relies on its skill or judgment.
- **Privity** (§ 2-318 alternatives): A extends to the buyer's household and guests; B to natural persons reasonably expected to use, consume or be affected who suffer personal injury; C to any person, for any injury. Most courts relax privity for personal injury.
- **Disclaimers** (§ 2-316); limitations on remedies (§ 2-719(3): limiting consequential damages for personal injury from consumer goods is prima facie unconscionable).
- **Notice of breach** within a reasonable time (§ 2-607(3)(a)).
- **Limitations:** four years from tender (§ 2-725).
- **Damages:** personal injury (consequential) and economic loss (unlike strict tort).

#### Products misrepresentation and intentional torts
- **R2d § 402B** (R-TRT-090): a commercial seller's public misrepresentation of material fact about a product's character or quality creates strict liability for physical harm from justifiable reliance, even without fraud or negligence.
- **Fraud** (R-TRT-107) and **negligent misrepresentation** (R-TRT-109) also apply where their elements are met. Fraud supports punitive damages.
- **Intentional torts** (R-TRT-091): battery requires purpose or substantial certainty that the product will cause harmful contact. Statistical knowledge of risk is not enough. On an essay, mention this and conclude it likely fails unless the facts show specific knowledge.

#### Defenses in products cases
(R-TRT-087)

- **Comparative fault** reduces recovery (*Daly*). In contributory-negligence states, a mere failure to discover the defect is no defense.
- **Assumption of risk:** Π knowingly and voluntarily used a product with a known defect.
- **Misuse:** unforeseeable misuse defeats defect or proximate cause. Foreseeable misuse (standing on a chair) does not; the design or warnings must address it.
- **Substantial alteration** after sale that caused the harm, if unforeseeable.
- **State of the art:** relevant to design feasibility and warning knowability; irrelevant to manufacturing defects.
- **Compliance with government standards:** evidence of non-defectiveness, not conclusive (R3d PL § 4(b)).
- **Federal preemption:**
  - Premarket-approved medical devices preempt state-law claims imposing different requirements (*Riegel v. Medtronic, Inc.*, 552 U.S. 312 (2008)).
  - Brand-name drug failure-to-warn claims generally are not preempted (*Wyeth v. Levine*, 555 U.S. 555 (2009)).
- **Sophisticated user or intermediary:** no duty to warn sophisticated users of risks they know or should know (*Johnson v. American Standard*). A supplier may discharge its duty by warning a sophisticated intermediary it reasonably relies on (*Webb v. Special Electric Co.* (2016) 63 Cal.4th 167).
- **Warranty-specific defenses:** disclaimer, lack of notice, privity, the four-year limitations period.
- **CA note:** subsequent remedial measures are admissible in strict products liability (*Ault v. International Harvester Co.* (1974) 13 Cal.3d 113; Evid. Code § 1151), unlike FRE 407. Under Civ. Code § 1714.45, a seller is not liable for inherently unsafe common consumer products known to be unsafe by ordinary consumers (sugar, butter, alcohol) [VERIFY V-609].

**How products is tested.**
- **MBE:** which defect type; whether the retailer is liable (yes, strictly); foreseeable misuse; comparative fault; whether Π must show privity (no); and design-defect tests.
- **California essays:** every theory in order, then defenses (Jul 2023 Q2: a dishwasher detergent whose residue reacted with aluminum cookware) [VERIFY V-071].

## IV Other torts

### IV.A Claims based on nuisance, and defenses

**Private nuisance** (R-TRT-092; R2d §§ 821D, 822, 826).
- **Definition:** a nontrespassory invasion of another's interest in the private use and enjoyment of land.
- **Basis of liability:** the invasion is either (a) intentional and unreasonable, or (b) unintentional but negligent, reckless or abnormally dangerous.
- **Intentional** here means Δ knows the interference is resulting from his conduct, e.g., Δ continues after being told.
- **Substantial:** it would offend, annoy or inconvenience a normal person in the community. Hypersensitive plaintiffs and abnormally sensitive uses (a drive-in theater bothered by light) do not count.
- **Unreasonable:**
  - the gravity of the harm outweighs the utility of Δ's conduct (§ 826(a)); **or**
  - the harm is serious and Δ could compensate for it and stay in business (§ 826(b)).
  - **Gravity factors (§ 827):** extent and character of the harm; social value of Π's use; suitability of Π's use to the locality; Π's burden of avoiding the harm.
  - **Utility factors (§ 828):** social value of Δ's purpose; suitability to the locality; impracticability of preventing the harm.
- **Typical invasions:** noise, smoke, dust, odors, vibration, bright lights, flooding, pollution, and sometimes fear (a halfway house; a funeral home in a residential area).
- **Plaintiffs:** possessors, including tenants and family members in possession.
- **Nuisance vs. trespass:** trespass protects exclusive possession against physical entry; nuisance protects use and enjoyment. The same conduct can be both: dust deposits are trespass in California (*Wilson*), and noise alone is a nuisance.
- **CA note:** Civ. Code § 3479 defines a nuisance as anything injurious to health, indecent or offensive to the senses, or an obstruction to the free use of property, so as to interfere with the comfortable enjoyment of life or property. A nuisance that is not public is private (§ 3481). The interference must be substantial and unreasonable (*San Diego Gas & Electric Co. v. Superior Court* (1996) 13 Cal.4th 893).

**Public nuisance** (R-TRT-093; R2d §§ 821B, 821C).
- **Definition:** an unreasonable interference with a right common to the general public, such as public health, safety, peace, comfort or convenience. It is often shown by a statutory violation.
- **Who sues:** public officials sue to abate. A private person must show **special injury** different in kind from the public's.
- **CA note:**
  - A public nuisance affects at the same time an entire community or neighborhood, or any considerable number of persons (Civ. Code § 3480).
  - A private person may sue if it is specially injurious to himself (§ 3493).
  - No lapse of time legalizes a public nuisance (§ 3490).

**Defenses** (R-TRT-094).
- **Coming to the nuisance:** a factor, not a bar (R2d § 840D) [VERIFY V-600].
- **Zoning and permits:** relevant to reasonableness, not conclusive.
- **Statutory authorization:** a defense only if the statute expressly authorizes the specific conduct (Civ. Code § 3482, construed narrowly).
- **Contributory or comparative negligence:** a defense only where the nuisance rests on negligence.
- **Right-to-farm statutes:** Civ. Code § 3482.5 [VERIFY V-609].
- **Assumption of risk:** sometimes.

**Remedies** (R-TRT-095; see Remedies R-REM-024, R-REM-055, R-REM-060, R-REM-062).
- **Damages:**
  - For a **permanent** nuisance, past and future loss (diminution in value) in one action.
  - For a **continuing** (abatable) nuisance, damages accrued so far, with new actions for later periods.
- **Injunction:** inadequate legal remedy, balancing of hardships, the public interest. A court may award permanent damages in lieu of an injunction (*Boomer v. Atlantic Cement Co.*, 26 N.Y.2d 219 (1970)), or require Π to pay to move Δ (in a coming-to-the-nuisance case).
- **Self-help abatement:** reasonable means, after notice, without breaching the peace (Civ. Code § 3501 et seq.) [VERIFY V-609].

**Tested:**
- smoke and smells from a neighbor's smokehouse (Feb 2018 Q3, with trespass and takings);
- noise from an open-air theater next to a later-built subdivision (Feb 2022 Q3, with trespass and remedies) [VERIFY V-071].

The essay pattern: private nuisance (substantial + unreasonable, balancing), public nuisance (special injury), trespass if there is any physical entry, defenses (coming to the nuisance), then the injunction analysis.

### IV.B Claims based on defamation and invasion of privacy, defenses, and constitutional limitations

#### Defamation

**Common-law elements** (R-TRT-096).
1. **Defamatory statement:** it tends to harm reputation. Meaning is judged by a reasonable recipient. Extrinsic facts may be pleaded (inducement and innuendo).
2. **Of and concerning Π:** reasonably understood to refer to Π (colloquium).
   - **Groups:** members of a small group can sue (e.g., "the 6 officers on the night shift took bribes"); members of a large group cannot.
   - **Who can be defamed:** corporations and other entities, for statements about their business. The dead cannot.
3. **Publication:** communication to at least one person other than Π, intentionally or negligently.
   - Each republication is a new publication. Repeaters are liable as if original publishers.
   - **Single-publication rule:** one edition of a book or newspaper is one publication.
   - Online platforms are not treated as the publisher of user content (47 U.S.C. § 230(c)(1)).
4. **Damages** (see below).
5. **Constitutional elements:** falsity and fault, where required (below).

**Libel vs. slander** (R-TRT-097).

| | Libel | Slander |
|---|---|---|
| Form | Written, printed, pictures, permanent forms; usually broadcasts | Spoken, transitory |
| General damages | Presumed (majority) | Presumed only for slander per se |
| Special damages | Not required (majority); some states require them for libel per quod | Required unless per se |
| Per se categories | — | (1) serious crime (moral turpitude); (2) loathsome disease; (3) business, trade, profession or office; (4) serious sexual misconduct |

**CA note:**
- Libel is a false and unprivileged publication by writing, printing, picture, effigy or other fixed representation to the eye that exposes Π to hatred, contempt, ridicule or obloquy, causes him to be shunned or avoided, or injures him in his occupation (Civ. Code § 45).
- **Libel on its face** needs no proof of special damages. Libel that is defamatory only by reference to extrinsic facts requires special damages (Civ. Code § 45a).
- **Slander per se** categories (Civ. Code § 46): charging a crime; imputing an infectious, contagious or loathsome disease; tending directly to injure Π in his office, profession, trade or business; imputing impotence or want of chastity; or words that by natural consequence cause actual damage.

**Constitutional overlay** (R-TRT-098, R-TRT-099).

| Plaintiff / speech | Falsity | Fault | Presumed and punitive damages |
|---|---|---|---|
| Public official or public figure | Π proves falsity | **Actual malice**, by clear and convincing evidence (*New York Times Co. v. Sullivan*, 376 U.S. 254 (1964); *Curtis Publishing Co. v. Butts*, 388 U.S. 130 (1967)) | With actual malice |
| Private figure, matter of **public concern** | Π proves falsity (*Philadelphia Newspapers, Inc. v. Hepps*, 475 U.S. 767 (1986)) | At least **negligence** (*Gertz v. Robert Welch, Inc.*, 418 U.S. 323 (1974)); California requires negligence (*Brown v. Kelly Broadcasting Co.* (1989) 48 Cal.3d 711 [S-717]) | Only with actual malice; otherwise actual injury only |
| Private figure, **private concern** | State law (common law: Δ proves truth) | State law | Allowed without actual malice (*Dun & Bradstreet, Inc. v. Greenmoss Builders, Inc.*, 472 U.S. 749 (1985)) |

- **Actual malice** means knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard: Δ in fact entertained serious doubts (*St. Amant v. Thompson*, 390 U.S. 727 (1968)). It is subjective. Failure to investigate, alone, is not enough. Spite is not actual malice.
- **Public figures:**
  - **all-purpose:** pervasive fame or power;
  - **limited-purpose:** voluntarily thrust into a particular public controversy to influence its outcome, for statements about that controversy;
  - **involuntary:** rare.
- **Public concern** is judged by content, form and context (*Dun & Bradstreet*: a credit report sent to five subscribers was private concern).
- **Opinion:** there is no separate constitutional privilege. A statement is actionable only if it asserts or implies a provably false fact (*Milkovich v. Lorain Journal Co.*, 497 U.S. 1 (1990)). Rhetorical hyperbole and loose, figurative language are protected; "In my opinion, he is a thief" implies facts.

**Defenses** (R-TRT-100).
1. **Truth:** substantial truth is enough. At common law Δ bears the burden. Under the Constitution, Π bears it where the speech is of public concern.
2. **Absolute privileges:**
   - judicial proceedings (judges, lawyers, parties, witnesses, jurors; the statement must relate to the proceeding);
   - legislative proceedings;
   - high executive officials acting in their duties;
   - communications between spouses;
   - publications required by law (equal-time broadcasts).
3. **Qualified (conditional) privileges:**
   - protecting the publisher's own interest;
   - protecting the recipient's or a third person's interest (job references, credit reports);
   - common interest (members of a business, family or organization);
   - communications to public officers to report crime or misconduct;
   - fair and accurate reports of official proceedings and public meetings.
4. **Abuse** defeats a qualified privilege: knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard; in some states, a primary purpose of ill will; publication beyond what the privilege's purpose requires; or including unprivileged matter.
5. **Consent.**
6. **Retraction:** reduces damages at common law. **CA note:** Civ. Code § 48a [S-713] [VERIFY V-606]. For a publication in a daily or weekly news publication or a broadcast, Π recovers only special damages unless Π served a correction demand within 20 days after knowledge of the publication and the correction was not published.

**CA note: Civil Code § 47.** Privileged publications include those made:
- (a) in the proper discharge of an official duty;
- (b) in any legislative, judicial or other official proceeding authorized by law (the absolute **litigation privilege**, which bars every tort claim based on the communication except malicious prosecution, *Silberg v. Anderson* (1990) 50 Cal.3d 205);
- (c) without malice, to a person interested, by one also interested or with a reasonable relationship to the person interested, or on request (common interest; employment references);
- (d) as a fair and true report in a public journal of a judicial, legislative or other public official proceeding;
- (e) as a fair and true report of a public meeting.

**Anti-SLAPP:** CCP § 425.16 lets a Δ sued for protected speech or petitioning move early to strike the claim. Π must then show a probability of prevailing, and a prevailing Δ recovers fees. It is procedural; mention it in one line on a California essay.

**Damages.**
- **General:** presumed, reputational and emotional harm.
- **Special:** specific pecuniary loss.
- **Punitive:** subject to the constitutional limits above and Civ. Code § 3294.
- **Injunctions:** a court may enjoin repetition of statements adjudicated defamatory (*Balboa Island Village Inn, Inc. v. Lemen* (2007) 40 Cal.4th 1141 [S-476]; R-REM-063).

**Tested:**
- a ballplayer's libel suit against a business (Feb 2015 Q5);
- a professor's statements about a student, with IIED (Feb 2020 Q1) [VERIFY V-071].

The order: elements → type (libel/slander, per se) → plaintiff status and constitutional fault → defenses and privileges → damages.

#### Invasion of privacy
Four separate torts (R2d § 652A).
- They protect **natural persons** only, not corporations.
- The right is personal: it ends at death, except statutory publicity rights (Civ. Code § 3344.1).

| Tort | Elements | Key points |
|---|---|---|
| **Appropriation** (R-TRT-101; R2d § 652C) | Use of Π's name or likeness for Δ's benefit (usually commercial), without consent | Newsworthiness and incidental use are exempt. **CA:** statutory claim for knowing use in products or advertising (Civ. Code § 3344); transformative-use First Amendment test (*Comedy III Productions, Inc. v. Gary Saderup, Inc.* (2001) 25 Cal.4th 387) |
| **Intrusion upon seclusion** (R-TRT-102; R2d § 652B) | Intentional intrusion, physical or otherwise, into a private place, conversation or matter; highly offensive to a reasonable person | No publication needed; reasonable expectation of privacy; not for public places. **CA:** *Shulman v. Group W Productions, Inc.* (1998) 18 Cal.4th 200 |
| **Public disclosure of private facts** (R-TRT-103; R2d § 652D) | Publicity of private facts, highly offensive to a reasonable person, not of legitimate public concern | **Truth is no defense.** Public-record facts are protected. Newsworthiness defense |
| **False light** (R-TRT-104; R2d § 652E) | Publicity placing Π in a false light highly offensive to a reasonable person; knowledge or reckless disregard | Overlaps defamation; same privileges. Actual malice required for matters of public interest (*Time, Inc. v. Hill*, 385 U.S. 374 (1967)) |

- **Defenses:** consent; newsworthiness (for disclosure and appropriation); defamation privileges (for false light and disclosure); truth (for false light only).
- **CA note:** the California Constitution's privacy right (art. I, § 1) is enforceable against private parties. Π must show (1) a legally protected privacy interest, (2) a reasonable expectation of privacy, and (3) a serious invasion, balanced against countervailing interests (*Hill v. National Collegiate Athletic Assn.* (1994) 7 Cal.4th 1).

### Misuse of legal process: malicious prosecution and abuse of process (Intentional injuries to reputation or similar interests)
The State Bar's essay outline groups defamation, privacy, malicious prosecution and abuse of process as intentional injuries to reputation or similar interests [S-092] [VERIFY V-024]. The NCBE MBE outline does not list malicious prosecution separately.

**Malicious prosecution** (R-TRT-105; R2d § 653 [VERIFY V-600]).
1. Δ instituted or continued criminal proceedings against Π. Giving false information that causes police to prosecute counts; merely giving truthful information to police does not.
2. The proceedings terminated in Π's favor, in a way that reflects on Π's innocence. Acquittal or dismissal for lack of evidence qualifies; settlement or plea bargains do not.
3. Absence of probable cause.
4. Malice: a primary purpose other than bringing an offender to justice.
5. Damages.

- **Wrongful use of civil proceedings** (R2d § 674 [VERIFY V-600]): the same elements for civil suits. Some states also require special injury (arrest, seizure of property).
- **CA note:**
  - Malicious prosecution lies for civil actions.
  - Probable cause is an objective legal question: whether any reasonable attorney would have thought the claim tenable (*Sheldon Appel Co. v. Albert & Oliker* (1989) 47 Cal.3d 863).
  - Continuing to prosecute after discovering the claim lacks probable cause is actionable (*Zamos v. Stroud* (2004) 32 Cal.4th 958).
  - The litigation privilege (Civ. Code § 47(b)) does not bar malicious prosecution.
- **Immunity:** prosecutors have absolute immunity.

**Abuse of process** (R-TRT-106; R2d § 682 [VERIFY V-600]).
- **Elements:** (1) an ulterior purpose; (2) a willful act in the use of process not proper in the regular conduct of the proceeding; (3) damages.
- Typical examples: using a subpoena, attachment or deposition to coerce payment of an unrelated claim. Merely filing a suit with a bad motive is not enough.
- **CA note:** the litigation privilege bars abuse-of-process claims based on communicative acts in litigation, such as filing pleadings [VERIFY V-610].

### IV.C Claims based on misrepresentations, and defenses

**Intentional misrepresentation (fraud, deceit)** (R-TRT-107; R2d § 525).
1. **Misrepresentation of material fact:**
   - by words or conduct, or by active concealment (painting over cracks);
   - **half-truths** that mislead;
   - **opinions** are actionable when Δ has superior knowledge, is a fiduciary, or the opinion implies false facts;
   - **predictions** generally are not;
   - **promissory fraud:** a promise made with no intention to perform.
2. **Scienter:** knowledge of falsity, or reckless disregard (no belief in its truth) (*Derry v. Peek*, 14 App. Cas. 337 (H.L. 1889)).
3. **Intent to induce reliance,** by Π or a class including Π.
4. **Actual reliance.**
5. **Justifiable reliance:** no duty to investigate unless the falsity is obvious. Reliance on puffery or obvious falsehoods is unjustifiable. Materiality matters.
6. **Pecuniary damages:**
   - **out-of-pocket:** value paid minus value received;
   - **benefit of the bargain:** value as represented minus value received (majority for fraud);
   - consequential and punitive damages.
   - **CA note:** in purchases and sales of property, Civ. Code § 3343 limits recovery to the out-of-pocket measure plus specified additional damages; other contexts follow Civ. Code § 3333 (R-REM-023) [S-453].

**CA note.**
- Civ. Code § 1709 (liability for deceit).
- Civ. Code § 1710 defines deceit as:
  1. suggestion as a fact of what is not true, by one who does not believe it;
  2. assertion of what is not true, by one who has no reasonable ground for believing it (negligent misrepresentation);
  3. suppression of a fact by one bound to disclose it, or who gives information likely to mislead for want of communication of that fact;
  4. a promise made without any intention of performing it.
- Fraud in the inducement of employment survives the economic loss rule (*Lazar v. Superior Court* (1996) 12 Cal.4th 631).

**Nondisclosure and concealment** (R-TRT-108).
- Active concealment is actionable. Silence generally is not, unless there is a duty to disclose:
  1. a fiduciary or confidential relationship;
  2. a half-truth;
  3. a statement that was true when made but that Δ learns is now false, or a statement Δ learns was false;
  4. facts basic to the transaction that Δ knows Π is mistaken about and cannot reasonably discover (R2d § 551 [VERIFY V-600]).
- **Real estate:** sellers must disclose known material defects not reasonably observable by the buyer. **CA note:** *Lingsch v. Savage* (1963) 213 Cal.App.2d 729 [VERIFY V-611]; statutory transfer disclosure statements.
- **Fraud during contract performance: *Rattagan v. Uber Technologies, Inc.* (2024) 17 Cal.5th 1 [S-132] (Apply).** The economic loss rule does not bar a fraudulent-concealment claim between contracting parties where:
  - (1) Π pleads all elements of the tort independently of the contract; and
  - (2) Δ's conduct exposed Π to a risk of harm beyond the parties' reasonable contemplation at contracting.

  This is the Contracts–Torts crossover point (R-CTR-117; R-REM-032).

**Negligent misrepresentation** (R-TRT-109; R2d § 552).
- **Elements:**
  - a false statement of fact made in the course of Δ's business or profession, or in a transaction in which Δ has a pecuniary interest;
  - supplied for the guidance of others in their business transactions;
  - lack of reasonable care in obtaining or communicating the information;
  - Π belongs to the limited group Δ intended (or knew the recipient intended) to guide, in the type of transaction Δ intended to influence;
  - justifiable reliance;
  - pecuniary loss (out-of-pocket measure).
- **Defenses:** comparative negligence applies (unlike fraud); the limited class of plaintiffs.
- **CA note:**
  - Auditors are liable in negligence only to their client.
  - Third parties may sue for negligent misrepresentation only if they are specifically intended beneficiaries of the report (§ 552 approach).
  - Any third party may sue for fraud (*Bily v. Arthur Young & Co.* (1992) 3 Cal.4th 370).
  - Negligent misrepresentation requires a positive assertion, not an omission.

**Misrepresentation causing physical harm.** R2d §§ 310–311 [VERIFY V-600] (negligent misrepresentation involving risk of physical harm) and § 402B (products, R-TRT-090).

**Defenses to misrepresentation:**
- no justifiable reliance;
- no materiality;
- puffery or opinion;
- the economic loss rule (for negligent misrepresentation between contracting parties);
- limitations (CCP § 338(d): three years from discovery);
- the parol evidence rule does not bar evidence of fraud.

**Tested:**
- a home buyer against a broker (Feb 2014 Q6, with remedies);
- a product marketed as "safe" (Jul 2023 Q2);
- a fraud suit with damages and equitable remedies (Jul 2024 Q5) [VERIFY V-071].

### IV.D Claims based on intentional interference with business relations, and defenses
NCBE's inclusion of IV.D on the MBE outline is unconfirmed [VERIFY V-023]. It is on the California essay outline as economic torts [S-092].

**Interference with contractual relations** (R-TRT-110; R2d § 766).
- **Elements:**
  1. A valid contract between Π and a third party. Contracts that are void or illegal do not count. Voidable and at-will contracts count, subject to the privileges.
  2. Δ's knowledge of the contract.
  3. Intentional acts designed to induce breach or disrupt performance.
  4. Actual breach or disruption. **CA note:** disruption or increased cost suffices (*Pacific Gas & Electric Co. v. Bear Stearns & Co.* (1990) 50 Cal.3d 1118).
  5. Damages.
  6. Improper conduct, weighed under the § 767 factors: the nature of the conduct; Δ's motive; the interests interfered with and advanced; social interests; proximity of the conduct to the interference; and the relations of the parties.
- **Privileges and justifications:**
  - competition for an **at-will** relationship by proper means (§ 768);
  - a financial interest in the third party (a parent company);
  - responsibility for another's welfare (a parent advising a child);
  - truthful information or honest advice on request (§ 772) [VERIFY V-600].
- **CA note:**
  - Interference with an **at-will** contract requires an **independently wrongful act** (*Ixchel Pharma, LLC v. Biogen, Inc.* (2020) 9 Cal.5th 1130 [S-716], extending *Reeves v. Hanlon* (2004) 33 Cal.4th 1140 on at-will employment).
  - A contracting party cannot be liable in tort for interfering with its own contract (*Applied Equipment Corp. v. Litton Saudi Arabia Ltd.* (1994) 7 Cal.4th 503).
- **Damages:** the pecuniary loss of the contract's benefits, consequential losses, emotional distress, punitive damages. The breaching party remains liable on the contract.

**Interference with prospective economic advantage** (R-TRT-111; R2d § 766B).
- **Elements:**
  1. An economic relationship with a probability of future benefit (a prospective customer, a bid, an inheritance expectancy in some states).
  2. Δ's knowledge.
  3. Intentional acts designed to disrupt.
  4. Actual disruption.
  5. Damages.
- **Improper means:** wrongful means include fraud, defamation, violence, threats, bribery, unfair competition, antitrust violations and misuse of confidential information. Competition by lawful means is privileged.
- **CA note:**
  - Π must prove the act was **independently wrongful**, meaning proscribed by a constitutional, statutory, regulatory, common-law or other determinable legal standard (*Della Penna v. Toyota Motor Sales, U.S.A., Inc.* (1995) 11 Cal.4th 376; *Korea Supply Co. v. Lockheed Martin Corp.* (2003) 29 Cal.4th 1134).
  - **Negligent** interference is recognized where a special relationship exists (*J'Aire Corp. v. Gregory* (1979) 24 Cal.3d 799).

**Injurious falsehood (trade libel, slander of title)** (R-TRT-112; R2d § 623A [VERIFY V-600]).
- **Elements:**
  - a false statement disparaging Π's property, products or business, or Π's title;
  - published to third persons;
  - Δ knew it was false or acted in reckless disregard;
  - special damages (specific lost sales) must be proved.
- **Privileges:** the defamation privileges apply; a competitor may make general claims of superiority (puffery); a rival claimant may assert a good-faith claim of title.

## Damages, wrongful death and survival (cross-cutting)

### Compensatory and punitive damages in tort
- **Compensatory damages** (R-TRT-113; Civ. Code § 3333 [S-469]): all detriment proximately caused.
  - **Economic:** medical expenses, lost earnings and earning capacity, repair or replacement, loss of use.
  - **Non-economic:** pain, suffering, emotional distress, disfigurement, loss of enjoyment.
  - Future damages are reduced to present value. All items need reasonable certainty.
  - **Collateral source rule:** independent payments (insurance, gifts, employment benefits) do not reduce damages (*Helfend v. Southern Cal. Rapid Transit Dist.* (1970) 2 Cal.3d 1). **CA:** past medical damages are capped at the amount actually paid or incurred (*Howell v. Hamilton Meats & Provisions, Inc.* (2011) 52 Cal.4th 541 [S-472]).
  - **Avoidable consequences:** mitigation.
  - Detailed measures: Remedies R-REM-007 to R-REM-025.
- **Nominal damages:** for the intentional torts that need no actual harm. Not for negligence.
- **Punitive damages** (R-TRT-114):
  - **Standard:** malice, oppression or fraud, by clear and convincing evidence (Civ. Code § 3294 [S-451]).
  - **Employers:** liable only through an officer, director or managing agent (§ 3294(b)).
  - **Proof:** evidence of Δ's financial condition is required (*Adams v. Murakami* (1991) 54 Cal.3d 105 [S-464]).
  - **Due process:** *BMW of North America, Inc. v. Gore*, 517 U.S. 559 (1996); *State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co. v. Campbell*, 538 U.S. 408 (2003).
  - **Not available:** against public entities (Gov. Code § 818) [VERIFY V-607] or in wrongful-death actions.
  - **Available** in survival actions.
- **CA damages statutes:**
  - **Proposition 51** (Civ. Code § 1431.2), R-TRT-042.
  - **Proposition 213** (Civ. Code § 3333.4) [S-720] [VERIFY V-608]: in motor-vehicle cases, uninsured owners or operators, and drivers convicted of DUI in connection with the accident, cannot recover non-economic damages.
  - **MICRA** (Civ. Code § 3333.2), R-TRT-115. The non-economic damages cap is on the AB 35 schedule: $350,000 (injury) and $500,000 (death) from Jan 1, 2023, plus $40,000 and $50,000 each year. That gives **$510,000 and $700,000 in 2027** [S-701] [S-702] [S-703] [VERIFY V-601].

### Wrongful death and survival (CBX III)
**Wrongful death** (R-TRT-059; CCP §§ 377.60–377.62).
- **Purpose:** a statutory action for the heirs' **own** losses from the death.
- **Beneficiaries (§ 377.60):**
  - the surviving spouse, domestic partner, children, and issue of deceased children;
  - if none, those who would take by intestate succession;
  - plus, if dependent on the decedent: a putative spouse, children of a putative spouse, stepchildren, parents, and minors who lived with the decedent for the prior 180 days and received half their support from the decedent.
- **Damages:** loss of financial support, gifts and household services; funeral and burial expenses; and the loss of the decedent's love, companionship, comfort, care, assistance, protection, affection, society and moral support.
- **Not recoverable:** the heirs' grief and sorrow, the decedent's pain and suffering, and punitive damages.
- **Defenses:** the decedent's comparative fault reduces recovery. Δ may raise any defense it had against the decedent.
- **Procedure:** a single joint action; two-year limitations period from death (CCP § 335.1).

**Survival** (R-TRT-060; CCP §§ 377.20, 377.30, 377.34).
- **Purpose:** the decedent's own cause of action survives and passes to the personal representative or successor in interest.
- **Damages:** the loss the decedent sustained before death (medical expenses, lost earnings until death, property damage) and punitive damages the decedent could have recovered.
- **Pain, suffering and disfigurement are excluded.** The exception in § 377.34(b) covered actions filed Jan 1, 2022 through Dec 31, 2025 (and earlier cases granted trial preference). It **expired**; SB 29 (2025), which would have extended or removed the sunset, was moved to the inactive file and not enacted [S-704] [S-705] [S-723]. Check for any 2026–2027 legislation before the exam [VERIFY V-602].
- **Common law:** personal tort actions abated at death; survival statutes reverse that.

**MBE:** assume both actions are available [S-040]. Know the difference:
- **Survival:** the estate recovers what the decedent could have recovered up to death.
- **Wrongful death:** the survivors recover their own losses after death.

**Essay:** after a fatal accident, write two headings: "Survival action (Estate v. Δ)" and "Wrongful death (Heirs v. Δ)".
