# Evidence — outline

**How to read this outline.**
- Headings follow the NCBE MBE Subject Matter Outline for Evidence (I–V) [S-040]. Each rule cites its Federal Rule of Evidence (FRE) and, in a **CA note**, the California Evidence Code (CEC or "§") counterpart.
- The **MBE** asks for the FRE "as currently in effect" [S-040]. **California essays** name either the FRE or the CEC in the call, and the State Bar's scope statement asks you to compare and contrast them [S-083]. Every material FRE–CEC difference is collected in [`ca-distinctions.md`](ca-distinctions.md).
- Rule IDs (`R-EVD-###`) point to the essay-ready statements in [`rule-statements.md`](rule-statements.md).
- Current law is the law in effect on **July 27, 2027**: FRE amendments of 2023 (106, 615, 702), 2024 (107, 613(b), 801(d)(2), 804(b)(3), 1006) and the **Dec. 1, 2026 amendment of 801(d)(1)(A)** are built in [S-104] [S-105] [S-101]. See `00-exam-intel/recent-changes.md` §§ 2 and 5.

**The one-paragraph map.** Every item of evidence must be (1) **relevant** (FRE 401–402; § 210, § 350–351) and not excluded by **discretionary balancing** (FRE 403; § 352); (2) offered in proper **form** by a **competent** witness with **personal knowledge**; (3) free of **policy exclusions** (character, remedial measures, settlement, insurance, pleas, rape shield) and **privileges**; (4) **authenticated** and, if its contents are being proved, compliant with the **best evidence / secondary evidence rule**; and (5) if it is an out-of-court statement offered for its truth, within a **hearsay exclusion or exception**, plus, in a criminal case, consistent with the **Confrontation Clause**. Witnesses can then be **impeached** and **rehabilitated**.

## I Presentation of evidence

About one-quarter of the Evidence MBE (~6 of 25 scored questions) [S-040]. On essays, these rules supply the objections to the *form* of testimony and the impeachment issues that sit under almost every item.

### I.A Introduction of evidence

#### I.A.1 Requirement of personal knowledge
- **Rule (R-EVD-009).** A lay witness may testify to a matter only if evidence is introduced sufficient to support a finding that the witness has personal knowledge of it; the witness's own testimony can supply that foundation (FRE 602). Expert witnesses are exempt (FRE 602, 703).
- **Standard.** Personal knowledge is a **conditional-relevance** question (FRE 104(b)): the judge asks only whether a reasonable jury could find the witness perceived the event. Firm certainty is not required; "I think" or "I believe" based on actual perception is acceptable, but a guess or a report of what others said is not.
- **Hearsay link.** A witness who repeats what someone else told her lacks personal knowledge of the underlying event; the objection is often both "lack of personal knowledge" and "hearsay". The **declarant** of an admitted hearsay statement must generally have had personal knowledge too (exceptions such as 803(2) and 803(6) assume it; party admissions under 801(d)(2) do not require it).
- **CA note:** § 702 (personal knowledge required; may be shown by the witness's own testimony); § 403(a)(2) (personal knowledge is a jury-determined preliminary fact). Same result.
- **How tested.** MBE: witness who "heard about" the accident; witness testifying to what happened inside a house she never entered. Essays: a one-line objection on any eyewitness item.

#### I.A.2 Refreshing recollection
- **Rule (R-EVD-012).** A witness whose memory fails may look at **anything** (a writing, photo, song) to jog it; the witness then testifies from **revived present memory**, and the item itself is **not evidence** (FRE 612). Contrast **recorded recollection** (FRE 803(5)), where the memory is not revived and the writing substitutes for it (V.E).
- **Adverse party's rights (FRE 612(b)).** If a **writing** is used to refresh **while testifying**, the adverse party is entitled to have it produced, to inspect it, cross-examine on it, and introduce relevant portions. If used **before** testifying, production is in the court's **discretion** "if justice requires" (FRE 612(a)(2)). In criminal cases, the Jencks Act (18 U.S.C. § 3500) controls government witness statements. Failure to produce can lead the court to strike testimony or, for the prosecution, declare a mistrial.
- **Privilege.** Using a privileged document to refresh may waive the privilege as to that document (courts balance under 612(a)(2)).
- **CA note:** § 771 requires production at the adverse party's request **whether the writing was used before or while testifying**; if it is not produced, the witness's testimony on that subject **must be stricken**, unless the writing is neither in the control of the witness or the proponent nor reasonably procurable (§ 771(c)) [S-521]. The adverse party may inspect, cross-examine, and introduce pertinent portions (§ 771(b)). **Essay line:** "California is stricter than FRE 612: pre-testimony refreshing also triggers mandatory production."
- **How tested.** MBE: whether the refreshing document must itself be admissible (no); whether it may be read to the jury by the proponent (no — only the adverse party may introduce it). Essays: police officer reviews report before testifying.

#### I.A.3 Objections and offers of proof
- **Rule (R-EVD-008).** Error in **admitting** evidence is preserved only by a **timely** objection or motion to strike that states the **specific ground**, unless the ground was apparent from context; error in **excluding** evidence is preserved only by an **offer of proof** informing the court of its substance, unless apparent (FRE 103(a)). A ruling must also affect a **substantial right** (harmless-error rule).
- **Definitive pretrial rulings** (motions in limine) need not be renewed at trial (FRE 103(b)). **Plain error** affecting a substantial right may be noticed without objection (FRE 103(e)). The court must conduct jury trials so inadmissible evidence is not suggested to the jury (FRE 103(d)).
- **General vs. specific objection.** A general objection ("objection!") that is overruled preserves nothing unless the ground was obvious; a specific objection on the **wrong** ground does not preserve the right ground. Evidence admitted without objection may be considered for any purpose.
- **Opening the door / curative admissibility.** When one party introduces inadmissible evidence, the court may let the other side respond in kind to cure the prejudice.
- **Luce rule.** A criminal defendant who does not testify cannot appeal a ruling that his prior conviction would be admissible to impeach him (*Luce v. United States*, 469 U.S. 38 (1984)); a defendant who preemptively introduces his own conviction on direct cannot appeal its admission (*Ohler v. United States*, 529 U.S. 753 (2000)).
- **CA note:** § 353 (erroneous admission: timely objection stating the specific ground + miscarriage of justice) and § 354 (erroneous exclusion: offer of proof, unless futile or the substance was made known). Same structure; California's harmless-error test is the "miscarriage of justice" standard of Cal. Const. art. VI, § 13 [S-146].
- **How tested.** MBE: whether an unobjected-to hearsay statement can support a verdict (yes). Essays (Jul 2026 Q5 format): "what objections should have been made and how should the court have ruled" — name each objection precisely.

#### I.A.4 Lay opinions
- **Rule (R-EVD-013).** A lay witness may give an opinion only if it is (a) **rationally based on the witness's perception**, (b) **helpful** to understanding the testimony or determining a fact in issue, and (c) **not based on scientific, technical, or other specialized knowledge** within FRE 702 (FRE 701).
- **Common permitted lay opinions:** speed of a car; intoxication; another's emotional state ("she looked terrified"); identity of a voice or handwriting the witness is familiar with (FRE 901(b)(2), (5)); sanity of a person well known to the witness; **value of one's own property** (owner testimony, typically allowed); smell, size, distance, weight.
- **Not allowed:** legal conclusions ("he was negligent"), opinions on another's credibility, conclusions that need expertise (cause of a disease), or speculation about what someone else knew or intended (unless rationally based on observed conduct).
- **701(c) and dual witnesses.** A police officer who explains drug-trade jargon is giving expert testimony and must qualify under 702; the 2000 amendment prevents evasion of 702 by "lay" labels.
- **CA note:** § 800 — lay opinion is limited to one (a) rationally based on the witness's perception and (b) helpful to a clear understanding of the testimony. California has no express "(c)" clause, but the § 801 expert standard does the same work. Same result on the facts that recur on essays (Feb 2023 Q5 tested an owner-buyer's value testimony under the FRE [VERIFY V-071]).
- **How tested.** MBE: the "collective facts" opinion ("he was drunk") is admissible; "the defendant was at fault" is not. Essays: lay value opinion; lay identification of handwriting.

#### I.A.5 Competency of witnesses
- **Rule (R-EVD-010).** Every person is competent to be a witness unless the Rules provide otherwise; in a civil case, **state law** governs competency on a claim or defense for which state law supplies the rule of decision (FRE 601). The witness must take an **oath or affirmation** to testify truthfully (FRE 603), have **personal knowledge** (FRE 602), and use a qualified **interpreter** if needed (FRE 604).
- **Abolished common-law incapacities:** infancy, insanity, felony conviction, interest in the case, and spousal incompetency are no longer grounds of incompetency under the FRE; they go to **weight** and **impeachment**. Children testify if they can perceive, remember, communicate and understand the duty to tell the truth.
- **Dead Man's statutes.** The FRE has none; in a diversity case a state Dead Man's statute applies through FRE 601. California repealed its Dead Man's statute; instead, a decedent's statements may be admitted against the estate under the § 1261 hearsay exception (R-EVD-109) [VERIFY V-409].
- **Judges and jurors (R-EVD-011).** The presiding judge may not testify (FRE 605; no objection needed). A juror may not testify before the other jurors at the trial (FRE 606(a)). On an inquiry into the **validity of a verdict**, a juror may not testify about statements during deliberations, the effect of anything on any juror's vote, or mental processes — **except** about (A) **extraneous prejudicial information** improperly brought to the jury's attention, (B) an **outside influence** improperly brought to bear, or (C) a **mistake in entering the verdict** on the form (FRE 606(b)). Juror intoxication is internal (*Tanner v. United States*, 483 U.S. 107 (1987)); a juror's clear statement that he relied on **racial stereotypes or animus** to convict must be heard despite the no-impeachment rule (*Peña-Rodriguez v. Colorado*, 580 U.S. 206 (2017)).
- **Hypnosis.** A per se rule barring a criminal defendant's own hypnotically refreshed testimony violates the right to testify (*Rock v. Arkansas*, 483 U.S. 44 (1987)).
- **CA note:**
  - § 700 (every person qualified unless provided otherwise); § 701 (disqualified if **incapable of expressing himself so as to be understood**, directly or through an interpreter, or **incapable of understanding the duty to tell the truth**). California keeps these two express disqualification grounds; the FRE folds them into 602/603 and 403.
  - § 703 (judge), § 704 (juror): the presiding judge or a sitting juror may testify only if no party objects; an objection requires a mistrial (FRE 605 bars the judge outright) [VERIFY V-991]. § 1150 allows evidence of **statements made, or conduct, conditions, or events occurring**, within or outside the jury room, likely to have influenced the verdict improperly, but not evidence of their effect on a juror's mental processes. California thus admits **overt acts and statements** of jurors (e.g., a juror's statement during deliberations revealing concealed bias) more readily than FRE 606(b).
  - § 795: hypnotically refreshed testimony of a witness is admissible only if the testimony concerns matters recalled and related before the hypnosis and statutory safeguards were followed [VERIFY V-416].
- **How tested.** MBE: six-year-old witness; juror affidavit that another juror researched the case online (extraneous information — admissible). Essays (Jul 2018 Q2 tested a witness's testimony under California law [VERIFY V-071]).

#### I.A.6 Judicial notice
- **Rule (R-EVD-014).** A court may judicially notice an **adjudicative fact** that is **not subject to reasonable dispute** because it is (1) **generally known** within the trial court's territorial jurisdiction or (2) **accurately and readily determined** from sources whose accuracy cannot reasonably be questioned (FRE 201(b)).
- **Procedure.** The court may notice on its own and **must** notice if a party requests it and supplies the necessary information (201(c)); notice may be taken at any stage, including appeal (201(d)); a party is entitled to be heard (201(e)).
- **Effect (FRE 201(f)).** **Civil case:** the court instructs the jury to accept the noticed fact as **conclusive**. **Criminal case:** the court instructs the jury that it **may or may not** accept it (protects the jury-trial right).
- **Scope.** FRE 201 governs only adjudicative facts (the who-what-when of the case). **Legislative facts** (policy facts used to make law) and **law** itself are not governed by 201.
- **CA note:** §§ 450–460. § 451 lists matters that **must** be noticed (e.g., California and federal decisional, constitutional and statutory law; facts and propositions of generalized knowledge so universally known they cannot reasonably be disputed); § 452 lists matters that **may** be noticed (e.g., regulations, records of courts, facts of common knowledge within the court's territorial jurisdiction, facts capable of immediate and accurate determination); § 453 makes § 452 notice **mandatory** on a party's request with notice and sufficient information. Under § 457 the court may, and on request must, instruct the jury to accept the noticed matter as a fact [S-522]. Whether California reproduces FRE 201(f)'s criminal-case "may or may not accept" instruction is unconfirmed [VERIFY V-403]. California notices **law** under the Evidence Code; the FRE does not.
- **How tested.** MBE: the classic split — conclusive in civil, permissive in criminal; noticing that a date fell on a Sunday (calendar = indisputable source).

#### I.A.7 Roles of judge and jury
- **Rule (R-EVD-005).** The **judge** decides preliminary questions about whether a witness is qualified, a privilege exists, or evidence is admissible — by a **preponderance**, and **without being bound by the rules of evidence** except privilege (FRE 104(a); *Bourjaily v. United States*, 483 U.S. 171 (1987)). Examples: hearsay foundations (was the declarant still under stress? was the statement made in furtherance of a conspiracy?), expert qualification and reliability, privilege elements, unavailability.
- **Conditional relevance (R-EVD-006).** When relevance depends on whether a fact exists, the judge admits the evidence if there is proof **sufficient to support a finding** that the fact exists (FRE 104(b)); the **jury** decides whether it does. Examples: authenticity (FRE 901(a)), personal knowledge (602), whether a letter was received, whether the defendant committed a prior act offered under 404(b) (*Huddleston v. United States*, 485 U.S. 681 (1988)).
- **Hearings outside the jury** are required for the admissibility of a **confession**, when a defendant testifies on a preliminary question and asks, or when justice requires (FRE 104(c)). A defendant who testifies on a preliminary matter is not thereby subject to cross-examination on other issues (104(d)). The opponent may still attack **weight and credibility** before the jury (104(e)).
- **Best evidence questions:** the court decides whether a condition for secondary evidence is met; the jury decides whether the writing ever existed, whether another writing is the original, and whether secondary evidence accurately reflects content (FRE 1008).
- **CA note:** §§ 400–406. § 403 lists the jury-type preliminary facts (relevance depending on a preliminary fact; personal knowledge; authenticity; whether the declarant made a statement or engaged in conduct) — proponent produces evidence sufficient to sustain a finding; § 405 gives the court the rest. Same split as FRE 104(a)/(b). Under § 405, California courts generally apply the Evidence Code in deciding preliminary facts (no general equivalent of FRE 104(a)'s "not bound" sentence) [VERIFY V-409].
- **How tested.** MBE: in deciding a co-conspirator foundation, may the judge consider the statement itself (yes — FRE 801(d)(2) says it must be considered, but it is not alone sufficient). Essays: one sentence placing authentication and personal knowledge in the jury's hands.

#### I.A.8 Limited admissibility
- **Rule (R-EVD-007).** If evidence is admissible for one purpose or against one party but not for another purpose or against another party, the court, **on timely request**, must restrict it to its proper scope and **instruct the jury** accordingly (FRE 105).
- **When a limiting instruction is not enough:** the court may exclude under 403 where the risk of misuse substantially outweighs the proper use; and a **nontestifying codefendant's confession** that facially incriminates the defendant cannot be cured by instruction (*Bruton v. United States*, 391 U.S. 123 (1968); V.L).
- **Typical pairings:** prior inconsistent statement for impeachment only (pre-Dec 2026 FRE); 404(b) acts for intent only; subsequent remedial measure for feasibility only; statement offered for effect on listener.
- **CA note:** § 355 (same; instruction on request). Essays reward a sentence at the end of each item: "If admitted, the court should give a limiting instruction (§ 355 / FRE 105)."

### I.B Presumptions
- **Burdens.** The **burden of production** (going forward) requires enough evidence to avoid a directed verdict; the **burden of persuasion** decides who loses if the jury is in equipoise. Standards: preponderance (most civil), clear and convincing (fraud, some defenses), beyond a reasonable doubt (every element of a crime — *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358 (1970)).
- **Presumption vs. inference.** A presumption **requires** the factfinder to find the presumed fact once the basic fact is proved (until rebutted); an inference **permits** it.
- **Civil cases — FRE 301 (R-EVD-015).** Unless a statute or rule provides otherwise, a presumption imposes on the party against whom it is directed the **burden of producing evidence** to rebut it, but **does not shift the burden of persuasion**, which stays on the party who had it originally ("bursting bubble", Thayer view). If rebuttal evidence sufficient to support a finding of the non-existence of the presumed fact is introduced, the presumption disappears, though the jury may still draw a permissible inference from the basic facts.
- **Diversity cases.** State law governs the effect of a presumption on a claim or defense for which state law supplies the rule of decision (FRE 302).
- **Criminal cases (R-EVD-016).** Mandatory presumptions that shift to the defendant the burden of persuasion on an element violate due process (*Sandstrom v. Montana*, 442 U.S. 510 (1979)); **permissive inferences** are valid if the inferred fact is "more likely than not" to flow from the proved fact as applied (*County Court of Ulster County v. Allen*, 442 U.S. 140 (1979)). Instructions must leave the jury free to reject the inference.
- **Common presumptions:** a properly addressed, stamped and mailed letter was received; a person missing without explanation for seven years is dead; legitimacy of a child born during marriage; a driver of an owner's car is the owner's agent (some states); the regularity of official acts.
- **CA note:** §§ 600–606. California **classifies** presumptions:
  - **Presumptions affecting the burden of producing evidence** (§§ 603–604): implement no public policy other than facilitating the action; they **burst** when contrary evidence is introduced (like FRE 301). Examples: mailed letter received (§ 641).
  - **Presumptions affecting the burden of proof** (§§ 605–606): implement a **public policy** and **shift the burden of persuasion** to the opponent. Example: the owner of legal title is presumed to be the owner of full beneficial title, rebuttable only by clear and convincing evidence (§ 662) — important in Community Property.
  - Burden of proof generally rests on the party asserting the claim or defense (§ 500); the default standard is preponderance (§ 115). **Essay line:** "Unlike FRE 301, California presumptions that implement public policy shift the burden of persuasion (§ 606)."
- **How tested.** MBE: effect of rebuttal evidence on a civil presumption; unconstitutional burden-shifting instruction. Essays: rare as a standalone issue; arises in CP crossovers (title presumptions).

### I.C Mode and order

#### I.C.1 Control by court
- **Rule (R-EVD-017).** The court exercises reasonable control over the mode and order of examining witnesses and presenting evidence so as to (1) make those procedures effective for determining the truth, (2) avoid wasting time, and (3) protect witnesses from harassment or undue embarrassment (FRE 611(a)). The court may call and question witnesses itself (FRE 614), and recall witnesses.
- **Order of trial:** opening statements → plaintiff/prosecution case-in-chief → defense case → rebuttal → surrebuttal → closing. Each witness: direct → cross → redirect → recross. The court can vary the order (e.g., let a witness testify out of turn).
- **Illustrative aids** used in argument or testimony are governed by FRE 107 (2024) (II.E).
- **CA note:** § 765 (court's duty to control interrogation, including special protection for young witnesses and witnesses with cognitive impairments [VERIFY V-409]); § 772 (order of examination); § 778 (recall). Same substance.

#### I.C.2 Scope of examination
- **Rule (R-EVD-018).** Cross-examination should not go beyond the **subject matter of the direct** examination and **matters affecting the witness's credibility**; the court may allow inquiry into additional matters as if on direct, in which case leading questions are generally not allowed (FRE 611(b)). Redirect is limited to matters raised on cross; recross to matters raised on redirect.
- **Criminal defendant.** A testifying defendant waives the Fifth Amendment privilege as to matters within the scope of cross; he does not waive it as to unrelated crimes.
- **CA note:** § 773(a) — a witness examined by one party may be cross-examined upon any matter **within the scope of the direct examination** by each other party. § 761 defines cross; § 776 lets a party call an adverse party (or its agent) and examine as if under cross-examination. Same substance; California's text does not separately mention credibility, but credibility is always within scope (§ 780).

#### I.C.3 Form of questions
- **Leading questions (R-EVD-019).** A leading question suggests the answer. It should **not** be used on **direct** except as necessary to develop testimony (preliminary or undisputed matters, refreshing a forgetful witness, children, witnesses with communication difficulties); it is **ordinarily allowed on cross**; and it is allowed on direct when a party calls a **hostile witness, an adverse party, or a witness identified with an adverse party** (FRE 611(c)).
- **Other objectionable forms (R-EVD-020):**
  - **Compound** (two questions in one);
  - **Argumentative** (argues with the witness rather than seeking facts);
  - **Assumes facts not in evidence**;
  - **Calls for speculation / conjecture**;
  - **Calls for a narrative** (open-ended "tell us everything");
  - **Asked and answered** / cumulative;
  - **Misleading or misquoting** the witness;
  - **Harassing or embarrassing** (611(a)(3));
  - **Nonresponsive answer** (at common law only the examining party may move to strike; under CEC § 766 any party may).
- **CA note:** § 764 (definition of leading question); § 767 (leading questions not allowed on direct or redirect except in special circumstances where the interests of justice require; allowed on cross and recross; court may allow leading a child under 10 in certain abuse cases [VERIFY V-409]); § 776 (adverse party). Same substance.
- **How tested.** Essays (Jul 2026 Q5 asks which objections should have been made): a question in the facts that begins "Isn't it true that…" on direct is a leading-question objection.

#### I.C.4 Exclusion of witnesses
- **Rule (R-EVD-021).** At a party's request the court **must** order witnesses excluded so they cannot hear other witnesses' testimony, and may do so on its own (FRE 615(a)). It may **not** exclude (1) a party who is a natural person; (2) one officer or employee designated as an entity party's representative; (3) a person whose presence a party shows to be essential to presenting its claim or defense (e.g., an expert who must hear the testimony); or (4) a person authorized by statute to be present (e.g., crime victims under 18 U.S.C. § 3771).
- **2023 amendment (Apply).** The court may also, by order, **prohibit disclosure** of trial testimony to excluded witnesses and **prohibit excluded witnesses from accessing** trial testimony (FRE 615(b)) [S-104]. The amendment also clarifies that the entity-party exemption covers one designated representative [VERIFY V-415].
- **Remedies for violation:** contempt, instruction, or (rarely) exclusion of the witness's testimony.
- **CA note:** § 777 (exclusion on court's own motion or a party's; a party who is a natural person, or an officer/employee designated by an entity party, may not be excluded). California also gives crime victims a right to be present (Cal. Const. art. I, § 28(b)) [S-145]. Same substance.

### I.D Impeachment, contradiction, and rehabilitation
- **Who may impeach (R-EVD-022).** Any party, **including the party that called the witness**, may attack the witness's credibility (FRE 607; CEC § 785). The common-law voucher rule is gone. A party may not, however, call a witness **solely** to put an otherwise inadmissible prior inconsistent statement before the jury under the guise of impeachment (subterfuge doctrine; largely moot for the FRE once 801(d)(1)(A) makes such statements substantive [S-101]).
- **Five main methods ("BICCS" mnemonic):** **B**ias/interest; **I**nconsistent statements; **C**onvictions; **C**haracter for untruthfulness (reputation/opinion and specific instances); **S**ensory or mental deficiencies. Plus **contradiction**.
- **Intrinsic vs. extrinsic.** *Intrinsic* impeachment is done through the witness's own answers on cross. *Extrinsic* evidence (other witnesses, documents) is allowed for **bias, prior convictions, capacity, and non-collateral inconsistent statements or contradictions**, but **not** for specific instances of untruthful conduct (608(b)) or for **collateral** matters.
- **Religious beliefs** may not be used to attack or support credibility (FRE 610; CEC § 789).
- **CA note:** § 780 lists the credibility factors (demeanor, capacity to perceive/recollect/communicate, character for honesty or veracity, bias, interest or other motive, prior consistent or inconsistent statements, existence of the fact testified to, attitude toward the action, admission of untruthfulness). § 786: character evidence **other than** honesty or veracity is inadmissible to attack or support credibility.

#### I.D.1 Inconsistent statements and conduct
- **Rule (R-EVD-023).** A witness may be impeached with a prior statement (oral or written) or conduct that is **inconsistent** with the trial testimony. The statement need not be shown or disclosed to the witness when questioning, but must be shown or disclosed to opposing counsel **on request** (FRE 613(a); the *Queen's Case* rule is abolished).
- **Extrinsic evidence (FRE 613(b), as amended Dec. 1, 2024 — Apply).** Unless the court orders otherwise, extrinsic evidence of a witness's prior inconsistent statement **may not be admitted until after** the witness is given an opportunity to explain or deny it and the adverse party an opportunity to examine the witness about it [S-105]. Before 2024, the opportunity could come at any time (even after the extrinsic evidence). The foundation requirement does **not** apply to an **opposing party's statement** under 801(d)(2). Extrinsic evidence is also barred if the inconsistency is on a **collateral** matter.
- **What counts as inconsistent:** a direct contradiction; an omission of a fact that would naturally have been mentioned; a claimed lack of memory that the judge finds **feigned**. **Inconsistent conduct** (e.g., a plaintiff who claims severe injury is filmed playing tennis) impeaches in the same way.
- **Impeachment vs. substantive use.**
  - **Before Dec. 1, 2026 (FRE):** a prior inconsistent statement was admissible **for its truth** only if made **under penalty of perjury at a trial, hearing, other proceeding, or deposition**; otherwise impeachment only, with a limiting instruction.
  - **From Dec. 1, 2026 (FRE 801(d)(1)(A) as amended):** **any** prior inconsistent statement of a witness who testifies and is subject to cross-examination about it is admissible **substantively**, subject to Rule 403 (and the 613(b) foundation) [S-101] [S-102]. See V.A.2 and R-EVD-085.
- **Silence as inconsistency.** A defendant's **pre-arrest** silence may be used to impeach his trial testimony (*Jenkins v. Anderson*, 447 U.S. 231 (1980)); **post-Miranda** silence may not (*Doyle v. Ohio*, 426 U.S. 610 (1976)). A voluntary statement obtained in violation of Miranda may impeach (*Harris v. New York*, 401 U.S. 222 (1971)); an involuntary one may not.
- **CA note:**
  - § 769: the statement need not be shown or disclosed to the witness while examining him.
  - § 770: extrinsic evidence of an inconsistent statement shall be **excluded unless** (a) the witness was so examined as to give him an opportunity to explain or deny it, **or** (b) the witness **has not been excused** from giving further testimony in the action. So in California the foundation may come **after** the extrinsic evidence, as long as the witness can be recalled — more flexible than amended FRE 613(b).
  - § 1235: a prior inconsistent statement that complies with § 770 is admissible **for its truth** (no oath requirement) — California's long-standing rule, upheld against confrontation challenge in *California v. Green*, 399 U.S. 149 (1970). A **deliberately evasive** claim of lack of memory is treated as an implied inconsistency (*People v. Green* (1971) 3 Cal.3d 981) [S-519].
  - **Essay line:** "Under § 1235 (and now FRE 801(d)(1)(A) as amended in 2026) the statement is admissible both to impeach and as substantive evidence."
- **How tested.** MBE: the pre-2026 answer ("admissible only to impeach because not under oath at a proceeding") will be wrong on statements made after the amendment takes effect if the question asks under current FRE; read the facts carefully. Essays: Feb 2025 retest Q1 (witness testimony under California law) and Feb 2020 Q4 (notes of a cooperating co-defendant) [VERIFY V-071].

#### I.D.2 Bias and interest
- **Rule (R-EVD-024).** A witness may be impeached by showing **bias, interest, or motive** to testify falsely — family or romantic relationship, employment, payment (including expert fees), a plea or immunity deal, a pending lawsuit, hostility, membership in an organization sworn to perjury (*United States v. Abel*, 469 U.S. 45 (1984)). Bias is **not a collateral matter**, so it may be shown on cross and by **extrinsic evidence**, subject to FRE 403 and the court's control (611(a)). Most courts require a **foundation** first (ask the witness about the facts showing bias) before extrinsic proof.
- **Confrontation.** In a criminal case, cutting off cross-examination on a prosecution witness's bias can violate the Confrontation Clause (*Davis v. Alaska*, 415 U.S. 308 (1974): juvenile probation status could be shown to establish bias despite a state confidentiality law).
- **Overlaps.** Evidence otherwise excluded by policy rules can come in to show bias: liability insurance (411), settlement (408(b)), and a cooperating witness's plea agreement.
- **CA note:** § 780(f) ("existence or nonexistence of a bias, interest, or other motive"). Same rule. Prop 8 does not narrow cross on bias; Truth-in-Evidence only widens admissibility (R-EVD-004).

#### I.D.3 Conviction of crime
- **FRE 609 (R-EVD-025).**
  - **(a)(1) Felonies** (crimes punishable by death or more than one year):
    - against a **witness other than a criminal defendant** — admitted **subject to Rule 403** (exclude only if probative value is *substantially outweighed* by unfair prejudice);
    - against a **criminal defendant** who testifies — admitted only if the **probative value outweighs its prejudicial effect to that defendant** (reverse-weighted, plain balancing; the government bears the burden). Factors: impeachment value of the crime, its recency, similarity to the charged crime (more similar = more prejudicial), importance of the defendant's testimony, centrality of credibility.
  - **(a)(2) Crimes of dishonesty or false statement** (perjury, fraud, embezzlement, false pretenses, forgery — where the court can readily determine that establishing the elements required proving a **dishonest act or false statement**): **must** be admitted for any witness, **felony or misdemeanor, no balancing**.
  - **(b) Ten-year limit:** if more than 10 years have passed since the **conviction or release from confinement, whichever is later**, the conviction is admissible only if its probative value, supported by specific facts and circumstances, **substantially outweighs** its prejudicial effect, and the proponent gives reasonable **written notice**.
  - **(c)** Pardon, annulment, or certificate of rehabilitation bars use if based on rehabilitation (and no later felony) or on a finding of innocence.
  - **(d) Juvenile adjudications** are admissible only in a **criminal** case, against a witness **other than the defendant**, if an adult's conviction would be admissible and admission is necessary to fairly determine guilt or innocence.
  - **(e)** A pending appeal does not bar use; the pendency is admissible.
- **What may be shown.** Name of the crime, date, and sentence — not the underlying details, unless the witness opens the door. Proof by the witness's admission on cross or by the public record (extrinsic evidence allowed; never collateral).
- **The 609 amendment proposal was withdrawn in 2026; current 609 applies** [S-106].
- **CA note (R-EVD-026):** see [`ca-distinctions.md`](ca-distinctions.md#impeachment-california-vs-federal-rules). In brief:
  - **Criminal cases:** Cal. Const. art. I, § 28(f)(4) allows prior **felony** convictions to be used "without limitation" for impeachment [S-145], but due process limits this to felonies involving **moral turpitude** (a "readiness to do evil"), judged by the least adjudicated elements, and the court keeps § 352 discretion (*People v. Castro* (1985) 38 Cal.3d 301). Under Truth-in-Evidence, **misdemeanor conduct** involving moral turpitude may also impeach, but the misdemeanor **conviction** record itself was hearsay when offered for impeachment (*People v. Wheeler* (1992) 4 Cal.4th 284) [S-502].
  - **Civil cases:** § 788 permits impeachment by a **felony** conviction; Prop 8 does not apply; § 352 applies; art. I, § 28(f) and the criminal-case rulings do not apply, so the moral-turpitude cases serve only as guidelines, but the court must weigh the conviction under § 352 on objection (*Robbins v. Wong* (1994) 27 Cal.App.4th 261, 273–274) [S-504] [S-1553]. Misdemeanors are not admissible under § 788.
- **How tested.** MBE: the 10-year clock; misdemeanor fraud is automatically admissible; a 3-year-old felony assault against a testifying defendant needs reverse balancing. Essays: Feb 2020 Q4 (defendant's prior **forgery** conviction under California law) and Feb 2025 retest Q1 (witness's prior convictions) [VERIFY V-071].

#### I.D.4 Specific instances of conduct
- **Rule (R-EVD-027).** Except for criminal convictions under 609, **extrinsic evidence** is not admissible to prove specific instances of a witness's conduct in order to attack or support the witness's character for truthfulness (FRE 608(b)). The court **may**, on cross-examination, allow **inquiry** into specific instances if they are **probative of truthfulness or untruthfulness** of (1) the witness or (2) another witness whose character the witness being cross-examined has testified about.
- **Good-faith basis** for the question is required. The examiner must **take the answer**: if the witness denies lying on a job application, the examiner cannot call the employer or introduce the application to prove it (it is extrinsic and collateral).
- **Probative of untruthfulness:** lying on applications or tax returns, false statements, fraud, bribery, using aliases. **Not** probative: violence, drug use, adultery (unless deception is involved).
- **608(b) does not bar** extrinsic evidence offered for another purpose — bias, contradiction of a non-collateral fact, or capacity.
- **CA note (R-EVD-028):** § 787 says evidence of specific instances of conduct relevant only as tending to prove a trait of character is **inadmissible** to attack or support credibility, **even on cross**. But in **criminal** cases, Truth-in-Evidence supersedes § 787: **non-felony conduct involving moral turpitude** is admissible to impeach, subject to § 352 (*People v. Wheeler* (1992) 4 Cal.4th 284) [S-502] — and, because Truth-in-Evidence makes relevant evidence admissible, it may be proved by **extrinsic** evidence subject to § 352 (the conviction record itself being hearsay unless an exception applies). **Civil cases:** § 787 applies — no specific-act impeachment at all.

#### I.D.5 Character for truthfulness
- **Rule (R-EVD-029).** A witness's credibility may be attacked or supported by testimony about the witness's **reputation** for having a character for truthfulness or untruthfulness, or by an **opinion** about that character (FRE 608(a)). Evidence of **truthful** character is admissible **only after** the witness's character for truthfulness has been attacked (by opinion/reputation, 608(b) inquiry, conviction, or a direct accusation of lying — but **not** merely by contradiction or a showing of bias, in most courts).
- **Foundation.** A reputation witness must know the witness's reputation in a relevant community (residential, work, school); an opinion witness must know the witness well enough to have formed an opinion.
- **Only truthfulness is in issue.** Evidence that a witness is violent or immoral is not admissible to impeach (FRE 404(a)(3), 608).
- **CA note:** § 786 (only honesty or veracity traits); § 790 (good character for honesty or veracity inadmissible to support credibility unless evidence of bad character for honesty has been admitted). In criminal cases Truth-in-Evidence may broaden what is relevant, but the § 790 timing rule is generally followed. Same core rule.

#### I.D.6 Ability to observe, remember, or relate accurately
- **Rule (R-EVD-030).** A witness may be impeached by showing a defect in **perception** (poor eyesight, darkness, distance, distraction), **memory** (passage of time, intoxication at the time of the event or at trial), or **ability to communicate**, including mental illness or drug use **at the relevant time** that affects reliability. Extrinsic evidence is allowed (capacity is not collateral), subject to 403.
- **Expert testimony** on eyewitness-identification problems is admissible in many courts; California permits it and requires it in some circumstances [VERIFY V-409].
- **CA note:** § 780(c) (capacity to perceive, recollect, or communicate). Same.

#### I.D.7 Impeachment of hearsay declarants
- **Rule (R-EVD-031).** When a hearsay statement — or a statement under 801(d)(2)(C), (D), or (E) — has been admitted, the **declarant's credibility** may be attacked, and then supported, by **any evidence that would be admissible if the declarant had testified** (FRE 806). A declarant's inconsistent statement or conduct is admissible **regardless of when it occurred** or whether the declarant had an opportunity to explain or deny it. The party against whom the statement was admitted may call the declarant and examine as if on cross.
- **Typical use:** the prosecution introduces a dying declaration; the defense shows the declarant had a felony fraud conviction and had earlier said someone else shot him.
- **CA note:** § 1202 (inconsistent statements or conduct of a hearsay declarant are not inadmissible for failure to give an opportunity to explain; other credibility evidence admissible as if the declarant had testified). § 1203 allows the adverse party to call and cross-examine the declarant. Same substance.

#### I.D.8 Rehabilitation of impeached witnesses
- **Rule (R-EVD-032).** After impeachment, the proponent may **rehabilitate** by: (1) redirect examination letting the witness **explain or deny** the impeaching fact; (2) **good character for truthfulness** (opinion or reputation) — only after an attack on character for truthfulness (608(a)); (3) a **prior consistent statement** when admissible under 801(d)(1)(B) (V.A.2).
- **Prior consistent statements** come in **substantively** (FRE 801(d)(1)(B)) if the declarant testifies and is subject to cross, and the statement is offered (i) to rebut an express or implied charge of **recent fabrication or improper influence or motive** — in which case it must have been made **before** the alleged motive arose (*Tome v. United States*, 513 U.S. 150 (1995)) — or (ii) to rehabilitate the declarant's credibility when attacked **on another ground** (e.g., faulty memory or a charge of inconsistency) (2014 amendment).
- Bolstering an **unimpeached** witness is improper.
- **CA note:** § 791 (prior consistent statement admissible only (a) after a prior inconsistent statement has been admitted and the consistent statement was made **before** the inconsistent one, or (b) after an express or implied charge of recent fabrication, bias, or improper motive, and the statement was made **before** the bias or motive arose); § 1236 (if § 791 is met, admissible for its truth); § 790 (character for truthfulness). California has no counterpart to the 2014 "other ground" clause — a narrower door.

#### I.D.9 Contradiction
- **Rule (R-EVD-033).** A witness may be impeached by showing that a fact the witness testified to is untrue. Contradiction **through cross-examination** is always allowed. **Extrinsic** contradiction is allowed only if the matter is **not collateral** — i.e., it is relevant to a substantive issue in the case, or independently admissible to impeach (bias, capacity, conviction). The collateral-matter rule is an application of FRE 403 and 611(a).
- **"Opening the door."** A witness who volunteers a broad assertion on direct ("I have never touched drugs") may be contradicted with evidence that would otherwise be excluded. A **testifying defendant** may even be contradicted with unlawfully seized evidence (*Walder v. United States*, 347 U.S. 62 (1954); *United States v. Havens*, 446 U.S. 620 (1980)); that exception does not extend to other defense witnesses (*James v. Illinois*, 493 U.S. 307 (1990)).
- **CA note:** § 780(i) (existence or nonexistence of any fact testified to). California has **no automatic collateral-matter rule**: contradiction on a collateral point is relevant to credibility and admissible unless the court excludes it under § 352, where its collateral character lowers its probative value (*People v. Lavergne* (1971) 4 Cal.3d 735, 742) [S-1557]. Truth-in-Evidence points the same way in criminal cases. On an essay, frame the issue as § 352 discretion (time and confusion against credibility value), not as a fixed bar.

### I.E Proceedings to which evidence rules apply
- **Rule (R-EVD-034).** The FRE apply in civil and criminal proceedings in the federal courts (FRE 101, 1101(a)–(b)). They **do not apply** (except privileges) to: the court's determination of preliminary facts under 104(a); **grand jury** proceedings; and miscellaneous proceedings such as **extradition**, **preliminary examinations** in criminal cases, **sentencing**, granting or revoking **probation or supervised release**, issuing **warrants** and summonses, and **bail** (FRE 1101(d)). **Privilege rules apply at every stage** (1101(c)).
- **CA note:** § 300 (the Evidence Code applies in every action before a California court, except as otherwise provided by statute). In **preliminary hearings**, Pen. Code § 872(b) (Prop 115, 1990) lets a qualified officer relate hearsay statements [VERIFY V-406]. Truth-in-Evidence applies in criminal proceedings, "including pretrial and post conviction motions and hearings" (Cal. Const. art. I, § 28(f)(2)) [S-145].
- **How tested.** MBE: hearsay at a sentencing hearing (allowed); privilege at a grand jury (applies).

## II Relevancy and reasons for excluding relevant evidence

About one-third of the Evidence MBE (~8 of 25 scored) [S-040]. Every essay item starts here.

### II.A Probative value

#### II.A.1 Relevancy
- **Rule (R-EVD-002).** Evidence is **relevant** if it has **any tendency** to make a fact **more or less probable** than it would be without the evidence, and the fact is **of consequence** in determining the action (FRE 401). Relevant evidence is admissible unless the Constitution, a statute, the FRE, or Supreme Court rules provide otherwise; irrelevant evidence is inadmissible (FRE 402).
- **Two parts:** **probative** (any tendency — a low bar; "a brick is not a wall") and **material** (fact of consequence under the substantive law). Facts relating to credibility are of consequence.
- **Direct vs. circumstantial evidence.** Direct evidence proves the fact if believed; circumstantial evidence requires an inference. Both are relevant.
- **Recurring relevance problems** (decided under 401–403, not special rules): **similar occurrences** (prior accidents at the same place admissible to show a dangerous condition or notice if substantially similar conditions); **prior claims** by the plaintiff (generally excluded unless fraud pattern); **industry custom** (relevant to the standard of care, not conclusive); **similar sales** to prove value; **flight, concealment, false alibi** as consciousness of guilt.
- **CA note:** § 210 defines relevant evidence as evidence, "including evidence relevant to the credibility of a witness or hearsay declarant, having any tendency in reason to prove or disprove any **disputed** fact that is of consequence". § 350 (only relevant evidence admissible); § 351 (all relevant evidence admissible except as provided by statute). The word **"disputed"** in § 210 means evidence on an undisputed fact can be excluded as irrelevant; the FRE handles the same problem under 403 (*Old Chief v. United States*, 519 U.S. 172 (1997)). **In criminal cases**, Truth-in-Evidence: "relevant evidence shall not be excluded" except as the provision allows (R-EVD-004).
- **Essay method (R-EVD-001).** For every item write: "**Logical relevance:** … tends to show X, which is of consequence because … **Legal relevance:** probative value is/is not substantially outweighed by … (FRE 403 / § 352)."

#### II.A.2 Exclusion for unfair prejudice, confusion, or waste of time
- **Rule (R-EVD-003).** The court **may exclude** relevant evidence if its probative value is **substantially outweighed** by a danger of **unfair prejudice, confusing the issues, misleading the jury, undue delay, wasting time, or needlessly presenting cumulative evidence** (FRE 403). The balance **favors admission**.
- **Unfair prejudice** means an undue tendency to suggest decision on an improper basis, commonly an emotional one — not simply that evidence hurts the opponent. Examples: gruesome photographs beyond what is needed; evidence inviting propensity reasoning; inflammatory gang evidence.
- **Considerations:** the availability of other evidence and stipulations (*Old Chief*: when the only purpose is to prove felon status, refusing a defendant's offered stipulation and admitting the name and nature of the prior offense was an abuse of discretion; the prosecution may otherwise prove its case its own way); the effectiveness of a limiting instruction; how contested the point is.
- **403 is not a ground to exclude** a conviction under 609(a)(2) (mandatory) and is modified for 609(a)(1)(B) and (b), 412(b)(2), 703 and 107.
- **CA note:** § 352: the court in its discretion may exclude evidence if its probative value is substantially outweighed by the probability that its admission will **(a) necessitate undue consumption of time** or **(b) create substantial danger of undue prejudice, of confusing the issues, or of misleading the jury**. Functionally the same as 403 (California does not list "cumulative" separately — it is "undue consumption of time"). § 352 survives Truth-in-Evidence by express exception, so it is the main tool for excluding relevant evidence in **California criminal cases** [S-145]. § 352.2 adds a special analysis for **creative expression** in criminal cases (R-EVD-047) [S-116]. See [`ca-distinctions.md`](ca-distinctions.md#discretionary-exclusion-california-balancing-vs-fre-403).
- **How tested.** MBE: photographs of a victim's wounds where cause of death is contested (admissible); evidence of a defendant's wealth in a negligence case (prejudicial). Essays: a 352/403 sentence on **every** item; Feb 2020 Q4 [VERIFY V-071].

### II.B Authentication and identification
- **Rule (R-EVD-048).** To authenticate an item, the proponent must produce evidence **sufficient to support a finding** that the item is **what the proponent claims it is** (FRE 901(a)). This is a 104(b) standard — prima facie; the jury decides genuineness.
- **Methods (R-EVD-049) — FRE 901(b) illustrations (non-exclusive):**
  1. **Testimony of a witness with knowledge** that the item is what it is claimed to be (signed it, saw it signed, recognizes it, "fair and accurate" depiction for a photograph).
  2. **Nonexpert opinion on handwriting** based on familiarity **not acquired for the litigation**.
  3. **Comparison** by an expert witness or the **trier of fact** with an authenticated specimen.
  4. **Distinctive characteristics** — appearance, contents, substance, internal patterns, taken with all the circumstances (e.g., an email with the sender's nickname and private facts; text messages from a phone in the defendant's possession).
  5. **Voice identification** by anyone who has heard the voice **at any time** under circumstances connecting it with the alleged speaker (including familiarity acquired for litigation — unlike handwriting).
  6. **Telephone conversations**: a call to the number assigned to a person, plus circumstances (including self-identification) showing the person answering was the one called; or to a business, with a conversation related to business reasonably transacted by phone. (Self-identification alone by an **incoming** caller is not enough.)
  7. **Public records** — from the office where items of that kind are kept.
  8. **Ancient documents** — in a condition creating no suspicion, in a place where it would likely be if authentic, and **at least 20 years old** when offered.
  9. **Process or system** producing an accurate result (computer output, radar, DNA analysis).
  10. Methods provided by statute or rule.
- **Reply-letter doctrine:** a letter that responds to an earlier letter and refers to its contents is authenticated as from the addressee of the first.
- **Self-authentication (R-EVD-050) — FRE 902** (no extrinsic evidence needed, though the opponent may still dispute genuineness): domestic public documents **under seal** and signed; certified copies of public records; official publications; **newspapers and periodicals**; **trade inscriptions** (labels, tags, signs indicating origin or ownership); **acknowledged (notarized)** documents; commercial paper; certified domestic and foreign **business records** (902(11)–(12), with written notice); certified **electronic process records** (902(13)) and data copied from a device, verified by **hash value** (902(14)).
- **Chain of custody (R-EVD-052)** for fungible items (drugs, blood): the proponent must show the item is the same and unchanged; gaps go to weight unless they make tampering plausible.
- **Identification of persons and objects:** an eyewitness who testifies "that is the man" is authenticating by knowledge; the reliability of a pretrial identification is a due process question (Crim Pro).
- **CA note:**
  - § 1400 defines authentication as introducing evidence sufficient to sustain a finding that the writing is the writing the proponent claims it is, or establishing it by other means provided by law; § 1401 requires authentication of a writing (and of secondary evidence of its content) before it is received.
  - Methods §§ 1410–1421: witness to execution (§ 1413), admission (§ 1414), handwriting by a person familiar with it (§ 1416), comparison by trier of fact or expert (§§ 1417–1418), **reply** (§ 1420), **content** (§ 1421). The **ancient-document** presumption requires **30 years** (§ 643), versus 20 under FRE 901(b)(8) [VERIFY V-409].
  - Printed representations of **computer information** and of **images on video or digital media** are presumed accurate (§§ 1552, 1553) — presumptions affecting the burden of producing evidence; machine-generated photographs are **not hearsay** (*People v. Goldsmith* (2014) 59 Cal.4th 258) [S-509].
  - Official-record copies and seals are self-authenticating under §§ 1450–1454 and 1530–1532.
- **How tested.** MBE: lay handwriting familiarity acquired for trial (bad) vs. voice familiarity acquired for trial (good); incoming caller's self-identification (insufficient alone). Essays: Feb 2023 Q5 (photocopied ad bearing a logo — FRE), Feb 2024 Q4 (photograph bearing a witness's signature; ATM records — CEC), Jul 2018 Q2 (computer printout — CEC) [VERIFY V-071].

### II.C Character and related concepts

#### II.C.1 Admissibility of character
- **The propensity ban (R-EVD-035).** Evidence of a person's **character or character trait** is **not admissible to prove** that on a particular occasion the person **acted in accordance** with it (FRE 404(a)(1)). Reason: unfair prejudice and distraction, not irrelevance.
- **First question: what is the purpose?**
  1. **Character in issue** (character is itself an **element** of a claim or defense): defamation (truth of a statement about character), negligent entrustment or hiring (the employee's incompetence), child custody (parental fitness), entrapment (predisposition). Character may then be proved by **any** method, including specific instances (FRE 405(b)) (R-EVD-036).
  2. **Circumstantial use to prove conduct** — banned, with the criminal-case exceptions below.
  3. **Impeachment** — governed by 607–609 (I.D).
  4. **Non-character purposes** for other acts — 404(b) (II.C.4).
- **Criminal-case exceptions ("the mercy rule").**
  - **(R-EVD-037) Accused's own character:** a defendant may offer evidence of **his own pertinent trait** (peacefulness in an assault case; honesty in a fraud case); once he does, the **prosecution may rebut** (FRE 404(a)(2)(A)).
  - **(R-EVD-038) Victim's character:** subject to Rule 412, a defendant may offer evidence of the alleged **victim's pertinent trait** (e.g., violence, in a self-defense case); if admitted, the prosecution may (i) rebut it **and** (ii) offer evidence of the **defendant's same trait** (FRE 404(a)(2)(B)). In a **homicide** case, the prosecution may offer evidence of the victim's **peacefulness** to rebut **any** evidence (not only character evidence) that the victim was the **first aggressor** (FRE 404(a)(2)(C)).
  - The defendant's **knowledge** of the victim's violent reputation or acts is not character evidence: it is offered to show the defendant's **reasonable fear**, so specific acts known to the defendant come in on that theory.
- **Civil cases.** No mercy rule: character evidence to prove conduct is inadmissible in civil cases even where the claim alleges criminal conduct (e.g., civil assault), except FRE 415 (sexual assault/child molestation) (II.C.5).
- **CA note:** § 1101(a) (ban); § 1102 (in a criminal action, **opinion or reputation** evidence of the **defendant's** character is admissible if offered by the defendant, or by the prosecution to rebut); § 1103(a) (defendant may offer evidence of the **victim's** character — **opinion, reputation, or specific instances** — to prove the victim's conduct; prosecution may rebut); § 1103(b) (once the defendant offers evidence of the victim's **violence**, the prosecution may offer evidence of the **defendant's violent character** — opinion, reputation, or specific instances). California has **no** homicide first-aggressor rule matching FRE 404(a)(2)(C) — but the prosecution may rebut under § 1103(a)(2) once the defense offers victim-character evidence. § 1101 survives Truth-in-Evidence because the Legislature re-enacted it by a two-thirds vote in 1986 (*People v. Ewoldt* (1994) 7 Cal.4th 380) [S-505]; §§ 1103 and 782 are expressly preserved [S-145]. See [`ca-distinctions.md`](ca-distinctions.md#character-and-propensity-evidence-california-vs-federal-rules).

#### II.C.2 Methods of proving character
- **Rule (R-EVD-039).** When character evidence is admissible, it may be proved by **reputation** or **opinion** testimony (FRE 405(a)). **Specific instances** may be proved only (1) when character is **an essential element** of a charge, claim or defense (405(b)), or (2) on **cross-examination** of a character witness, as inquiry (405(a)).
- **Cross-examining a character witness (R-EVD-040).** The prosecution may ask a defense character witness **"have you heard"** (reputation) or **"did you know"** (opinion) about specific relevant acts of the defendant — including arrests — to test the witness's knowledge and standards, provided it has a **good-faith basis**; it must **take the answer** (no extrinsic proof); and the jury is instructed that the questions test the witness, not prove the acts (*Michelson v. United States*, 335 U.S. 469 (1948)).
- **CA note:** § 1100 (character may be proved by opinion, reputation, or specific instances, **except** as limited by §§ 1101–1109); § 1102 limits the **defendant's** character to opinion or reputation; § 1103(a)–(b) allows **specific instances** for the **victim's** character and for the defendant's violent character in rebuttal — a key difference from FRE 405(a). Cross-examination of character witnesses with "have you heard" questions is permitted in California as well.

#### II.C.3 Habit and routine practice
- **Rule (R-EVD-041).** Evidence of a person's **habit** or an organization's **routine practice** may be admitted to prove that on a particular occasion the person or organization acted in accordance with it; the court may admit it **regardless of corroboration or eyewitnesses** (FRE 406).
- **Habit vs. character.** Habit is a **specific, semi-automatic, regular response to a repeated specific situation** (always takes the stairs two at a time; always signals before turning at a particular corner); character is a **general disposition** (careful driver, honest person). Factors: specificity, regularity (number of instances), and the non-volitional nature. Behavior involving intentional, moral or emotional choices (drinking, violence) is rarely habit, though some courts accept consistent heavy drinking patterns.
- **Routine practice** of a business (mailing procedures, check-in procedures) proves that the practice was followed on the occasion.
- **Proof** is by opinion or by specific instances sufficient in number to show regularity.
- **CA note:** § 1105 (habit or custom admissible to prove conduct on a specified occasion in conformity with it); § 1104 (a trait for **care or skill** is inadmissible to prove the quality of conduct on a specified occasion). Same rule.

#### II.C.4 Other crimes, acts, transactions, and events
- **Rule (R-EVD-042).** Evidence of any other crime, wrong, or act is **not admissible to prove character** in order to show action in conformity (FRE 404(b)(1)), but **may be admissible for another purpose**, such as proving **motive, opportunity, intent, preparation, plan, knowledge, identity, absence of mistake, or lack of accident** (404(b)(2)) — "MIMIC" (motive, intent, absence of mistake, identity, common plan) is the usual mnemonic. The list is illustrative.
- **Four-step test** (*Huddleston v. United States*, 485 U.S. 681 (1988)):
  1. **Proper non-propensity purpose** that is genuinely in dispute;
  2. **Relevance** to that purpose (401) — the chain of inference must not run through character;
  3. **Sufficient proof** that the defendant committed the other act — enough for the jury to find it by a preponderance (104(b)); a prior **acquittal** does not bar use (lower standard; *Dowling v. United States*, 493 U.S. 342 (1990));
  4. **403 balancing**, plus a **limiting instruction** on request (105).
- **Identity** requires a **signature** — a modus operandi so distinctive it marks the crimes as the work of one person. **Intent / absence of mistake** need less similarity (the "doctrine of chances"). **Common plan** requires the acts to be parts of a single larger plan (or, in California, a common design evidenced by shared features).
- **Notice (criminal cases).** The prosecutor must give **reasonable written notice before trial** (or during trial if excused for good cause) of any such evidence it intends to offer, and **articulate the permitted purpose and the reasoning** supporting it (404(b)(3), as amended 2020).
- **Inextricably intertwined / res gestae** acts that are part of the charged crime are not "other" acts (circuits vary).
- **CA note:** § 1101(b) — same list, plus **"whether a defendant in a prosecution for an unlawful sexual act … did not reasonably and in good faith believe that the victim consented."** *People v. Ewoldt* (1994) 7 Cal.4th 380 sets a **sliding scale of similarity**: least for **intent**, more for **common design or plan**, most (distinctive) for **identity** [S-505]. § 352 balancing follows. No statutory notice rule like 404(b)(3). § 1101(c): nothing in § 1101 affects evidence offered to attack or support credibility.
- **How tested.** MBE: prior burglaries with the same unusual method (identity); prior fraud to show knowledge; acquitted conduct (admissible). Essays: frequent in Evidence + Criminal crossovers [VERIFY V-071].

#### II.C.5 Prior sexual misconduct of a defendant
- **Rule (R-EVD-043).** In a **criminal case** in which a defendant is accused of **sexual assault** (FRE 413) or **child molestation** (FRE 414), evidence that the defendant committed **any other** sexual assault or child molestation is admissible and **may be considered on any matter to which it is relevant** — including **propensity**. In a **civil case** based on a party's alleged sexual assault or child molestation, the same evidence is admissible (FRE 415). The prosecution/party must disclose the evidence at least **15 days** before trial (or later for good cause). Courts still apply **403**, weighing similarity, closeness in time, frequency, intervening events, and need.
- The other act need not have resulted in a charge or conviction.
- **CA note (R-EVD-043, R-EVD-044):**
  - § 1108: in a **criminal** action in which the defendant is accused of a **sexual offense**, evidence of the defendant's commission of another sexual offense is not made inadmissible by § 1101, **if not inadmissible under § 352**. Upheld against due process challenge because § 352 preserves fairness (*People v. Falsetta* (1999) 21 Cal.4th 903).
  - § 1109: in a criminal action in which the defendant is accused of an offense involving **domestic violence**, evidence of other domestic violence is admissible subject to § 352; acts **more than 10 years** before the charged offense are inadmissible unless the court finds admission is "in the interest of justice" [S-514]. "Domestic violence" has the Pen. Code § 13700 meaning (spouses, cohabitants, dating or engagement relationships, co-parents [VERIFY V-993]) and, for acts within five years of the charged offense, the broader Fam. Code § 6211 meaning, subject to a § 352 hearing (§ 1109(d)(3)) [S-1550]. The section also covers other acts of abuse of an elder or dependent person, and of child abuse, when the charged offense is of that kind; the 10-year limit (§ 1109(e)) applies to every category [S-1550]. **No FRE counterpart.**
  - California has **no civil counterpart to FRE 415** (§§ 1108–1109 apply only "in a criminal action").
  - Essay relevance: Jul 2018 Q2 (battery on a live-in companion) — § 1109 may be in play [VERIFY V-071].

### II.D Expert testimony
- **Overview (R-EVD-054).** Under FRE 702 as amended Dec. 1, 2023, a witness qualified as an expert by **knowledge, skill, experience, training, or education** may testify in the form of an opinion or otherwise **if the proponent demonstrates to the court that it is more likely than not that**: (a) the expert's specialized knowledge **will help** the trier of fact understand the evidence or determine a fact in issue; (b) the testimony is based on **sufficient facts or data**; (c) the testimony is the product of **reliable principles and methods**; and (d) the expert's opinion **reflects a reliable application** of the principles and methods to the facts of the case [S-104].
- **The 2023 amendment (Apply, high-yield).** It confirms that the **preponderance standard of FRE 104(a)** applies to **each** of the four requirements — sufficiency of basis and reliable application are questions of **admissibility for the judge**, not merely weight for the jury — and that an opinion must stay within what a reliable application of the methodology can support.
- **CA note:** §§ 720, 801–805; *Sargon Enterprises, Inc. v. University of Southern California* (2012) 55 Cal.4th 747 (trial court gatekeeping); *People v. Kelly* (1976) 17 Cal.3d 24 (new scientific techniques: general acceptance). See II.D.4 and [`ca-distinctions.md`](ca-distinctions.md#lay-and-expert-opinion-california-vs-federal-standards).

#### II.D.1 Qualifications of witnesses
- **Rule (R-EVD-055).** The court decides under 104(a) whether a witness is qualified by **knowledge, skill, experience, training, or education** — any one suffices; formal credentials are not required (a mechanic with 30 years' experience can testify about brake failure). Qualification is **topic-specific**: a general practitioner may not be qualified on a narrow surgical standard. The opponent may **voir dire** the expert on qualifications. Once qualified, gaps go to **weight**.
- **Court-appointed experts** (FRE 706) may be appointed on motion or the court's own initiative; their appointment may be disclosed to the jury in the court's discretion.
- **CA note:** § 720(a) — a person is qualified as an expert if he has **special knowledge, skill, experience, training, or education sufficient to qualify him as an expert on the subject** to which his testimony relates; against objection, the special knowledge must be shown before the witness testifies; § 720(b) — may be shown by any otherwise admissible evidence, including the witness's own testimony. § 721(a): an expert may be cross-examined to the same extent as any witness and also as to qualifications, subject matter, and the matter on which the opinion is based. § 730 (court-appointed experts). Same substance.

#### II.D.2 Bases of testimony
- **Rule (R-EVD-056).** An expert may base an opinion on facts or data in the case that the expert has been **made aware of or personally observed** — (1) personal observation, (2) facts presented at trial (including hypothetical questions), or (3) facts made known outside court. If **experts in the particular field would reasonably rely** on those kinds of facts or data in forming an opinion, they **need not be admissible** (FRE 703).
- **Disclosure of inadmissible bases.** The **proponent** may disclose otherwise inadmissible underlying facts to the jury **only if** their probative value in helping the jury evaluate the opinion **substantially outweighs** their prejudicial effect (reverse 403) (FRE 703).
- **Order of proof.** Unless the court orders otherwise, an expert may state an opinion and reasons **without first testifying to the underlying facts or data**, but may be required to disclose them on cross (FRE 705). Hypothetical questions are allowed but not required.
- **Conduit problem in criminal cases (R-EVD-057).** When an expert relates an absent analyst's statements in support of her opinion, and the statements support the opinion only if true, the statements are **offered for their truth**; if they are **testimonial**, the Confrontation Clause applies (*Smith v. Arizona* (2024) [VERIFY V-035]). See V.L.
- **CA note:** § 801(b) (opinion based on matter perceived by or personally known to the witness or made known to him, **whether or not admissible**, that is **of a type that reasonably may be relied upon** by an expert in forming an opinion on the subject); § 802 (expert may state the reasons and the matter on which the opinion is based, and the court may require it to be stated first); § 803 (court may exclude an opinion based on improper matter). **Key difference:** *People v. Sanchez* (2016) 63 Cal.4th 665 — an expert may relate **general background** knowledge, but may **not relate as true case-specific hearsay** (facts about the particular events and participants) unless it is **independently proven by competent evidence or covered by a hearsay exception**; if the case-specific hearsay is **testimonial**, the Confrontation Clause also bars it absent unavailability and a prior opportunity for cross. California thus rejected the "not for its truth" rationale years before *Smith v. Arizona*.

#### II.D.3 Ultimate issue rule
- **Rule (R-EVD-058).** An opinion is **not objectionable just because it embraces an ultimate issue** (FRE 704(a)). **Exception (704(b)):** in a **criminal case**, an expert witness must **not state an opinion about whether the defendant did or did not have a mental state or condition** that constitutes an element of the crime charged or of a defense; those matters are for the trier of fact alone. The expert may describe a diagnosis and its characteristics, and may testify about what most people in a group know or intend, because that is not an opinion about **the defendant's** own mental state (*Diaz v. United States* (2024) [VERIFY V-035]).
- **Still excluded** under 701/702/403: opinions that merely tell the jury what result to reach or state **legal conclusions** ("the defendant was negligent"; "the contract is unconscionable"), and opinions on a witness's **credibility**.
- **CA note:** § 805 — testimony in the form of an opinion that is otherwise admissible is not objectionable because it embraces the ultimate issue. California has no Evidence Code counterpart to 704(b), but **Pen. Code § 29** bars an expert in the guilt phase from testifying whether the defendant had or did not have the required mental states (e.g., purpose, intent, knowledge, malice aforethought) [S-1554]. California also excludes expert opinion on a witness's truthfulness and on legal conclusions.

#### II.D.4 Reliability and relevancy
- **Federal gatekeeping (R-EVD-059).** The trial judge is the **gatekeeper** who must ensure that expert testimony is both **reliable** and **relevant** ("fit") (*Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals, Inc.*, 509 U.S. 579 (1993)). Non-exclusive *Daubert* factors: (1) whether the theory or technique **can be and has been tested**; (2) **peer review and publication**; (3) known or potential **error rate**; (4) **standards** controlling the technique's operation; (5) **general acceptance** in the relevant scientific community. The focus is on methodology, but a court may exclude an opinion connected to the data only by the expert's *ipse dixit* where there is "too great an analytical gap" (*General Electric Co. v. Joiner*, 522 U.S. 136 (1997), which also set **abuse-of-discretion** review). Gatekeeping applies to **all** expert testimony — technical and experience-based, not only scientific (*Kumho Tire Co. v. Carmichael*, 526 U.S. 137 (1999)). *Frye v. United States*, 293 F. 1013 (D.C. Cir. 1923) ("general acceptance" as the sole test) was superseded in federal court by the FRE.
- **2023 amendment.** The proponent must show **each** 702 element by a **preponderance**; courts had erroneously treated basis and application as weight questions [S-104].
- **California gatekeeping (R-EVD-060).**
  - **Sargon** (2012) 55 Cal.4th 747: under §§ 801(b) and 802, the trial court acts as a gatekeeper to exclude expert opinion that is (1) based on matter of a type on which an expert may not reasonably rely, (2) based on reasons unsupported by the material on which the expert relies, or (3) **speculative**. The court does not choose between competing reliable opinions; it checks whether the reasoning is logical and supported.
  - **Kelly** (*People v. Kelly* (1976) 17 Cal.3d 24): evidence based on a **new scientific technique** is admissible only if (1) the technique is **generally accepted** as reliable in the relevant scientific community, (2) the witness is properly qualified, and (3) **correct scientific procedures** were used in the particular case. California kept Kelly after *Daubert* (*People v. Leahy* (1994) 8 Cal.4th 587) [S-510]. Kelly applies only to **new** scientific techniques that may carry a misleading aura of infallibility — not to ordinary expert opinion (e.g., medical opinion), which is governed by Sargon.
- **How tested.** MBE: whether the judge or jury decides reliability (judge, 104(a), preponderance); whether general acceptance is required in federal court (no — one factor). Essays: Feb 2023 Q5 (expert on a painting's age, FRE) [VERIFY V-071].

#### II.D.5 Proper subject matter for expert testimony
- **Rule (R-EVD-061).** Expert testimony is proper when **specialized knowledge will help** the trier of fact (FRE 702(a)) — i.e., the subject is beyond the common understanding of jurors, or the expert can add meaningfully even to a familiar subject. Experts may testify in narrative or opinion form, and may educate the jury on general principles without applying them (702 advisory note).
- **Typical proper subjects:** medical causation, standard of care in professional negligence, accident reconstruction, valuation, DNA and fingerprints, battered-person syndrome and child sexual-abuse accommodation (to explain behavior, not to prove the abuse occurred), eyewitness-memory science (court's discretion), drug-trade practices.
- **Improper:** matters within common knowledge; opinions on credibility of a witness; legal conclusions; testimony that merely repeats hearsay.
- **CA note:** § 801(a) — expert opinion is limited to one **related to a subject that is sufficiently beyond common experience** that the opinion would **assist the trier of fact**. This is often described as a slightly stricter helpfulness test than FRE 702(a). § 1107 makes expert testimony on **intimate partner battering and its effects** admissible in criminal actions (not to prove the occurrence of the charged act) [VERIFY V-409].

### II.E Real, demonstrative, and experimental evidence
- **Real evidence (R-EVD-052)** — the actual object involved (the gun, the contract, the drugs). Requires **authentication** by (1) **ready identification** of a unique or marked item by a witness with knowledge, or (2) **chain of custody** for fungible or alterable items. It must be relevant and pass 403; the condition must be **substantially unchanged** or the change explained.
- **Photographs, video and recordings (R-EVD-051).** Authenticated by a witness with knowledge that it **fairly and accurately depicts** the scene (pictorial-testimony theory) — the witness need not be the photographer; or, for surveillance or automated systems, by evidence that the **process or system produces an accurate result** (silent-witness theory; FRE 901(b)(9)). Gruesome photographs → 403. **Social media and text messages** → distinctive characteristics (901(b)(4)) plus circumstantial evidence linking the account to the author.
- **Demonstrative evidence and illustrative aids (R-EVD-053).**
  - **FRE 107 (new Dec. 1, 2024 — Apply).** The court may allow a party to present an **illustrative aid** (a chart, model, animation, drawing) to help the trier of fact understand evidence or argument **if its utility** in assisting comprehension is **not substantially outweighed** by the danger of unfair prejudice, confusing the issues, misleading the jury, undue delay, or wasting time. An illustrative aid **is not evidence** and must not go to the jury room during deliberations unless all parties consent or the court for good cause orders otherwise; the court must keep it in the record [S-105]. Summaries of voluminous evidence offered **as evidence** are governed instead by FRE 1006 (IV.B).
  - **Maps, models, diagrams and computer animations** must be fair and accurate representations; **computer simulations** that reach conclusions (reconstruction) are treated as expert evidence (702).
- **Experimental evidence.** Admissible if the experiment was conducted under **conditions substantially similar** to those of the event; differences go to weight unless they make the results misleading (403). Experiments in front of the jury are in the court's discretion.
- **Views and exhibitions.** The court may allow the jury to view a scene; a person may exhibit an injury (subject to 403).
- **CA note:** §§ 1552–1553 (presumed accuracy of printouts and digital-image printouts) and *Goldsmith* [S-509]; California has no codified equivalent of FRE 107 — demonstrative aids are controlled by § 352 and the court's power under § 765. Experiments: substantial similarity and § 352. Video recordings are "writings" under § 250, so the secondary evidence rule can apply.
- **How tested.** MBE: day-in-the-life video (403); photograph authenticated by a witness who did not take it (fine). Essays: Feb 2024 Q4 and Jul 2018 Q2 (photographs and printouts under the CEC) [VERIFY V-071].

## III Privileges and other policy exclusions

Categories III and IV together ≈ one-sixth of the Evidence MBE (~4 of 25 scored) [S-040]. On California essays, **privileges** are tested heavily in Evidence + PR crossovers (Jul 2017 Q2, Feb 2022 Q4) and in Feb 2020 Q4 [VERIFY V-071].

**Governing law (R-EVD-062).**
- **Federal court:** privileges are governed by **federal common law** "in the light of reason and experience", but in a **civil** case **state law** governs privilege on a claim or defense for which **state law supplies the rule of decision** (diversity; supplemental state claims) (FRE 501). The Supreme Court's proposed specific privilege rules were never enacted, so federal privileges come from cases.
- **California:** privileges exist **only by statute** (§ 911); courts may not create new ones. They apply in all proceedings (§ 910), and survive Truth-in-Evidence in criminal cases [S-145].
- **Common machinery:** a privilege belongs to a **holder**, who may claim or **waive** it; waiver by voluntary disclosure of a **significant part** of the communication or consent to disclosure (§ 912; FRE 502 for attorney-client/work product in federal proceedings) (R-EVD-067). In California, neither the judge nor counsel may **comment** on the exercise of a privilege, and no presumption arises from it (§ 913). The court may not require disclosure of information claimed to be privileged in order to rule on the claim (§ 915; *Costco Wholesale Corp. v. Superior Court* (2009) 47 Cal.4th 725) [S-517]; federal courts may review in camera, including for crime-fraud on a threshold showing (*United States v. Zolin*, 491 U.S. 554 (1989)).

### III.A Spousal immunity and marital communications
- **Two separate privileges** — always analyze both.
- **(1) Spousal testimonial privilege / adverse spousal testimony (R-EVD-068).**
  - **Federal:** in a **criminal** case, the **witness-spouse** holds the privilege and may refuse to testify **against** the defendant-spouse, but may choose to testify (*Trammel v. United States*, 445 U.S. 40 (1980)). Requires a **valid marriage at the time of trial**; covers testimony about **anything**, including events before the marriage. No federal civil testimonial privilege. Exceptions: crimes against the spouse or the spouse's (or either spouse's) children; many courts add sham marriages and joint participation in crime.
  - **California:** a married person has a privilege **not to testify against** the spouse in **any proceeding** (§ 970) and a privilege **not to be called as a witness** by a party adverse to the spouse (§ 971) — **civil and criminal**; the **witness-spouse** holds it. Exceptions in § 972(e): a criminal proceeding in which the defendant-spouse is charged with a crime against the person or property of the other spouse or of a child, parent, relative or cohabitant of either (whether committed before or during the marriage), with bigamy, or with a Pen. Code § 270 or § 270a failure-to-provide offense [S-1559]; § 972 also excepts proceedings between the spouses, commitment and competency proceedings, juvenile-court proceedings and others [VERIFY V-992]. Waiver (§ 973(a)): a spouse who testifies in a proceeding to which the other spouse is a party, or testifies against the spouse in any proceeding, has no privilege in that proceeding; § 973(b): no privilege in a civil proceeding brought or defended for the immediate benefit of the spouse [S-1559].
- **(2) Confidential marital communications privilege (R-EVD-069).**
  - **Federal (common law):** protects **confidential communications** made **during a valid marriage**; **both spouses** hold it (either can refuse and prevent the other); it **survives divorce or death**; applies in **civil and criminal** cases. Not covered: communications made before marriage or after divorce, statements in the presence of third parties, observations of non-communicative conduct, communications about crimes against the spouse or children, and (in many circuits) communications in furtherance of joint crime.
  - **California:** § 980 — a spouse, **whether or not a party**, has a privilege **during the marriage and afterwards** to refuse to disclose and to prevent another from disclosing a communication made **in confidence** between spouses **while they were spouses**. Exceptions: communications to enable or aid anyone to commit or plan a crime or fraud (§ 981); proceedings between spouses (§ 984); criminal proceedings for crimes against the other spouse or a child, and similar (§ 985); juvenile proceedings (§ 986); and communications offered by a **defendant-spouse** in a criminal case (§ 987) [VERIFY V-409].
- **How tested.** MBE: divorced spouse asked about a confidential statement made during the marriage (communications privilege survives; testimonial privilege does not); spouse willing to testify in federal criminal case (Trammel — witness decides).

### III.B Attorney-client and work product
- **Attorney-client privilege (R-EVD-063).** A client has a privilege to refuse to disclose, and to prevent others from disclosing, **(1) confidential communications (2) between client (or representative) and lawyer (or representative) (3) made for the purpose of obtaining or providing legal advice or services**. The **client** holds it; the lawyer must assert it on the client's behalf. It protects **communications**, not **underlying facts** (a client cannot hide facts by telling them to a lawyer) and not pre-existing documents merely handed to the lawyer. Identity of the client and fee arrangements are generally not privileged.
  - **Confidentiality:** the client must intend confidentiality; presence of an unnecessary third party destroys it, but agents needed for the communication (interpreters, paralegals, an accountant translating financial data) do not.
  - **Prospective clients** are covered (consultation for the purpose of retaining counsel).
  - **Duration:** survives the client's death in federal court (*Swidler & Berlin v. United States*, 524 U.S. 399 (1998)).
- **Entity clients (R-EVD-064).** Federal courts protect communications by employees **at any level**, made at the direction of superiors to secure legal advice for the corporation, on matters within the employee's duties, where the employee is aware the purpose is legal advice and the communication is confidential (*Upjohn Co. v. United States*, 449 U.S. 383 (1981); the "control group" test is rejected). The **corporation** holds the privilege (management may waive it).
  - **California:** §§ 951–954 apply to entity clients. For an employee's statement, *D.I. Chadbourne, Inc. v. Superior Court* (1964) 60 Cal.2d 723 asks whether the employee spoke as the **corporation's representative** for a dominant purpose of transmitting to counsel, or as an **independent witness**; the employer's **dominant purpose** in requiring the statement controls [S-518].
- **Exceptions (R-EVD-065).**
  - **Crime-fraud:** no privilege if the client sought or used the lawyer's services to **enable or aid anyone to commit or plan a crime or fraud** (future or ongoing, not past) (federal common law; § 956).
  - **Breach of duty / self-defense:** communications relevant to an issue of breach by lawyer or client (fee disputes, malpractice) (§ 958).
  - **Joint clients:** in later litigation **between** the joint clients, communications made in the common matter are not privileged (§ 962).
  - **Deceased client / claims through the same deceased client** (will contests): § 957 (no privilege between parties claiming through a deceased client); federal courts recognize a testamentary exception.
  - **California-only:** § 956.5 — no privilege if the lawyer **reasonably believes disclosure is necessary to prevent a criminal act likely to result in death or substantial bodily harm** (pairs with Bus. & Prof. Code § 6068(e)(2) in PR). A California client's privilege lasts while a **holder** exists; after the estate is distributed and the personal representative discharged, no holder remains [VERIFY V-414].
- **Work product (R-EVD-066).** Documents and tangible things **prepared in anticipation of litigation or for trial** by or for a party or its representative are discoverable only on a showing of **substantial need** and inability to obtain the substantial equivalent **without undue hardship**; **mental impressions, conclusions, opinions, or legal theories** of counsel ("opinion work product") receive near-absolute protection (*Hickman v. Taylor*, 329 U.S. 495 (1947); FRCP 26(b)(3)). It is a **qualified immunity from discovery**, not an evidentiary privilege, and is waived by disclosure to an adversary (not merely to third parties). *California:* CCP § 2018.030 — a writing reflecting an attorney's **impressions, conclusions, opinions, or legal research or theories** is **not discoverable under any circumstances**; other work product is discoverable only if denial would **unfairly prejudice** the party or result in **injustice**; the protection belongs to the **attorney** (*Coito v. Superior Court* (2012) 54 Cal.4th 480 on witness statements). Cross-reference: `03-subjects/civil-procedure/` ca-distinctions (work product row).
- **Federal waiver rules (FRE 502).** A disclosure in a federal proceeding waives undisclosed communications on the same subject only if the waiver was **intentional**, the disclosed and undisclosed material concern the **same subject matter**, and they **ought in fairness** be considered together (502(a)). An **inadvertent** disclosure is not a waiver if the holder took **reasonable steps to prevent** it and **promptly** took reasonable steps to rectify it (502(b)). A federal court order can make non-waiver binding in other proceedings (502(d)).
- **CA note:** § 952 (confidential communication, including legal opinion formed and advice given); § 954 (the privilege; holder is the client, a guardian or conservator, or the personal representative of a deceased client); § 955 (lawyer must claim); § 912 (waiver by holder's disclosure of a significant part or consent; disclosure reasonably necessary to accomplish the purpose of the consultation is not a waiver). PR crossover: privilege vs. the broader **duty of confidentiality** (Bus. & Prof. Code § 6068(e)(1); CRPC 1.6) — see `03-subjects/professional-responsibility/` (Phase 4 group 3).
- **How tested.** Essays: Jul 2017 Q2 (compel lawyer to testify and produce a memorandum — privilege and work product); Feb 2022 Q4 (lawyer's testimony about a client statement and about turning over bloody pants — physical evidence is not privileged; the lawyer's observations derived from the client's communication may be); Feb 2020 Q4 (privilege claim under California law) [VERIFY V-071].

### III.C Physician/psychotherapist-patient
- **Physician-patient (R-EVD-070).** **No federal privilege** in federal-question or criminal cases (state law may apply in diversity). **California:** § 994 — the **patient** holds a privilege for **confidential communications** between patient and physician made for **diagnosis or treatment**. Major exceptions: **patient-litigant** (patient tenders own condition) (§ 996); **criminal proceedings** — the physician-patient privilege **does not apply** (§ 998); services sought to commit a crime or tort or to escape detection (§ 997); and others (e.g., will contests, required reports).
- **Psychotherapist-patient (R-EVD-071).** **Federal:** recognized under FRE 501 for confidential communications to licensed **psychiatrists, psychologists and clinical social workers** in the course of diagnosis or treatment; **no balancing** of need (*Jaffee v. Redmond*, 518 U.S. 1 (1996)). Waived by putting mental condition in issue. **California:** § 1014 — the patient holds the privilege, **applies in criminal cases too**; exceptions include **patient-litigant** (§ 1016), **court-appointed** examinations (§ 1017), services to commit a crime or tort (§ 1018), and the **dangerous-patient** exception where the therapist has reasonable cause to believe the patient is dangerous and disclosure is necessary to prevent the danger (§ 1024).
- **CA note:** the presence of a physician-patient privilege at all, and its criminal-case exception, are California-only points to write on a CEC essay.

### III.D Other privileges
- **Rule (R-EVD-072).**
  - **Privilege against self-incrimination** (U.S. Const. amend. V; CEC § 940): a witness may refuse to give testimonial answers that could tend to incriminate; a criminal defendant may refuse to take the stand, and no comment on that refusal is allowed (*Griffin v. California*, 380 U.S. 609 (1965)). Corporations have no privilege; physical evidence is not testimonial. Immunity (use and derivative-use) displaces it.
  - **Clergy-penitent:** recognized in federal common law; in California both the **penitent** (§ 1033) and the **clergy member** (§ 1034) hold a privilege for a penitential communication made in confidence.
  - **Government privileges:** **identity of an informer** (federal common law, *Roviaro v. United States*, 353 U.S. 53 (1957): yields when necessary to a fair defense; CEC § 1041) and **official information** (CEC § 1040); state secrets (federal common law).
  - **Journalist:** no First Amendment privilege before a grand jury (*Branzburg v. Hayes*, 408 U.S. 665 (1972)); **California** protects newspersons from contempt for refusing to disclose sources or unpublished information (Cal. Const. art. I, § 2(b); CEC § 1070) — an immunity, not a true privilege.
  - **California statutory privileges with no general federal counterpart:** sexual-assault counselor–victim (§ 1035.8); domestic-violence counselor–victim (§ 1037.5); trade secrets (§ 1060, if allowance would not conceal fraud or work injustice); **mediation confidentiality** (§ 1119; statements in mediation are inadmissible and undiscoverable).
  - **No privilege** in either system for parent-child, accountant-client (federal), or general friendship.
- **How tested.** MBE: rarely beyond the Fifth Amendment and informer privilege. Essays: one line where facts present it.

### III.E Insurance coverage
- **Rule (R-EVD-073).** Evidence that a person was or was not **insured against liability** is **not admissible to prove** whether the person **acted negligently or otherwise wrongfully** (FRE 411). It may be admitted for another purpose — **bias or prejudice of a witness** (e.g., witness works for the insurer), **agency, ownership, or control** (when disputed). Inadvertent mentions are handled by instruction or mistrial.
- **CA note:** § 1155 — evidence that a person was, at the time a harm was suffered by another, insured wholly or partially against loss arising from liability for that harm is inadmissible to prove negligence or other wrongdoing. Same; other purposes allowed under general relevance principles.
- **How tested.** Essays: Feb 2021 Q1 (insurer's report in a slip-and-fall) [VERIFY V-071].

### III.F Remedial measures
- **Rule (R-EVD-074).** When measures are taken **after** an injury or harm that would have made the earlier injury or harm **less likely to occur**, evidence of the **subsequent measures** is **not admissible to prove negligence, culpable conduct, a defect in a product or its design, or a need for a warning or instruction** (FRE 407). Admissible for **another purpose**: **impeachment**, or — **if disputed** — **ownership, control, or the feasibility of precautionary measures**.
- **Policy:** encourage safety measures. Measures taken **before** the accident (pre-accident design changes) are not covered. Measures taken by a **non-party** (third-party repairs) are generally not excluded by 407 (no policy reason), though 403 may apply.
- **"Feasibility" must actually be disputed** — a defendant who says the measure was not possible or would have been too costly opens the door; one who concedes feasibility does not.
- **CA note:** § 1151 — subsequent remedial or precautionary measures are inadmissible to prove **negligence or culpable conduct** in connection with the event. **California does not apply § 1151 to strict products liability** (*Ault v. International Harvester Co.* (1974) 13 Cal.3d 113: excluding the evidence would not further the policy, because a mass producer will not forgo improvements). So in a **California** product-defect case, a post-accident design change **is admissible to prove defect**. FRE 407 (since 1997) expressly covers product defect. See [`ca-distinctions.md`](ca-distinctions.md#federal-rules-of-evidence-vs-california-evidence-code-compare-and-contrast).
- **How tested.** MBE: repair after the accident offered to show negligence (excluded) vs. to rebut "it couldn't be done safer" (feasibility — admitted). Essays: Feb 2021 Q1 (slip-and-fall: manager's later mat) [VERIFY V-071].

### III.G Compromise, payment of medical expenses, and plea negotiations
- **Compromise (R-EVD-075).** Evidence of (1) furnishing, promising or offering — or accepting, promising to accept, or offering to accept — valuable consideration in **compromising or attempting to compromise a disputed claim**, and (2) **conduct or statements made during compromise negotiations** about the claim is **not admissible** — on behalf of any party — **to prove or disprove the validity or amount of a disputed claim**, or **to impeach by a prior inconsistent statement or a contradiction** (FRE 408(a)).
  - **Requirement: a dispute** about validity or amount must exist when the offer is made (a demand letter and response suffices; an unconditional admission of a debt with a plea for time is not a compromise).
  - **Criminal-case exception:** conduct or statements made in compromise negotiations with a **public office** exercising regulatory, investigative, or enforcement authority are admissible in a later **criminal** case (408(a)(2)) — offers and acceptances themselves are not.
  - **Other purposes allowed (408(b)):** witness **bias or prejudice**, negating a contention of **undue delay**, proving an effort to **obstruct** a criminal investigation or prosecution.
- **Medical expenses (R-EVD-076).** Evidence of **furnishing, promising to pay, or offering to pay medical, hospital, or similar expenses** resulting from an injury is not admissible to prove liability for the injury (FRE 409). **Statements of fact accompanying** the offer are **admissible** ("I'll pay your hospital bills — I ran the red light": the payment offer is excluded; the admission of running the light comes in, typically as a party-opponent statement).
- **Pleas and plea discussions (R-EVD-077).** In a civil or criminal case, the following are **not admissible against the defendant who made the plea or participated in the discussions**: (1) a **guilty plea later withdrawn**; (2) a **nolo contendere** plea; (3) statements made during **Rule 11** plea proceedings on either of those pleas; (4) statements made during **plea discussions with an attorney for the prosecuting authority** that did not result in a guilty plea, or resulted in a later-withdrawn guilty plea (FRE 410(a)). Statements to **police officers** (not prosecutors) are not covered unless the officer has authority to negotiate. **Exceptions (410(b)):** completeness (if another statement from the same discussions has been introduced), and a **perjury or false-statement** prosecution if the statement was made under oath, on the record, with counsel present. The protection can be **waived** by agreement (*United States v. Mezzanatto*, 513 U.S. 196 (1995)). A **guilty plea not withdrawn** is admissible as a party-opponent statement and (if a felony conviction) under 803(22).
- **CA note:**
  - § 1152(a): evidence that a person has, **in compromise or from humanitarian motives**, furnished or offered or promised to furnish money or any other thing, act, or service to another who has sustained or claims loss or damage, **as well as any conduct or statements made in negotiation thereof**, is inadmissible to prove liability [S-511]. California thus folds **humanitarian** offers into the compromise rule and protects statements **made in negotiation**; whether a statement of fault accompanying a pure humanitarian offer (no negotiation) is excluded is less settled [VERIFY V-405]. No "dispute" requirement appears in the text (an offer covering prospective claims is protected) [S-511]. Exceptions in § 1152(b)–(c) (e.g., certain insurance bad-faith actions; partial satisfaction offered to prove validity).
  - § 1154: offers to **discount a claim** (by the claimant) are inadmissible to prove the claim's invalidity.
  - § 1160 (**benevolent gestures**): statements, writings, or gestures **expressing sympathy** relating to the pain, suffering or death of a person involved in an **accident**, made to that person or the family, are **inadmissible as evidence of liability in a civil action**, but an accompanying **statement of fault** is **not** excluded by § 1160. No FRE counterpart.
  - § 1153: a guilty plea later withdrawn, or an **offer to plead guilty** to the crime charged or another crime, made by the defendant in a criminal action, is inadmissible in any action or proceeding. Nolo contendere pleas: Pen. Code § 1016 — a nolo plea to a **misdemeanor** may not be used as an admission in a civil suit based on the same act; to a **felony**, it has the same legal effect as a guilty plea [VERIFY V-409].
- **How tested.** MBE: statements during settlement talks offered to impeach (barred since 2006 amendment); statement accompanying an offer to pay medical bills (admissible); statements to a police officer during an interview (not plea discussions). Essays: statements at an accident scene ("I'm so sorry — I wasn't looking") under § 1160 vs. FRE.

### III.H Past sexual conduct of a victim
- **Rule (R-EVD-045) — FRE 412 (rape shield).** In any civil or criminal proceeding involving alleged **sexual misconduct**, evidence offered to prove that a victim engaged in **other sexual behavior** or to prove a victim's **sexual predisposition** is **not admissible** (412(a)).
  - **Criminal exceptions (412(b)(1)):** (A) **specific instances** offered to prove that someone other than the defendant was the **source of semen, injury, or other physical evidence**; (B) specific instances of sexual behavior **with the defendant**, offered by the defendant to prove **consent** or by the prosecutor; (C) evidence whose exclusion would **violate the defendant's constitutional rights** (e.g., to show bias or motive to fabricate).
  - **Civil cases (412(b)(2)):** admissible if its **probative value substantially outweighs** the danger of harm to any victim **and** of unfair prejudice to any party (a reverse, heightened balancing test); **reputation** evidence only if the victim has placed it in controversy.
  - **Procedure (412(c)):** written motion at least **14 days** before trial describing the evidence and its purpose, served on all parties and notice to the victim; **in camera** hearing; records sealed.
- **CA note (R-EVD-046):**
  - § 1103(c)(1): in prosecutions for specified sex offenses, **opinion, reputation, or specific-instance** evidence of the complaining witness's **sexual conduct** is **inadmissible to prove consent**; exception for **sexual conduct with the defendant** (§ 1103(c)(3)). The manner of the victim's dress is also generally inadmissible on consent (§ 1103(c)(2)) [VERIFY V-409].
  - § 782: evidence of the complaining witness's sexual conduct offered **to attack credibility** is admissible only through a **written motion, offer of proof, sworn affidavit, and hearing outside the jury's presence**, and subject to § 352.
  - § 1106: in **civil** actions alleging sexual harassment, assault or battery, opinion, reputation and specific-instance evidence of the **plaintiff's sexual conduct** is inadmissible to prove consent or absence of injury, except conduct with the alleged perpetrator [VERIFY V-417].
  - **AB 2556 (2026)** extends §§ 782 and 1103 protections to offenses alleged in local detention facilities and state prisons (likely effective Jan. 1, 2027) [S-117] [VERIFY V-030].
  - §§ 782 and 1103 are expressly preserved from Truth-in-Evidence [S-145].
- **How tested.** MBE: defendant offers the victim's prior sex with him (admissible on consent); with others (excluded unless source-of-semen). Essays: rare; know the California § 782 procedure for credibility.

## IV Writings, recordings, and photographs

### IV.A Requirement of original
- **The best evidence rule (R-EVD-078).** An **original** writing, recording, or photograph is required **in order to prove its content**, unless the FRE or a federal statute provides otherwise (FRE 1002).
- **When it applies — only when content is being proved:**
  1. the writing is a **legally operative document** (contract, will, deed, judgment) whose terms are in issue; or
  2. the witness's knowledge of the fact **comes from** the writing (testimony "the ledger showed a $5,000 payment"; describing what a video showed).
- **When it does not apply:** the fact exists **independently** of the writing and the witness has personal knowledge (a payment can be proved by the payer's testimony though a receipt exists; a person's statement can be proved by a listener though it was also recorded); **collateral** matters; **summaries** under 1006; public records (1005).
- **Definitions (FRE 1001):** "writing" = letters, words, numbers or their equivalent set down in any form; "recording" = same, recorded in any manner; "photograph" = photographic image or equivalent stored in any form. **Original** = the writing or recording itself or any counterpart intended to have the same effect by the person who executed or issued it; for electronically stored information, **any printout or other output readable by sight**, if it accurately reflects the information; for a photograph, the negative or a print from it.
- **Duplicates (R-EVD-079).** A **duplicate** (a counterpart produced by a mechanical, photographic, chemical, electronic, or equivalent process that accurately reproduces the original — photocopy, scan, carbon) is **admissible to the same extent as the original** unless (a) a **genuine question is raised about the original's authenticity** or (b) the circumstances make it **unfair** to admit the duplicate (FRE 1003). A **handwritten copy** is not a duplicate.
- **Excuses — other evidence of content allowed (FRE 1004)** if: (a) all originals are **lost or destroyed**, and not by the proponent acting in **bad faith**; (b) the original **cannot be obtained by any available judicial process**; (c) the party against whom it is offered **had control** of the original, was **on notice** that it would be a subject of proof, and **fails to produce** it; or (d) the writing **is not closely related to a controlling issue** (collateral). The FRE recognize **no degrees** of secondary evidence once an excuse is shown (oral testimony is as good as a copy).
- **Other routes:** certified copies of **public records** (1005); **testimony, deposition, or written statement of the party** against whom offered (1007); functions of judge and jury (1008) (I.A.7).
- **California — the secondary evidence rule (R-EVD-080).** California **repealed its best evidence rule** and replaced it in 1998 with the **secondary evidence rule** [S-507]:
  - § 1520: the content of a writing may be proved by an otherwise admissible **original**.
  - § 1521(a): the content of a writing may be proved by **otherwise admissible secondary evidence** (copies, etc.); the court **shall exclude** secondary evidence if it determines (1) a **genuine dispute** exists concerning **material terms** of the writing **and justice requires** exclusion, or (2) admission would be **unfair**. Secondary evidence must still be **authenticated** (§ 1401) and survive the hearsay rule.
  - § 1522: in **criminal** actions, an additional ground — the court shall exclude secondary evidence of a writing in the **proponent's possession, custody or control** that the proponent has not made reasonably available for inspection, subject to exceptions [VERIFY V-409].
  - § 1523: **oral testimony** of the content of a writing is **not admissible** except where (b) the proponent does not have the original or a copy and the original was **lost or destroyed without the proponent's fraudulent intent**; (c) the proponent has neither the original nor a copy, and either neither was **reasonably procurable** by the court's process or other means, or the writing is **not closely related to the controlling issues** and it would be inexpedient to require its production; (d) the writing consists of **numerous accounts** that cannot be examined in court without great loss of time and the evidence sought is only the general result [S-506].
  - "Writing" includes photographs, recordings and electronic data (§ 250). Printouts of computer information and digital images are presumed accurate (§§ 1552–1553).
  - **Essay line:** "Under the CEC, the best evidence rule has been replaced by the secondary evidence rule; a photocopy is admissible unless there is a genuine dispute about material terms and justice requires exclusion or admission would be unfair (§ 1521), but oral testimony about the contents is barred unless § 1523 applies." See [`ca-distinctions.md`](ca-distinctions.md#secondary-evidence-rule-california-vs-best-evidence-rule-fre).
- **How tested.** MBE: witness testifies to a contract's terms without producing it (BER objection); testimony that the defendant confessed, where the confession was also recorded (no BER — independent knowledge). Essays: Feb 2012 Q3 (photocopy of notes, FRE), Jul 2018 Q2 (computer printout, CEC), Feb 2023 Q5 (photocopied advertisement, FRE) [VERIFY V-071].

### IV.B Summaries
- **Rule (R-EVD-081).** The court may admit **as evidence** a **summary, chart, or calculation** offered to prove the content of **voluminous** admissible evidence that **cannot be conveniently examined in court**, **whether or not** the underlying evidence has been introduced (FRE 1006(a), as amended Dec. 1, 2024 — Apply) [S-105]. The underlying originals or duplicates must be **admissible** (though not necessarily admitted) and must be made **available** for examination or copying, or both, by other parties at a reasonable time and place; the court may order them produced in court (1006(b)). A summary that merely illustrates admitted evidence or argument is an **illustrative aid** governed by FRE 107, not by 1006 (1006(c)).
- **CA note:** § 1523(d) (oral testimony of the general result of numerous accounts); § 1521 (secondary evidence generally). California has no exact counterpart to FRE 1006, but secondary-evidence and § 352 principles reach the same result: a fair summary of voluminous admissible records is admissible.

### IV.C Completeness rule
- **Rule (R-EVD-082).** If a party introduces **all or part of a statement**, an adverse party may require the introduction, **at that time**, of **any other part — or any other statement — that in fairness ought to be considered at the same time**. The adverse party may do so **over a hearsay objection** (FRE 106, as amended Dec. 1, 2023 — Apply) [S-104].
- **2023 changes:** (1) applies to **oral** as well as written or recorded statements; (2) the completing portion is admissible **over a hearsay objection** (it is offered to provide context, so it comes in for whatever purpose fairness requires). Only what is **necessary to correct a misleading impression** is admitted.
- **CA note:** § 356 — where part of an act, declaration, conversation, or writing is given in evidence by one party, the **whole on the same subject** may be inquired into by an adverse party; when a letter is read, the answer may be given; and any other act, declaration, conversation, or writing necessary to make it understood may be given. California's rule has long covered **oral** statements and is not limited to the time of introduction (the adverse party may use it on cross or in its own case). Now largely aligned with FRE 106.
- **How tested.** MBE: prosecutor introduces part of a defendant's recorded statement; the defense wants the exculpatory remainder immediately (106 — yes, if needed to avoid a misleading impression, and hearsay is no bar).

## V Hearsay and circumstances of its admissibility

About one-quarter of the Evidence MBE (~6 of 25 scored) [S-040] and the **single most-tested topic on California Evidence essays** (Phase 3 tags: what is hearsay 3×, party admissions 2×, business records 2×, hearsay and exceptions 2× [VERIFY V-071]).

**Essay sequence for every out-of-court statement (R-EVD-083):** (1) Is it a **statement**? (2) Made **out of court**? (3) Offered **for its truth**? If not, name the non-hearsay purpose. (4) If hearsay: an **exclusion** (FRE 801(d)) or an **exception** (803, 804, 807)? Under the CEC, everything is an **exception** (§§ 1220–1390). (5) **Multiple hearsay** — each layer. (6) **Confrontation** if criminal and the statement is testimonial. (7) 403/352 and a limiting instruction.

### V.A Definition of hearsay

#### V.A.1 What is hearsay
- **Rule (R-EVD-083).** Hearsay is a **statement** that the **declarant does not make while testifying at the current trial or hearing**, offered in evidence **to prove the truth of the matter asserted** in the statement (FRE 801(c)). Hearsay is not admissible unless a federal statute, the FRE, or other Supreme Court rules provide otherwise (FRE 802).
- **"Statement"** = a person's **oral assertion, written assertion, or nonverbal conduct intended as an assertion** (801(a)). So:
  - **Assertive conduct** (pointing to identify a suspect; nodding yes) is a statement.
  - **Non-assertive conduct** (a sea captain inspecting a ship and then sailing with his family aboard, offered to prove seaworthiness) is **not** a statement under the FRE, so not hearsay.
  - **Machines and animals** are not "persons" (radar readouts, drug-dog alerts, automatically generated timestamps, red-light camera photos — authenticity issues only).
  - **Questions and commands** are generally not assertions, unless they contain an implied assertion intended by the speaker.
- **Not offered for the truth (R-EVD-084) — the main non-hearsay uses:**
  1. **Verbal acts / legally operative words**: words of offer, acceptance, contract terms, defamation, bribery offer, gift words, threats when the threat is the crime.
  2. **Effect on the listener or reader**: notice, knowledge, warning, reasonableness of the listener's fear or conduct, motive ("the officer was told a man with a gun was in the car" — to explain why the officer approached).
  3. **Declarant's state of mind, circumstantially**: "I am Napoleon" offered to show insanity (not that he is Napoleon); a statement showing knowledge the declarant could only have gained in a particular way.
  4. **Impeachment**: prior inconsistent statements offered only to show the witness says different things.
  5. **Verbal markers / identification by content**, and statements offered to show they were **false** (a false alibi offered to show consciousness of guilt).
- **The "truth test."** Ask: **does the inference the proponent needs depend on the declarant being right?** If yes, it is hearsay. Explain the purpose expressly and consider whether 403 risk of misuse requires exclusion or a limiting instruction.
- **CA note:** § 1200 (hearsay = a statement made other than by a witness while testifying at the hearing, offered to prove the truth of the matter stated); § 225 (statement = oral or written verbal expression, or **nonverbal conduct** of a person **intended as a substitute** for oral or written verbal expression). Same definition. **Party admissions and prior statements of witnesses are hearsay under the CEC** but fall within **exceptions** (§§ 1220–1238); the FRE defines them as "not hearsay". Label correctly on a CEC essay: "Hearsay, but admissible under the § 1220 exception." Hearsay survives Truth-in-Evidence by express exception [S-145].
- **How tested.** MBE: classic traps — a statement offered for notice (not hearsay), implied assertions, mechanical outputs. Essays: every hearsay item starts with the definition (Feb 2012 Q3, Jul 2014 Q2, Feb 2021 Q1) [VERIFY V-071].

#### V.A.2 Prior statements by witness
- **Requirement for all three FRE 801(d)(1) exclusions:** the **declarant testifies** at the current trial or hearing and is **subject to cross-examination about the prior statement**. A witness who says he cannot remember the underlying event is still "subject to cross" (*United States v. Owens*, 484 U.S. 554 (1988)).
- **(A) Prior inconsistent statement (R-EVD-085).**
  - **Current rule (FRE 801(d)(1)(A) as amended effective Dec. 1, 2026):** a prior statement that is **inconsistent with the declarant's testimony** is not hearsay, and so is admissible **as substantive evidence**, whether or not it was made under oath; the court retains 403 discretion, and FRE 613(b) still requires an opportunity to explain or deny before extrinsic proof [S-101] [S-102].
  - **Pre-amendment rule (through Nov. 30, 2026):** substantive use only if the statement was **given under penalty of perjury at a trial, hearing, or other proceeding, or in a deposition** (e.g., grand jury testimony qualified; a sworn police affidavit did not, in most courts). Otherwise, impeachment only.

| | FRE 801(d)(1)(A) before Dec. 1, 2026 | FRE 801(d)(1)(A) from Dec. 1, 2026 [S-101] | CEC § 1235 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Oath / proceeding needed for substantive use? | **Yes** — under penalty of perjury at a trial, hearing, other proceeding, or deposition | **No** | **No** |
| Witness must testify and be subject to cross | Yes | Yes | Yes (and § 770 foundation) |
| Foundation | 613(b): opportunity to explain or deny at any time | 613(b) (2024): opportunity **before** extrinsic evidence, unless court orders otherwise | § 770: opportunity to explain or deny, **or** witness not yet excused |
| Unsworn statement to police | Impeachment only (limiting instruction) | Substantive and impeachment, subject to 403 | Substantive and impeachment |
| What to write | Pre-amendment fact patterns, or an MBE item written before the change | **The rule for July 2027** | California essay |

- **Exam guidance.** For July 2027, apply the amended rule; if an MBE answer choice turns on "not under oath", recognize it reflects the old rule. On a California essay, the FRE and CEC now **largely match**; note that the CEC foundation (§ 770) remains more flexible than amended 613(b).
- **(B) Prior consistent statement (R-EVD-086)** — not hearsay if offered (i) to rebut an express or implied charge that the declarant **recently fabricated** it or acted from a **recent improper influence or motive** — the statement must **pre-date** the motive (*Tome*); or (ii) to **rehabilitate** the declarant's credibility when attacked **on another ground** (e.g., inconsistency, faulty memory) (2014 amendment). CEC §§ 791, 1236 (narrower — no "other ground" clause).
- **(C) Prior identification (R-EVD-087)** — a statement that **identifies a person as someone the declarant perceived earlier** (lineup, photo array, on-scene identification) is not hearsay; it may be proved by the declarant or by the officer who witnessed it. CEC § 1238: admissible if (a) the statement would have been admissible if made at the hearing, (b) it was made when the crime or occurrence was **fresh in the witness's memory**, and (c) the witness testifies that he made the identification and that it **was a true reflection of his opinion at that time**.
- **CA note:** §§ 1235–1238 make all of these **exceptions** to the hearsay rule. California's prior-inconsistent-statement rule (§ 1235) is long-standing; it was upheld against confrontation challenge (*California v. Green*, 399 U.S. 149 (1970)); a feigned lack of memory counts as inconsistency (*People v. Green* (1971) 3 Cal.3d 981) [S-519]. See [`ca-distinctions.md`](ca-distinctions.md#impeachment-california-vs-federal-rules).

#### V.A.3 Statements attributable to party-opponent
- **Rule (R-EVD-088).** A statement **offered against an opposing party** is not hearsay if it was (A) made by the **party** in an individual or representative capacity; (B) one the party **manifested that it adopted** or believed to be true; (C) made by a person whom the party **authorized** to make a statement on the subject; (D) made by the party's **agent or employee** on a matter **within the scope of that relationship and while it existed**; or (E) made by the party's **coconspirator during and in furtherance of the conspiracy** (FRE 801(d)(2)).
- **Key points.**
  - No requirement that the statement was **against interest** when made, or based on **personal knowledge**, or in a form that would be admissible as testimony (opinions are fine).
  - **Only against** the party who made (or is charged with) it — a party cannot offer its own statement under 801(d)(2).
  - In a multi-party case, a party's statement is admissible only against that party (limiting instruction; *Bruton* if a criminal codefendant).
  - **Guilty pleas** (not withdrawn) are party admissions; **nolo** pleas are excluded by 410.
- **(B) Adoptive admissions (R-EVD-089).** Express adoption ("That's right"), or adoption by **silence** when (1) the party heard and understood the statement, (2) was able to respond, and (3) a reasonable person would have **denied** it under the circumstances. In a criminal case, silence in the face of police accusations **after Miranda warnings** cannot be used (*Doyle*); pre-arrest silence is fact-specific.
- **(C)–(D) Authorized and employee statements (R-EVD-090).** Under (D), the employee need not have authority to speak — it is enough that the statement concerns a matter within the scope of employment and was made during the employment (a truck driver's admission after an accident that he was speeding binds the employer). The statement itself is considered but **does not by itself establish** the authority (C) or the existence or scope of the relationship (D) (801(d)(2), final sentence).
- **(E) Coconspirator statements (R-EVD-091).** The proponent must show by a **preponderance** (104(a)) that (1) a conspiracy existed, (2) the declarant and the party were members, and (3) the statement was made **during** and **in furtherance** of it. The court **must consider the statement itself** (801(d)(2), last sentence, codifying *Bourjaily v. United States*, 483 U.S. 171 (1987), which held that it may), but the statement **cannot alone** establish the conspiracy or participation. Statements after arrest or the conspiracy's end (confessions, narratives to police) are not in furtherance. No conspiracy charge is required.
- **Successor liability (2024 amendment — Apply).** If a party's claim, defense, or potential liability is **directly derived** from a declarant or the declarant's principal, a statement that would be admissible against the declarant or principal is also admissible against the party (e.g., a predecessor-in-interest's statement against the successor; an assignor's statement against the assignee; a decedent's statement in a wrongful-death or estate claim) [S-105].
- **CA note:**
  - § 1220 (party's own statement, offered against the declarant in an action to which he is a party); § 1221 (**adoptive**: statement of which the party, with knowledge of its content, has by words or other conduct manifested adoption or belief in its truth); § 1222 (**authorized**: statement by a person **authorized by the party to make a statement on the subject**, offered after evidence of authority); § 1223 (**coconspirator**: made while participating in the conspiracy and in furtherance of its objective, before or during the party's participation, offered after **independent** evidence sufficient to sustain a finding of the conspiracy and participation, or subject to it). The California foundation is only a **prima facie** showing, by evidence independent of the statement (independent-evidence rule: *People v. Hardy* (1992) 2 Cal.4th 86, 139), not the judge's preponderance finding of FRE 104(a); in a criminal case the jury is told to consider the statement only if the conspiracy is proved by a preponderance with evidence other than the statement (CALCRIM No. 418) [S-1558].
  - **No general employee-admission rule.** California has **no counterpart to FRE 801(d)(2)(D)**; an employee's statement comes in against the employer only if **authorized** (§ 1222), or under § 1224 where the **employer's liability is based on the employee's** conduct — which *Labis v. Stopper* (1970) 11 Cal.App.3d 1003 applied to an employee's statement in a respondeat-superior case, distinguishing contrary dictum in *Markley v. Beagle* (1967) 66 Cal.2d 951, 960 [S-503] [S-523] [VERIFY V-401]. Otherwise look for another exception (spontaneous statement; declaration against interest if the employee is unavailable).
  - **Essay line:** "Under the FRE, the driver's statement is a vicarious admission (801(d)(2)(D)); under the CEC it is admissible against the employer only if authorized (§ 1222) or under § 1224 because the employer's liability derives from the driver's."
- **How tested.** MBE: statement of a fired employee about events during employment (admissible if made while employed); coconspirator's post-arrest confession (not in furtherance). Essays: Feb 2012 Q3, Jul 2014 Q2 (party admissions) [VERIFY V-071].

#### V.A.4 Multiple hearsay
- **Rule (R-EVD-092).** Hearsay within hearsay is admissible only if **each part** of the combined statements conforms with an exception or exclusion (FRE 805). A business record that records a bystander's statement: the record layer may fit 803(6), but the bystander (not under a business duty) needs his own exception (*Johnson v. Lutz*, 253 N.Y. 124 (1930), is the classic illustration).
- **Method:** identify each declarant; for each, ask "statement? out of court? for its truth?" and find an exception.
- **CA note:** § 1201 (a statement within the scope of an exception is not inadmissible on the ground that it includes a statement made by another declarant, if that included statement itself meets an exception). Same.
- **How tested.** Essays: police reports, 911 recordings, medical records with patient histories (Jul 2014 Q2) [VERIFY V-071].

### V.B Present sense impressions and excited utterances
- **Present sense impression (R-EVD-093)** — FRE 803(1): a statement **describing or explaining** an event or condition, made **while or immediately after** the declarant **perceived** it. Rationale: no time to fabricate. No excitement needed; the statement must be close in time (seconds to minutes) and about the event itself. Declarant availability is immaterial (true of all 803 exceptions).
- **Excited utterance (R-EVD-094)** — FRE 803(2): a statement **relating to a startling event or condition**, made **while the declarant was under the stress of excitement** that it caused. Time lapse matters only as evidence of continuing stress (factors: time, nature of event, declarant's condition, spontaneity, whether in response to questions). The startling event may be proved by the statement itself plus some independent evidence in most courts.
- **CA note:**
  - § 1240 (**spontaneous statement**): admissible if it (a) **purports to narrate, describe, or explain an act, condition, or event perceived by the declarant**, and (b) was **made spontaneously while the declarant was under the stress of excitement** caused by such perception. Same as 803(2), with the CEC wording "narrate, describe, or explain".
  - California has **no general present sense impression exception**. § 1241 (**contemporaneous statement**) is narrower: a statement offered to **explain, qualify, or make understandable conduct of the declarant**, made **while the declarant was engaged in that conduct**.
  - **Essay line:** "A calm description of a passing car is admissible under FRE 803(1) but not under the CEC unless it explains the declarant's own conduct (§ 1241) or was made under stress (§ 1240)."
- **Confrontation overlay.** 911 calls and on-scene statements to police are usually **nontestimonial** when made to meet an **ongoing emergency** (*Davis v. Washington*, 547 U.S. 813 (2006); *Michigan v. Bryant*, 562 U.S. 344 (2011)), but statements narrating past events once the emergency has ended are testimonial (*Hammon v. Indiana*, decided with *Davis*). See V.L.
- **How tested.** MBE: statement made 15 minutes later while still shaking (excited utterance); calm statement "that car is going too fast" (PSI). Essays: Feb 2012 Q3 (passenger's statement about a reckless SUV, FRE), Jul 2018 Q2 (911 recording, CEC) [VERIFY V-071].

### V.C Statements of mental, emotional, or physical condition
- **Rule (R-EVD-095)** — FRE 803(3): a statement of the declarant's **then-existing state of mind** (such as motive, intent, or plan) or **emotional, sensory, or physical condition** (such as mental feeling, pain, or bodily health), **but not** a statement of **memory or belief to prove the fact remembered or believed**, **unless** it relates to the validity or terms of the declarant's **will**.
- **Uses:**
  1. **State of mind directly in issue** (intent to make a gift; fear; knowledge; domicile intent; a victim's fear when the defense claims she consented).
  2. **Intent to prove later conduct of the declarant** — *Mutual Life Insurance Co. v. Hillmon*, 145 U.S. 285 (1892) (letters stating an intent to travel with Hillmon admitted to show the declarant probably went). Statements of intent offered to prove the **conduct of another** ("I'm going to meet Angelo tonight" to prove Angelo met her) are admissible in many courts only with corroboration; the advisory committee note expresses doubt.
  3. **Then-existing physical condition** ("my back is killing me") — to anyone, not just a doctor.
- **Backward-looking statements are out:** "I'm scared because my husband beat me yesterday" is admissible for the fear but not to prove the beating (*Shepard v. United States*, 290 U.S. 96 (1933): "Dr. Shepard has poisoned me" was a statement of memory, not state of mind).
- **CA note (R-EVD-096):**
  - § 1250: same then-existing mental or physical state exception (including intent, plan, motive, design, mental feeling, pain, or bodily health), offered to prove the state at that time or to prove or explain the declarant's acts or conduct; § 1250(b) excludes **memory or belief to prove the fact remembered or believed**.
  - § 1251: a statement of the declarant's **prior** (past) mental or physical state is admissible if the declarant is **unavailable** and the prior state itself is **in issue** and the evidence is not offered to prove any fact other than that state — **no FRE counterpart**.
  - § 1252: statements under §§ 1250–1251 are **inadmissible if made under circumstances indicating lack of trustworthiness** — an express trustworthiness filter the FRE lack.
  - § 1260: statements of an unavailable declarant about his will (making, revoking, identification) are admissible [VERIFY V-409].
- **How tested.** MBE: Hillmon intent; Shepard memory exclusion. Essays: a homicide victim's statement of fear of the defendant (relevance to a contested issue only — e.g., where the defendant claims suicide, accident, or self-defense).

### V.D Statements for purposes of medical diagnosis and treatment
- **Rule (R-EVD-097)** — FRE 803(4): a statement **made for — and reasonably pertinent to — medical diagnosis or treatment** that describes **medical history, past or present symptoms or sensations, their inception, or their general cause**. 
  - Covers **past** symptoms (unlike 803(3)) and statements to **anyone** for the purpose (nurses, EMTs, family members relaying for a child, a doctor hired only to testify — diagnosis is enough).
  - **Fault and identity are generally not pertinent** ("I was hit by a car" — yes; "the car ran a red light" — no), but many courts admit a child-abuse victim's identification of the abuser as pertinent to treatment (psychological care, removal from the home).
- **CA note:** **California has no general medical-diagnosis exception.** § 1253 applies **only** to statements made for diagnosis or treatment by a **victim who is a minor at the time of the proceedings**, made **when the victim was under 12**, describing an act or attempted act of **child abuse or neglect**, and subject to § 1252 (trustworthiness) [S-500]. Otherwise, a patient's statement of **present** pain is admissible under § 1250; **past** symptoms only under § 1251 (if unavailable) or another exception (party admission, spontaneous statement). **Essay line:** "Under the CEC there is no general 803(4) analogue; § 1253 is limited to child-abuse victims under 12."
- **How tested.** MBE: statement to a doctor retained for litigation (admissible); statement assigning fault (excluded). Essays: patient history in medical records (multiple hearsay).

### V.E Past recollection recorded
- **Rule (R-EVD-098)** — FRE 803(5): a **record** that (A) is on a matter the witness **once knew but now cannot recall well enough to testify fully and accurately**; (B) was **made or adopted by the witness when the matter was fresh** in the witness's memory; and (C) **accurately reflects** the witness's knowledge. If admitted, the record **may be read into evidence** but **may be received as an exhibit only if offered by an adverse party**.
- **Foundation sequence:** try to refresh first (612) — if refreshing fails, lay the 803(5) foundation through the witness (or a second person who recorded what the witness said, in a **multi-party** record).
- **Compared with refreshing:** refreshing = the witness testifies from memory, the document is not evidence; recorded recollection = the document's content is the evidence.
- **CA note:** § 1237 — a previously recorded statement is admissible if it would have been admissible if made while testifying, concerns a matter as to which the witness has **insufficient present recollection to testify fully and accurately**, and the writing was (1) **made at a time when the fact recorded actually occurred or was fresh** in memory, (2) made by the witness, or under the witness's direction, or by another person for the purpose of recording the witness's statement at the time it was made, (3) offered after the witness testifies that the statement he made was a **true statement** of the fact, and (4) offered after the writing is **authenticated as an accurate record** of the statement. The writing may be **read into evidence**, but the writing itself may **not be received unless offered by an adverse party** (§ 1237(b)) [S-520]. Same in substance; California spells out the "witness testifies the statement was true" step.
- **How tested.** MBE: whether the proponent can introduce the memo as an exhibit (no). Essays: Feb 2012 Q3 (photocopy of notes — combine with best evidence) [VERIFY V-071].

### V.F Business records
- **Rule (R-EVD-099)** — FRE 803(6): a **record of an act, event, condition, opinion, or diagnosis** if (A) it was made **at or near the time** by — or from information transmitted by — **someone with knowledge**; (B) it was kept in the course of a **regularly conducted activity** of a business, organization, occupation, or calling, **whether or not for profit**; (C) **making the record was a regular practice** of that activity; (D) all these conditions are shown by the **testimony of the custodian or another qualified witness**, or by a **certification** under 902(11)/(12) or statute; and (E) the **opponent does not show** that the source of information or the method or circumstances of preparation **indicate a lack of trustworthiness** (burden on opponent since 2014).
- **Limits:**
  - **Every link** in the chain must be acting under a **business duty**; statements by outsiders (customers, bystanders) recorded in the record need their own exception (805).
  - Records **prepared in anticipation of litigation** may be excluded as untrustworthy (*Palmer v. Hoffman*, 318 U.S. 109 (1943): railroad accident report).
  - **Opinions and diagnoses** are covered under the FRE (still subject to 702 if expert).
  - **Absence of a record** (803(7)) is admissible to prove the event did not occur, if a record would regularly have been kept.
- **Confrontation.** Ordinary business records are **nontestimonial**; forensic lab certificates prepared for prosecution are testimonial even if routinely made (*Melendez-Diaz v. Massachusetts*, 557 U.S. 305 (2009)).
- **CA note:** § 1271 — a writing made as a record of an **act, condition, or event** is admissible if (a) made in the **regular course of a business**; (b) made **at or near the time** of the act, condition or event; (c) the **custodian or other qualified witness testifies** to its identity and the mode of its preparation; and (d) the **sources of information and method and time of preparation** were such as to **indicate its trustworthiness**. Differences:
  - § 1271 omits "opinion or diagnosis": California courts have excluded **opinions and diagnoses** in business records as not "acts, conditions, or events" (*People v. Reyes* (1974) 12 Cal.3d 486 — psychiatric report) while admitting recorded **observations** [S-515].
  - The **proponent** must show trustworthiness under § 1271(d); under FRE 803(6)(E) the **opponent** must show untrustworthiness.
  - § 1270 defines "business" broadly (including governmental activity and non-profit operations); § 1272 covers absence of entries. Affidavits of custodians for subpoenaed business records are allowed by statute (Evid. Code §§ 1560–1562) [VERIFY V-409].
- **How tested.** MBE: hospital record containing the patient's statement about how the accident happened (multiple hearsay); a store's incident report written by the manager for the insurer (anticipation of litigation). Essays: Jul 2014 Q2 (FRE), Feb 2024 Q4 (ATM records under the CEC, custodian Chet) [VERIFY V-071].

### V.G Public records and reports
- **Rule (R-EVD-100)** — FRE 803(8): a **record or statement of a public office** if (A) it sets out (i) the **office's activities**; (ii) a **matter observed while under a legal duty to report** — but **not including, in a criminal case, a matter observed by law-enforcement personnel**; or (iii) **in a civil case or against the government in a criminal case**, **factual findings from a legally authorized investigation**; and (B) the **opponent does not show** that the source of information or other circumstances indicate a lack of trustworthiness.
- **Factual findings include opinions and conclusions** that are based on a factual investigation (*Beech Aircraft Corp. v. Rainey*, 488 U.S. 153 (1988)).
- **Police reports against a criminal defendant** cannot come in under 803(8) (and courts block evasion through 803(6)); routine, non-adversarial records (e.g., a log of serial numbers) may be admitted. Third-party statements within a police report need their own exception.
- **Related exceptions:** public records of vital statistics (803(9)); absence of a public record (803(10)); judgment of a previous felony conviction (803(22)).
- **CA note:** § 1280 — a writing made as a record of an act, condition, or event is admissible if (a) made **by and within the scope of duty of a public employee**, (b) made **at or near the time**, and (c) the **sources of information and method and time of preparation indicate trustworthiness**. California has **no express criminal-case police-report exclusion** like 803(8)(A)(ii), but statements of witnesses inside a report still need their own exception, and confrontation applies to testimonial reports. Like § 1271, § 1280 speaks of acts, conditions, or events, so evaluative opinions are more vulnerable. § 1284 covers absence of a public record.
- **How tested.** MBE: police officer's report offered by the prosecution (excluded under 803(8)); NTSB-type findings offered in a civil case (admitted, including conclusions).

### V.H Learned treatises
- **Rule (R-EVD-101)** — FRE 803(18): a statement contained in a **treatise, periodical, or pamphlet** if (A) the statement is **called to the attention of an expert witness on cross-examination or relied on by the expert on direct**; and (B) the publication is **established as a reliable authority** by the expert's admission or testimony, by another expert's testimony, or by **judicial notice**. If admitted, the statement **may be read into evidence but not received as an exhibit**. Admissible **substantively** (not just to impeach).
- **Requires an expert on the stand** to explain and apply it; the treatise cannot be offered alone.
- **CA note:** **No general learned-treatise hearsay exception.** Under § 721(b), an expert may be **cross-examined** on a publication only if (1) the witness **referred to, considered, or relied upon** it, (2) it has been **admitted** in evidence, or (3) it has been established as a **reliable authority** by the witness's admission, other expert testimony, or judicial notice — and the content is used to **test the opinion**, not as substantive evidence [S-512]. § 1341 admits historical works, books of science or art, and published maps or charts, made by persons indifferent between the parties, **only on facts of general notoriety and interest** [S-1555]. Under *Sanchez*, an expert may still describe **general background** information from treatises on direct. **Essay line:** "Under FRE 803(18) the excerpt is substantive evidence; under the CEC it is limited to impeaching the expert (§ 721(b))."
- **How tested.** Essays: Feb 2023 Q5 (treatise excerpt and the expert's own journal article, FRE) [VERIFY V-071].

### V.I Former testimony; depositions
- **Unavailability (R-EVD-102)** — required for all FRE 804 exceptions. A declarant is unavailable if the declarant (1) is **exempted** by a ruling on **privilege**; (2) **refuses to testify** despite a court order; (3) **testifies to not remembering** the subject matter; (4) cannot be present or testify because of **death or then-existing infirmity, physical illness, or mental illness**; or (5) is **absent** and the proponent has been unable, by **process or other reasonable means**, to procure attendance (and, for 804(b)(2)–(4), attendance **or testimony**, e.g., by deposition) (FRE 804(a)). A declarant is **not** unavailable if the proponent **procured or wrongfully caused** the unavailability to prevent testimony.
- **Former testimony (R-EVD-103)** — FRE 804(b)(1): testimony that (A) was given **as a witness at a trial, hearing, or lawful deposition**, whether in the current or a different proceeding, and (B) is now offered **against a party who had — or, in a civil case, whose predecessor in interest had — an opportunity and similar motive to develop it** by direct, cross, or redirect examination.
  - **Similar motive** is fact-specific: a prosecutor's motive at a grand jury is often **not** similar to its motive at trial (*United States v. Salerno*, 505 U.S. 317 (1992), rejected an "adversarial fairness" bypass and left similar motive to the lower courts). A preliminary hearing usually supplies a similar motive.
  - **Predecessor in interest** is read broadly by many circuits in civil cases (a party with a like interest and motive), narrowly by others.
- **Depositions in civil cases** are also usable under **FRCP 32(a)** (e.g., an adverse party's deposition for any purpose; a witness who is dead, more than 100 miles away, or ill) independently of 804, and as party admissions under 801(d)(2).
- **CA note:**
  - § 240 (unavailability): exempted on the ground of **privilege**, **disqualified**, **dead or unable to attend or testify** because of then-existing physical or mental illness or infirmity, absent and the court is **unable to compel** attendance, absent despite the proponent's **reasonable diligence**, or (§ 240(a)(6), added in 2010) **persistent in refusing to testify despite having been found in contempt**; expert testimony of crime-caused trauma may establish unavailability; not unavailable if the proponent procured it to prevent testimony [S-516] [S-513] [S-1551]. A court order alone is not enough for refusal — California requires the contempt finding. **Memory loss** is not listed; whether it can qualify (e.g., as mental infirmity) depends on case law [VERIFY V-990].
  - § 1291 (former testimony **against a party** to the former proceeding): admissible if the declarant is unavailable and (a)(1) the former testimony is offered **against the person who offered it** in the former proceeding (or his successor in interest), or (a)(2) the party against whom it is offered was a party to the former proceeding and had the **right and opportunity to cross-examine** with an **interest and motive similar** to that at the hearing.
  - § 1292 (former testimony **against a non-party** to the former proceeding): admissible **in a civil action** if the declarant is unavailable and the **party to the former proceeding** against whom it was offered had the right and opportunity to cross-examine with an **interest and motive similar** to that of the party against whom it is now offered — broader than the FRE "predecessor in interest" in civil cases; **not available against a criminal defendant**.
  - § 1290 defines former testimony (including depositions taken in compliance with law).
- **How tested.** MBE: prior trial testimony offered against a party who cross-examined at the first trial (admissible); grand jury testimony offered against the defendant (no opportunity to cross — inadmissible, and testimonial). Essays: often combined with confrontation.

### V.J Statements against interest
- **Rule (R-EVD-104)** — FRE 804(b)(3): a statement that (A) a **reasonable person in the declarant's position would have made only if the person believed it to be true** because, **when made**, it was so contrary to the declarant's **proprietary or pecuniary interest** or had so great a tendency to **invalidate the declarant's claim** against someone else or to **expose the declarant to civil or criminal liability**; and (B) **if offered in a criminal case** as one that tends to expose the declarant to criminal liability, is **supported by corroborating circumstances that clearly indicate its trustworthiness — after considering the totality of circumstances under which it was made and any evidence that supports or undermines it** (2024 amendment — Apply) [S-105]. The declarant must be **unavailable**.
- **Only the self-inculpatory parts** come in; **collateral** statements — including ones that shift blame to others — are excised, even if within a broader inculpatory narrative (*Williamson v. United States*, 512 U.S. 594 (1994)).
- **Contrast party admissions:** a party admission (801(d)(2)) need not be against interest and needs no unavailability, but must be offered against the declarant-party. A statement against interest is by a **non-party** and may be offered by **anyone**.
- **Confrontation.** An accomplice's custodial confession implicating the defendant is testimonial and barred by Crawford if the accomplice does not testify, whatever 804(b)(3) says.
- **CA note:** § 1230 — the declarant is unavailable and the statement, when made, was so far contrary to the declarant's **pecuniary or proprietary interest**, or so far subjected him to the risk of **civil or criminal liability**, or so far tended to render invalid a claim by him against another, **or created such a risk of making him an object of hatred, ridicule, or social disgrace in the community**, that a reasonable person would not have made it unless he believed it true. Differences:
  - California adds **social interest** (hatred, ridicule, social disgrace).
  - No statutory **corroboration** requirement, but California courts require the statement to be **trustworthy** and exclude **collateral** and blame-shifting portions (*People v. Leach* (1975) 15 Cal.3d 419; *People v. Duarte* (2000) 24 Cal.4th 603) [S-508] — consistent with *Williamson*.
- **How tested.** MBE: a declarant's statement "I and the defendant robbed the bank" offered against the defendant (only "I robbed" is against interest; "and the defendant" is collateral). Essays: a co-defendant's notes or confession (Feb 2020 Q4) [VERIFY V-071].

### V.K Other exceptions to the hearsay rule
- **Dying declaration (R-EVD-105)** — FRE 804(b)(2): in a **prosecution for homicide or in a civil case**, a statement that the declarant, **while believing the declarant's death to be imminent**, made about its **cause or circumstances**. The declarant must be unavailable but **need not actually die** (in a civil case). Belief in imminent death can be shown by the declarant's words, the wound's severity, or a doctor's statements. Dying declarations are often treated as a historical exception to Crawford (dictum in *Crawford* and *Giles*).
  - **CA note:** § 1242 — a statement made by a **dying person** respecting the **cause and circumstances of his death**, if made upon **personal knowledge** and under a **sense of immediately impending death**. Applies in **any** case (civil or criminal, homicide or not), but the declarant **must have died**.
- **Forfeiture by wrongdoing (R-EVD-106)** — FRE 804(b)(6): a statement offered **against a party that wrongfully caused — or acquiesced in wrongfully causing — the declarant's unavailability** as a witness, **and did so intending that result**. Proved by preponderance. Forfeits both the hearsay objection and the Confrontation Clause objection, but only if the defendant **intended to prevent the witness from testifying** (*Giles v. California*, 554 U.S. 353 (2008): killing the victim, without that intent, does not forfeit confrontation). **CA note:** § 1390 — a statement is admissible against a party who engaged in, or aided and abetted, wrongdoing that was **intended to, and did, procure** the declarant's unavailability; proved by a **preponderance** at a foundational hearing outside the jury; the finding may not rest solely on the declarant's unconfronted statement and must be supported by **independent corroborative evidence**; the judge may consider whether the statement is trustworthy and reliable. Enacted by AB 1723 (2010); its Jan. 1, 2016 sunset was repealed in 2015 (AB 593) [S-513] [S-1552].
- **Statements of personal or family history** — 804(b)(4) (declarant unavailable); records of religious organizations, family records, marriage certificates, reputation concerning family history and boundaries (803(11)–(13), (19)–(20), (23)).
- **Other 803 exceptions (R-EVD-108):** ancient documents (**prepared before January 1, 1998**, with authenticity established) (803(16)); market reports and commercial publications (803(17)); **reputation concerning character** (803(21)) — the basis for 405 reputation testimony; **judgment of a previous conviction** (803(22)) — a final judgment after trial or guilty plea (not nolo) for a crime punishable by more than a year, offered to prove a fact essential to the judgment (in a criminal case, the prosecution may use only judgments against the defendant, except for impeachment); judgments on personal, family, or general history or boundaries (803(23)).
- **Residual exception (R-EVD-107)** — FRE 807 (as amended 2019): a hearsay statement not admissible under 803 or 804 is admissible if (1) it is supported by **sufficient guarantees of trustworthiness — after considering the totality of circumstances** under which it was made and evidence, if any, **corroborating** it; and (2) it is **more probative** on the point for which it is offered than any other evidence the proponent can obtain through reasonable efforts. Reasonable **written notice** must be given before trial (or during trial for good cause). Used sparingly; "near-miss" statements may qualify.
- **CA note — California-only exceptions (R-EVD-109).** California has **no residual exception**; instead it has many specific statutory exceptions, including:
  - § 1370 (**threats of infliction of physical injury**): the declarant is **unavailable**; the statement **narrates, describes, or explains the infliction or threat** of physical injury upon the declarant; it was made **at or near the time** of the infliction or threat; it was **not made more than five years before the filing** of the current action; it was made under circumstances indicating **trustworthiness**; and it was made **in writing, electronically recorded, or to a physician, nurse, paramedic, or law enforcement official**. Notice to the adverse party is required [S-501]. Testimonial statements (e.g., to police after the emergency) still face Crawford.
  - § 1360 (**child abuse statements** by a child under 12 in criminal prosecutions, with a reliability hearing and testimony or corroboration) [VERIFY V-413].
  - § 1261 (statement of a **decedent** offered in an action on a claim or demand against the decedent's estate, made on personal knowledge when the matter was recently perceived and the recollection clear, and not made under circumstances indicating lack of trustworthiness) [VERIFY V-409].
  - § 1300 (judgment of a **felony conviction** offered in a civil action to prove a fact essential to the judgment, unless based on a nolo plea) [VERIFY V-409].
  - § 1380 (elder or dependent-adult abuse statements) [VERIFY V-413]; § 1390 (forfeiture; see V.K above) [S-1552].
- **How tested.** MBE: dying declaration in a robbery prosecution (not allowed under FRE); 807 as the "best remaining answer" is usually wrong. Essays: under the CEC, ask whether a California-specific exception fits (§ 1370 in domestic-violence prosecutions; § 1242 in a civil case).

### V.L Right to confront witnesses
- **The Crawford rule (R-EVD-110).** In a **criminal prosecution**, the Sixth Amendment Confrontation Clause (applied to the states through the Fourteenth) bars the admission of **testimonial** statements of a declarant who **does not testify at trial**, unless the declarant is **unavailable** and the defendant had a **prior opportunity to cross-examine** (*Crawford v. Washington*, 541 U.S. 36 (2004)). Reliability is irrelevant; *Ohio v. Roberts* is gone. Nontestimonial statements raise **no** Confrontation Clause issue (only hearsay law and due process).
- **When the clause is satisfied:** the declarant **testifies** at trial and is subject to cross (even with memory loss: *California v. Green*; *United States v. Owens*); the statement is **not offered for its truth** (but see *Smith v. Arizona* on expert basis); a **dying declaration** (historical exception, dictum); **forfeiture by wrongdoing** with intent to prevent testimony (*Giles*).
- **Testimonial — the primary-purpose test (R-EVD-111).**
  - Testimonial: prior testimony at a preliminary hearing, grand jury, or former trial; **police interrogations** whose primary purpose is to **establish or prove past events potentially relevant to later prosecution**; affidavits and **forensic certificates** prepared for trial.
  - Nontestimonial: statements to police whose primary purpose is to **meet an ongoing emergency** (*Davis v. Washington*, 547 U.S. 813 (2006) — 911 call during the attack; contrast *Hammon* — calm statements after the danger passed); statements to identify and locate an armed shooter at large (*Michigan v. Bryant*, 562 U.S. 344 (2011) — formality, the emergency's scope, and the declarant's and questioners' purposes); statements by a young child to **teachers** (*Ohio v. Clark*, 576 U.S. 237 (2015) — statements to persons other than law enforcement are much less likely to be testimonial); casual remarks to friends; business records; coconspirator statements in furtherance.
- **Forensic reports and expert conduits (R-EVD-112).** A sworn certificate of a lab analyst is testimonial; the analyst must testify (*Melendez-Diaz v. Massachusetts*, 557 U.S. 305 (2009)); a "surrogate" analyst who did not perform or observe the test cannot substitute (*Bullcoming v. New Mexico*, 564 U.S. 647 (2011)). When an expert conveys an absent analyst's statements in support of her opinion and the statements support the opinion only if true, they are offered for their truth; whether they are testimonial is a separate question (*Smith v. Arizona* (2024) [VERIFY V-035]). California reached the same "for its truth" conclusion in *People v. Sanchez* (2016) 63 Cal.4th 665.
- **Codefendant confessions (R-EVD-113).** In a **joint trial**, admitting a **nontestifying codefendant's confession** that **facially incriminates** the defendant violates the Confrontation Clause even with a limiting instruction (*Bruton v. United States*, 391 U.S. 123 (1968)). A confession redacted to eliminate the defendant's name **and any reference to his existence** is allowed with an instruction (*Richardson v. Marsh*, 481 U.S. 200 (1987)); obvious redactions (blanks, "deleted") are not (*Gray v. Maryland*, 523 U.S. 185 (1998)); a confession redacted to refer to "the other person", with a limiting instruction, does not violate the clause even if other evidence links the defendant (*Samia v. United States* (2023) [VERIFY V-035]). Remedies: sever, redact, or do not use the confession.
- **Face-to-face confrontation (R-EVD-114).** The clause normally guarantees a face-to-face meeting (*Coy v. Iowa*, 487 U.S. 1012 (1988): a screen without individualized findings violated it). One-way closed-circuit testimony by a child witness is permitted only on a **case-specific finding of necessity** — that the child would be traumatized by the defendant's presence, not merely by the courtroom (*Maryland v. Craig*, 497 U.S. 836 (1990)); screening a child witness likewise requires a case-specific finding (*Pitts v. Mississippi* (2025) [VERIFY V-034]).
- **Right to present a defense (R-EVD-115).** Due process and the Compulsory Process Clause can require admission of reliable defense evidence despite state evidence rules: mechanical application of hearsay and voucher rules to exclude another person's reliable confessions violated due process (*Chambers v. Mississippi*, 410 U.S. 284 (1973)); a rule excluding third-party-guilt evidence merely because the prosecution's case is strong is arbitrary (*Holmes v. South Carolina*, 547 U.S. 319 (2006)); a per se bar on the defendant's hypnotically refreshed testimony is unconstitutional (*Rock v. Arkansas*).
- **CA note:** the Confrontation Clause is federal constitutional law and applies identically in California courts; Truth-in-Evidence cannot override it. California cases apply Crawford to § 1370 statements (testimonial statements to police after the emergency are barred unless the declarant testified or was cross-examined) and to expert basis testimony (*Sanchez*). § 1204 excludes a hearsay statement against a criminal defendant if it was obtained in violation of the declarant's constitutional rights in a way that would bar it against the declarant [VERIFY V-409]. California applies Bruton (and its own earlier *Aranda* rule) [VERIFY V-409].
- **How tested.** MBE: 911 call vs. station-house statement; lab certificate; Bruton redactions. Essays: Evidence + Criminal crossovers — Feb 2024 Q4 and **Jul 2026 Q5** ("under the California Evidence Code and U.S. Constitution") — always add a Confrontation Clause paragraph for any testimonial hearsay offered by the prosecution [VERIFY V-071].

### General provisions

### Testimonial evidence
