# Evidence — primer

## 1. What Evidence law is for
American trials put fact-finding in the hands of a **jury of lay people**. Evidence law is the set of filters that decides **what the jury may hear**. Many rules exist because judges distrust a jury's ability to discount weak or inflammatory information. That is the key cultural difference from your PRC training: in a civil-law system, a professional judge receives most material and decides how much **weight** to give it. In US trials, much of that weighing happens **before** the jury hears anything, through **admissibility** rules.

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: PRC procedure asks mainly about 证据的真实性、合法性、关联性 (authenticity, legality, relevance) and then weight; US law adds many categorical exclusions (hearsay, character, privilege) applied before the jury hears the evidence (approx.).

Two other features follow from the jury system:
- **Party control.** The lawyers choose the evidence and **must object** to keep it out (R-EVD-008). Evidence admitted without objection can be used for any purpose.
- **Live testimony and cross-examination.** The system prefers a witness who speaks in court, under oath, and can be cross-examined. That preference explains the **hearsay rule** and the **Confrontation Clause**.

## 2. Two codes, one exam
- The **MBE** tests the **Federal Rules of Evidence (FRE)** as currently in effect [S-040].
- **California essays** name either the FRE or the **California Evidence Code (CEC)**, and switch between exams [S-083]. The two codes share a structure — the FRE (1975) drew on many of the same ideas as the CEC (1965) — but differ on a list of specific points. Learn the FRE as your base, then learn the California **differences** as a list ([`ca-distinctions.md`](ca-distinctions.md)).
- **California's Proposition 8** ("Truth-in-Evidence", Cal. Const. art. I, § 28(f)(2)) says that in **criminal** cases relevant evidence shall not be excluded, except under the hearsay rule, privileges, § 352, §§ 782 and 1103, and later two-thirds statutes [S-145]. It is the single most important California-only idea.

## 3. Map of the subject (the five NCBE categories)
| Category | Question it answers | Typical rules |
|---|---|---|
| **I Presentation of evidence** | How does evidence get in front of the jury, and how is a witness tested? | Personal knowledge, competency, lay opinion, objections, judicial notice, presumptions, form of questions, **impeachment** and rehabilitation |
| **II Relevancy and its limits** | Does it matter, and is it too dangerous? | Relevance (401), balancing (403 / § 352), **character evidence**, authentication, **expert testimony**, demonstrative evidence |
| **III Privileges and policy exclusions** | Should we keep out true and relevant evidence to protect a relationship or a policy? | Attorney-client, spousal, psychotherapist; insurance, repairs, settlement offers, pleas, rape shield |
| **IV Writings** | When must you produce the document itself? | Best evidence rule (FRE) / **secondary evidence rule** (CEC), summaries, completeness |
| **V Hearsay** | Can a statement made outside court be used to prove that what it says is true? | Definition, exclusions, exceptions, **Confrontation Clause** |

## 4. Key vocabulary
| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Proponent / opponent** | The party offering the evidence / the party objecting |
| **Foundation** | The preliminary facts the proponent must show before the evidence comes in (e.g., personal knowledge, authenticity, business-record elements) |
| **Admissible vs. weight** | Admissibility is the judge's gate; weight (how persuasive) is for the jury |
| **Logical relevance / legal relevance** | Any tendency to prove a fact of consequence / not substantially outweighed by unfair prejudice (403 / § 352) |
| **Direct / cross / redirect** | Questioning by the party who called the witness / by the opponent / back to the calling party |
| **Leading question** | A question that suggests the answer — allowed on cross, mostly not on direct |
| **Impeach / rehabilitate** | Attack / restore a witness's credibility |
| **Extrinsic evidence** | Proof from a source other than the witness being impeached (another witness, a document) |
| **Hearsay** | An out-of-court statement offered to prove the truth of what it asserts |
| **Declarant** | The person who made the out-of-court statement |
| **Exclusion / exception** | FRE categories that are "not hearsay" (801(d)) / hearsay that is nevertheless admissible (803, 804, 807). The CEC calls them all exceptions |
| **Unavailable** | Declarant cannot be had at trial (death, privilege, refusal, memory loss, absence) — needed for 804 exceptions |
| **Testimonial** | A statement made primarily to create evidence for prosecution — triggers the Confrontation Clause |
| **Privilege / holder / waiver** | A right to keep confidential communications out of evidence / the person who owns it / losing it by disclosure |
| **Limiting instruction** | The judge tells the jury to use evidence only for its proper purpose |
| **Authentication** | Showing an item is what it is claimed to be |
| **Best evidence rule** | To prove the content of a writing, produce the original (FRE); California's **secondary evidence rule** allows copies unless unfair |

## 5. How the pieces connect: the life of one item of evidence
Think of every item as passing through gates in order:
1. **Relevance gate** — any tendency to prove a fact of consequence (R-EVD-002).
2. **Balancing gate** — FRE 403 / § 352: exclude if probative value is substantially outweighed by unfair prejudice, confusion or waste (R-EVD-003).
3. **Special-rule gates** — character (R-EVD-035), policy exclusions (insurance, repairs, settlements, pleas), privileges.
4. **Witness gates** — competency, personal knowledge, proper opinion, proper question form.
5. **Writing gates** — authentication; best evidence / secondary evidence.
6. **Hearsay gate** — if it is an out-of-court statement offered for its truth, find an exclusion or exception for **each layer**.
7. **Constitutional gate** — in a criminal case, the Confrontation Clause for testimonial statements; due process.
8. **Use instruction** — if admitted for a limited purpose, a limiting instruction.

Once a witness is on the stand, the opponent can **impeach** (bias, inconsistent statements, convictions, character for untruthfulness, capacity, contradiction) and the proponent can **rehabilitate**.

## 6. The ideas that matter most
1. **Purpose decides admissibility.** The same words can be inadmissible hearsay for one purpose and admissible non-hearsay for another (notice, effect on the listener, verbal acts). The same prior act is barred as propensity but admitted to show intent or identity. Always ask, "Offered to prove what?"
2. **Hearsay is about the truth inference.** If the proponent needs the jury to believe the declarant was right, it is hearsay. Then go hunting: party admission? excited utterance? business record? Is the declarant unavailable (804)?
3. **Character evidence is banned to prove conduct — with exceptions.** The criminal defendant opens the door (mercy rule); the victim's character in self-defense; sex-offense propensity (FRE 413–415; § 1108); domestic violence in California (§ 1109). Prior acts for a non-propensity purpose (404(b) / § 1101(b)) are the main workaround.
4. **Impeachment has its own rules.** Prior convictions (FRE 609 vs. California moral turpitude), specific acts (608(b) vs. § 787 / Prop 8), inconsistent statements (now substantive under both codes [VERIFY V-028]).
5. **Privileges protect relationships at the cost of truth.** Identify the holder, the confidential communication, the purpose, exceptions and waiver. California's privileges are statutory and more numerous (physician-patient; broader spousal privilege).
6. **Writings need two checks.** Authentication (is it genuine?) and the best/secondary evidence rule (if you are proving its content, do you have the original or an acceptable substitute?). Its content may also be hearsay.
7. **Confrontation sits above the hearsay rules.** In a criminal case, testimonial hearsay from a witness the defendant never cross-examined is barred whatever the hearsay exceptions say (*Crawford*).
8. **California differs on a known list.** Prop 8; no general present sense impression, medical-diagnosis, employee-admission, learned-treatise or residual exception; § 1230 social interest; § 1242 any case; strict-liability repairs admissible (*Ault*); secondary evidence rule; Kelly and Sargon for experts.

## 7. How the subject is examined
- **MBE (25 scored questions).** Short fact patterns asking "Is the evidence admissible?" with four reasons. The right answer usually depends on the **purpose** of the evidence or on one element of an exception. See [`mbe-rules.md`](mbe-rules.md).
- **Essays.** A trial with several items; the call asks whether each was properly admitted or what objections should have been made. Go **item by item**, with the full gate sequence above. See [`essay-templates.md`](essay-templates.md).
- **PTs.** Occasionally a motion in limine or trial memo using a Library of evidence rules — apply the Library's version.
