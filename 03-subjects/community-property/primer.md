# Community Property — primer

Read this first. It explains what California community property law is trying to do, the vocabulary, and how the pieces fit together. Details and citations are in [`outline.md`](outline.md); essay wording is in [`rule-statements.md`](rule-statements.md).

## 1. The big picture

California treats marriage as an **economic partnership**. What either spouse earns through work during the marriage belongs to both, equally, from the moment it is earned. That shared pool is **community property (CP)**. Everything else, what a spouse owned before marriage, received as a gift or inheritance, or earned after the couple separated, is that spouse's **separate property (SP)**.

Almost every CP problem asks the same question about a list of assets: **who owns what, in what shares, and what happens to it now** (at divorce, at death, or when a creditor comes knocking). The State Bar essay gives you four or five assets and wants each one analyzed in turn.

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: think 夫妻共同财产 (CP) vs. 个人财产 (SP); the logic is familiar, but California's presumptions, tracing methods and mandatory equal division are more mechanical (approx.).

## 3. Key vocabulary

- **Community property (CP):** property acquired during marriage by a spouse domiciled in California (§ 760). Each spouse owns half of it now, not just at divorce (§ 751).
- **Separate property (SP):** owned before marriage; gift, bequest, devise or descent; rents and profits of SP; earnings after separation (§§ 770, 771).
- **Quasi-community property (QCP):** property acquired while living in another state that would have been CP if the couple had lived in California (§ 125). California treats it like CP at divorce and (for the decedent's QCP) at death.
- **Characterization:** deciding whether an asset is CP, SP, QCP or a mix.
- **Presumption:** a starting conclusion the law draws (e.g., "acquired during marriage → CP") that the other side must disprove.
- **Transmutation:** spouses changing the character of property by agreement. It needs an **express written declaration** (§ 852).
- **Commingling:** mixing CP and SP (usually in a bank account).
- **Tracing:** proving that an asset came from SP despite commingling (direct tracing or the exhaustion method).
- **Apportionment:** splitting one asset between CP and SP when both contributed (Pereira, Van Camp, Moore/Marsden, time rule).
- **Reimbursement:** paying back an estate that contributed to another without changing ownership (§ 2640 SP contributions; § 2641 education; Epstein credits).
- **Date of separation (DOS):** the date of a complete and final break: one spouse expresses the intent to end the marriage and acts consistently with it (§ 70).
- **Pro tanto:** "to that extent" — a proportional ownership share.

## 4. How the pieces connect

1. **Start with the community's time frame.** It begins at marriage and ends at DOS (or death). Domicile matters: no CP is created while the couple lives in a common-law state (that property may become QCP later).
2. **Characterize each asset at acquisition.** Look at **source** and **time**. Earnings during marriage → CP. Inheritance → SP. Purchase with mixed money → trace.
3. **Apply presumptions.** The general presumption favors CP. Title matters less than you might think: putting a CP-funded asset in one spouse's name does not make it SP without a § 852 writing (Valli). But joint title at divorce makes property presumptively CP (§ 2581).
4. **Check whether the spouses changed it.** A premarital agreement (strict statutory requirements) or a transmutation (express writing). Interspousal deals that favor one spouse are presumed to be the product of undue influence.
5. **Account for contributions.** When CP labor grows an SP business, the community gets a share (Pereira/Van Camp). When CP pays down the mortgage on one spouse's premarital house, the community gets a proportional share (Moore/Marsden). When SP pays into a CP asset, the SP spouse gets reimbursed (§ 2640).
6. **Then the remedy.** At divorce, the community estate is divided equally (§ 2550), with statutory exceptions. At death, the survivor already owns half the CP; the decedent can will only the other half (Prob. Code §§ 100, 6101).

## 5. The eight ideas that matter most

1. **Source, time, then presumptions.** Every asset; every time. This is the backbone of every selected answer.
2. **Gifts and inheritances are SP** (and stay SP if traceable). This is the opposite of the PRC default, so watch it.
3. **Title is not ownership between spouses.** A CP-funded asset in one name is still CP unless an express writing transmuted it (Valli; Brace). Joint title at divorce means CP (§ 2581), with § 2640 reimbursement for SP down payments.
4. **Transmutation needs an express written declaration** (§ 852; MacDonald). No oral deals, no part performance (Benson). Exception: small personal gifts between spouses (§ 852(c)). And a writing that benefits one spouse triggers the undue-influence presumption (§ 721).
5. **Labor is community; capital is separate.** Apportion business growth (Pereira for effort-driven growth, Van Camp for capital-driven growth) and home equity (Moore/Marsden). Apply both business formulas and argue.
6. **Spouses are fiduciaries.** Equal management, but no gifts without written consent, joinder for real property, notice for business dispositions, full disclosure; breach costs 50% or 100% of the asset (§ 1101).
7. **Date of separation ends the community.** Use § 70's two prongs; then post-DOS earnings are SP, post-DOS payments create Epstein credits, exclusive use creates Watts charges.
8. **Death essays: characterize first, then apply probate rules.** Survivor owns half of CP and half of the decedent's QCP; out-of-state land is not QCP at death; QCP transferred with strings attached can be recaptured (Prob. Code § 102).

## 6. What a good CP answer looks like

- **Short opening:** "California is a community property state. Property acquired during marriage is presumed CP (§ 760); property acquired before marriage, after separation, or by gift or inheritance is SP (§ 770). Each asset is characterized by its source, the time of acquisition, and any action of the parties that changed its character."
- **One heading per asset**, in the call's order. Under each: source/time → presumption → transmutation or agreement → tracing or apportionment → reimbursement → conclusion and division.
- **Separate headings** for debts (creditor question vs. allocation) and for fiduciary-duty claims.
- **Numbers when the facts give them** (Moore/Marsden, Pereira/Van Camp, time rule). Graders reward showing the formula even if you cannot finish the arithmetic.
- **A conclusion for every asset** ("Likely CP; W and H each receive half, subject to W's $50,000 § 2640 reimbursement").

## 7. Where people lose points

- Treating title as decisive.
- Forgetting § 2640 reimbursement after applying § 2581.
- Missing the undue-influence presumption on interspousal deeds.
- Using only one of Pereira/Van Camp.
- Ignoring the date-of-separation dispute.
- Mixing up creditor liability (§§ 910–1000) with allocation at divorce (§§ 2620–2627).
- On death questions, applying the dissolution QCP definition (which includes out-of-state land) instead of Prob. Code § 66 (which does not).
