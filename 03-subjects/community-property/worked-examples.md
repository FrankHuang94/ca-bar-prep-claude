# Community Property — worked examples

Original fact patterns, each answered in the order you would write it: **Rule → Application → Conclusion**. All facts are invented. Assume California domicile and law unless stated.

---

**1. Inheritance into a joint account (tracing).** W inherits $60,000 and deposits it in the joint account that also receives both salaries. Six months later she buys a car for $25,000 from the account. Her bank statements show $3,000 of salary on hand that day and all earlier salary spent on household bills.
- **Rule:** Commingled funds are CP unless traced; under the exhaustion method, if CP was exhausted by family expenses when the purchase was made, the purchase was from SP (R-CMP-028, R-CMP-030).
- **Application:** $3,000 of CP remained, so not all CP was exhausted; but direct tracing may work if W shows she intended to use her inheritance (R-CMP-029). Without proof of intent, at least $22,000 of the price must have come from SP because only $3,000 of CP existed.
- **Conclusion:** The car is likely SP in proportion to the SP traced ($22,000/$25,000) and CP as to $3,000, unless W proves she intended to use SP for the whole price.

**2. Deed adding a spouse to inherited property (transmutation + undue influence).** W inherited a house. After their marriage she signed a grant deed conveying it "to H and W, as joint tenants." W says she did it because H insisted it was "the only way to refinance."
- **Rule:** Transmutation requires an express written declaration (§ 852(a)); a grant deed has been held sufficient; an interspousal transfer that advantages one spouse is presumed to result from undue influence (R-CMP-022, R-CMP-023, R-CMP-016).
- **Application:** The deed is a signed writing by the adversely affected spouse using words of grant. But H gained a half interest, so the presumption arises; H must show W acted freely with full understanding. H's pressure and the refinance pretext cut against him.
- **Conclusion:** The transmutation is likely set aside for undue influence; the house remains W's SP. If it stands, the house is CP at dissolution (§ 2581) and W can seek § 2640 reimbursement of its value at transfer unless the deed waived it (R-CMP-045).

**3. Down payment from inheritance on a jointly titled home (§ 2581 + § 2640).** H uses $80,000 of SP for the down payment on a $500,000 house titled "H and W, community property." At divorce the house is worth $700,000 with a $350,000 loan.
- **Rule:** Property acquired during marriage in joint form is presumed CP at dissolution, rebuttable only by a title statement or written agreement; traced SP contributions to its acquisition are reimbursed without interest, capped at net value, absent a written waiver (R-CMP-013, R-CMP-042).
- **Application:** No SP statement or agreement exists, so the house is CP. H traces $80,000 to SP; net equity is $350,000.
- **Conclusion:** H receives $80,000 off the top; the remaining $270,000 is divided equally ($135,000 each).

**4. Premarital house paid down during marriage (Moore/Marsden).** W bought a condo for $400,000 two years before marriage ($80,000 down; $320,000 loan) and paid $20,000 of principal before the wedding, when it was worth $450,000. During marriage, CP paid $60,000 of principal. At trial it is worth $650,000.
- **Rule:** Community principal payments on an SP purchase loan create a pro tanto CP interest; premarital appreciation is SP (R-CMP-037, R-CMP-038).
- **Application:** CP% = $60,000 ÷ $400,000 = 15%. Marital appreciation = $650,000 − $450,000 = $200,000. CP interest = $60,000 + 15% × $200,000 = $90,000. Loan balance $240,000; equity $410,000; SP interest $320,000.
- **Conclusion:** The community owns $90,000 of the equity ($45,000 to each spouse); W keeps $320,000 as SP.

**5. Inherited business, effort-driven growth (Pereira vs. Van Camp).** H inherited a landscaping company worth $150,000 at marriage. Over 12 years he worked 60-hour weeks and drew a $50,000 salary (a fair wage would have been $90,000). At DOS it is worth $1.2 million.
- **Rule:** Apportion growth from community effort; Pereira gives SP a fair return and the rest to CP; Van Camp gives CP the value of services less what was drawn (R-CMP-034 to R-CMP-036).
- **Application:** Pereira at 10%: SP = $150,000 + $180,000 = $330,000; CP = $870,000. Van Camp: CP = ($90,000 − $50,000) × 12 = $480,000; SP = $720,000. Growth came from H's personal labor in a service business.
- **Conclusion:** Pereira fits better; the community likely receives about $870,000, split equally.

**6. Separate rental building in a rising market (Van Camp).** W's inherited apartment building, managed by a professional company, rose from $2 million to $5 million during a 10-year marriage; W reviewed monthly statements.
- **Rule:** Growth caused by the SP capital or the market, with minimal spousal effort, remains SP (R-CMP-041, R-CMP-035).
- **Application:** W's effort was minimal; a manager ran the property; the gain tracks the market.
- **Conclusion:** The appreciation and rents are W's SP; no apportionment, or at most a Van Camp salary credit that the community has already received.

**7. Transmutation by conversation (no writing).** H tells W, "the boat is all yours," and W uses it every weekend for years. It was bought with CP. H never signed anything.
- **Rule:** A post-1984 transmutation needs an express writing; part performance does not excuse it; the personal-gift exception covers only personal items not substantial in value (R-CMP-022, R-CMP-024).
- **Application:** No writing; a boat is not a personal article like jewelry, and it is likely substantial in value.
- **Conclusion:** The boat remains CP.

**8. Gift of a necklace between spouses (§ 852(c)).** For their anniversary H gives W a $4,000 necklace bought with CP; their combined annual income is $300,000.
- **Rule:** No writing is needed for an interspousal gift of jewelry used principally by the donee and not substantial in value relative to the marriage (R-CMP-024).
- **Application:** Jewelry worn by W; $4,000 is modest for this couple.
- **Conclusion:** The necklace is W's SP. (A $60,000 necklace for a couple earning $80,000 would likely remain CP.)

**9. Prenuptial agreement signed under time pressure.** Three days before the wedding, H's lawyer gives W the final prenup making each spouse's earnings SP and waiving spousal support. W reads it, does not hire a lawyer, and signs a separate waiver of counsel.
- **Rule:** An agreement is deemed involuntary unless, among other things, the challenger had seven calendar days between first receiving the final agreement and signing; support waivers need independent counsel at signing (R-CMP-019, R-CMP-021).
- **Application:** Only three days passed. W had no counsel, so the support waiver also fails under § 1612(c).
- **Conclusion:** The agreement is unenforceable; W's and H's earnings are CP under default law, and support is determined under § 4320.

**10. Life insurance in one spouse's name (title vs. transmutation).** During marriage H buys a whole-life policy with CP, listing W as owner. At divorce W claims it as her SP.
- **Rule:** Title in one spouse's name does not make CP-funded property SP without a § 852 transmutation (R-CMP-012, R-CMP-054).
- **Application:** Paid with CP; no express written declaration.
- **Conclusion:** The policy is CP (Valli) and its cash value is divided equally.

**11. Pension with service before, during and after marriage (time rule).** W worked 30 years for a state agency: 5 before marriage, 20 during marriage before DOS, 5 after DOS. Her pension is $6,000 a month.
- **Rule:** Pensions are CP to the extent earned during marriage; time rule (R-CMP-048, R-CMP-049).
- **Application:** Community fraction = 20 ÷ 30 = 2/3; $4,000 a month is CP.
- **Conclusion:** H receives $2,000 a month (half of the CP portion), by QDRO or reserved jurisdiction.

**12. Disability benefits after separation.** H's employer-paid disability policy (premiums deducted from his salary during marriage) starts paying $5,000 a month after DOS when he is injured. His retirement benefit, had he retired then, would have been $2,000 a month.
- **Rule:** Characterize disability benefits by what they replace (R-CMP-051).
- **Application:** $3,000 replaces lost post-DOS earnings; $2,000 substitutes for retirement earned during marriage.
- **Conclusion:** $3,000 is H's SP; the $2,000 portion is CP to the extent of marital service.

**13. Sailboat on credit (lender intent).** During marriage W borrows $70,000 to buy a sailboat; the bank required her inherited bonds as collateral, but its file notes it also relied on "household income." Both spouses signed.
- **Rule:** Loan proceeds are CP unless the lender relied primarily (traditionally solely) on SP (R-CMP-058).
- **Application:** SP collateral favors W, but reliance on household (community) income and H's signature show community reliance (Grinius).
- **Conclusion:** The sailboat is likely CP.

**14. Secret gift to a friend (management).** Without W's knowledge, H gives his golf partner a $12,000 CP watch.
- **Rule:** A gift of CP personal property without written consent is voidable: entirely during the donor's life, half after death; § 1101 remedies apply (R-CMP-064, R-CMP-068).
- **Application:** No written consent; the watch is not a trivial gift.
- **Conclusion:** W can recover the watch; alternatively she may seek § 1101(g) relief at dissolution.

**15. Forged deed of trust (joinder; debts).** H forges W's signature on a $100,000 deed of trust on their CP home to fund his restaurant; the lender records it.
- **Rule:** Both spouses must join to encumber CP real property; the non-joining spouse may void the encumbrance (within one year of recording where title is in one name); the community estate remains liable for H's marital debt; W's SP is not (R-CMP-067, R-CMP-059, R-CMP-084, R-CMP-086).
- **Application:** Title is in both names, so the lender cannot rely on the § 1102(c) presumption. W did not join.
- **Conclusion:** A forged signature is no joinder at all, so W can set aside the deed of trust as to the CP home; the lender keeps an unsecured claim against the community estate and H's SP; W may seek § 1101 remedies against H.

**16. Concealed lottery prize (nondisclosure).** During the marriage W wins $200,000 with a ticket bought from the grocery money and deposits it in a secret account. She omits it from her disclosures.
- **Rule:** CP-funded winnings are CP; concealment with fraud triggers a 100% award (R-CMP-057, R-CMP-077).
- **Application:** The ticket was bought with CP; hiding it in a secret account and omitting it from sworn disclosures is fraudulent.
- **Conclusion:** H is likely awarded the entire $200,000 under § 1101(h).

**17. Date of separation in the same house.** In January H tells W he wants a divorce and moves into the guest room; they stop sharing meals and finances but take the children on a joint vacation in July. W says the DOS is August, when H moved out.
- **Rule:** DOS requires an expressed intent to end the marriage and consistent conduct, considering all evidence; separate residences are not required (R-CMP-070).
- **Application:** January has both prongs; the July vacation for the children's sake is weak evidence of reconciliation.
- **Conclusion:** January is likely the DOS; H's bonus earned from February is his SP.

**18. Post-separation mortgage payments and exclusive use (Epstein/Watts).** After DOS W lives in the CP house for 18 months and pays the $3,000 mortgage (mostly interest) from her salary; fair rent is $3,500.
- **Rule:** SP payments of community debts after DOS give Epstein credits; exclusive use may give Watts charges (R-CMP-073, R-CMP-074).
- **Application:** W paid $54,000; her use was worth $63,000.
- **Conclusion:** The court likely nets the claims; W's credit is offset by a Watts charge, leaving little or nothing owed either way.

**19. Accident settlement (§§ 780, 2603).** During marriage a delivery truck injures H; he settles for $150,000 and keeps it in an account in his name. At divorce W asks for half.
- **Rule:** PI damages for a claim arising during marriage are CP but assigned to the injured spouse at dissolution unless justice requires otherwise, with at least half to the injured spouse; commingling defeats this (R-CMP-056, R-CMP-078).
- **Application:** Not commingled; nothing shows W's special need.
- **Conclusion:** The whole $150,000 goes to H.

**20. Tort while running a family errand (debts).** W negligently injures a pedestrian while driving to buy groceries for the family; judgment $300,000. H has a $200,000 inheritance.
- **Rule:** Community estate liable for either spouse's marital debts; tort liability incurred for the community's benefit is satisfied first from CP, then the tortfeasor's SP; the non-tortfeasor's SP is not liable (R-CMP-084, R-CMP-086, R-CMP-088).
- **Application:** The errand benefited the community.
- **Conclusion:** The creditor collects first from CP, then from W's SP; H's inheritance is protected.

**21. Moving from a common-law state; divorce (QCP).** H and W lived in State X for 15 years, where H's salary bought a brokerage account and a lake house in State X, both in H's name. They then moved to California and now divorce here.
- **Rule:** Property acquired while domiciled elsewhere that would have been CP is QCP, divided equally at dissolution when both spouses are domiciled in California; out-of-state land is divided without changing title where possible (R-CMP-003, R-CMP-083).
- **Application:** H's salary would have been CP in California; both spouses now live here.
- **Conclusion:** Both assets are QCP; the court may award the State X lake house to H and give W the brokerage account or an equalizing payment.

**22. Same couple, but H dies instead (QCP at death; recapture).** Before dying domiciled in California, H deeded the State X lake house to his brother and put the brokerage account into joint tenancy with his daughter, without W's consent.
- **Rule:** At death QCP includes personal property anywhere and California land, not out-of-state land; the survivor may recapture half of QCP transferred without consideration or consent where the decedent kept control or survivorship (R-CMP-092, R-CMP-093).
- **Application:** The brokerage account is QCP and the joint tenancy is a survivorship transfer without consent. The lake house is not QCP at death.
- **Conclusion:** W can make the daughter restore half the account; the lake house transfer is governed by State X law.

**23. Education (§ 2641).** During a 7-year marriage CP paid $90,000 of H's dental school tuition 4 years before W filed; H also took $120,000 of student loans.
- **Rule:** The degree is not property; the community is reimbursed with interest; loans are assigned to the student; contributions within 10 years are presumed not to have substantially benefited the community (R-CMP-046, R-CMP-047, R-CMP-080).
- **Application:** Four years: presumption of no substantial benefit applies; no offsetting education for W.
- **Conclusion:** The community is reimbursed $90,000 plus interest; H keeps the loans.

**24. Stock options vesting after separation (Nelson).** Eighteen months before DOS, W's employer granted options vesting 36 months after grant, to retain her for future work.
- **Rule:** Options granted during marriage are CP to the extent earned by community effort; use the Nelson fraction for options rewarding future services (R-CMP-053).
- **Application:** Grant → DOS = 18 months; grant → vesting = 36 months.
- **Conclusion:** 50% of the vesting shares are CP; H gets one-quarter of the shares.

**25. Intestate death with a sibling (intestacy).** W dies intestate. She leaves H and a brother; no children or parents. Her estate includes a CP house, QCP investments and an SP inheritance.
- **Rule:** The surviving spouse takes the decedent's half of CP and QCP, and one-half of the SP when the decedent leaves no issue but a parent's issue (R-CMP-094).
- **Application:** The brother is the issue of W's parents.
- **Conclusion:** H takes the entire CP house and QCP investments and half the inheritance; the brother takes the other half of the inheritance.

**26. Putative spouse (threshold).** W married H in a church ceremony; unknown to her, H was still married. They lived together 10 years, buying a house with their salaries.
- **Rule:** A party who believed in good faith that the marriage was valid is a putative spouse; quasi-marital property is divided equally (R-CMP-006).
- **Application:** W honestly believed the marriage valid (subjective test).
- **Conclusion:** The house is quasi-marital property divided equally between W and H, subject to any claim of H's lawful spouse to H's earnings (R-CMP-006).

**27. Military retired pay waived for disability (preemption).** At the divorce W was awarded half of H's disposable military retired pay. Later H waives part of it to receive VA disability pay, cutting W's share.
- **Rule:** States may divide disposable retired pay under USFSPA but may not divide waived pay or order indemnification for the reduction (R-CMP-100).
- **Application:** The waiver reduced W's share after the decree.
- **Conclusion:** A California court cannot order H to reimburse W (Howell).
