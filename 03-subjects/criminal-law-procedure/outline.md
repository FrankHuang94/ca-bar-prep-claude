# Criminal Law and Procedure — comprehensive outline

**How this outline is organized.** Headings follow the NCBE MBE Subject Matter Outline numbering exactly (I–V) [S-040]. The State Bar's essay-outline sections are attached to the NCBE heading where their content lives (for example, "State Bar essay outline V: Exculpation" sits under IV.E). Each rule points to its essay-ready block in [rule-statements.md](rule-statements.md) (`R-CRM-###`).

**Reading order for a first pass (no coursework yet).** Read **IV General principles first** (act, mental state, causation, defenses), then I Homicide, II Other crimes, III Inchoate crimes and parties, and finally V Constitutional protections. The NCBE order puts the specific crimes first; the logic of the subject runs the other way.

**Which law to apply.**

## I Homicide

**Overview.** Homicide is the killing of a human being by another human being. Criminal homicide divides into **murder** (killing with malice aforethought) and **manslaughter** (unlawful killing without malice). Every homicide question asks two things: (1) which **malice state** (if any) the defendant had, and (2) whether a **mitigating** doctrine (provocation, imperfect self-defense) or **grading** statute (first vs. second degree) applies (R-CRM-031).

**Common-law murder.** The unlawful killing of a human being with malice aforethought. "Malice aforethought" is a term of art covering four mental states:

| Malice state | Meaning | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Intent to kill (express malice) | Purpose to cause death, or knowledge that death is practically certain | R-CRM-032 |
| 2. Intent to inflict serious bodily injury | Purpose to cause grievous harm; death results | R-CRM-035 |
| 3. Depraved heart (abandoned and malignant heart) | Conscious disregard of an extremely high, unjustified risk to human life | R-CRM-036 |
| 4. Felony murder | Killing during commission or attempt of an inherently dangerous felony | R-CRM-039 |

**Threshold points.**
- **Human being.** At common law the victim must have been born alive. Many statutes now cover a fetus; California's murder statute reaches "a human being, or a fetus" (Pen. Code § 187(a)), with exceptions for lawful abortion, and does not require viability (*People v. Davis* (1994) 7 Cal.4th 797) [VERIFY V-251].
- **Death.** Modern law uses brain death (irreversible cessation of all brain function).
- **Year-and-a-day rule.** At common law death had to occur within a year and a day of the act. Most jurisdictions have abolished it; retroactive judicial abolition does not violate due process (*Rogers v. Tennessee*, 532 U.S. 451 (2001)).
- **Causation** must be shown for every homicide: see IV.D.

**Grading.** Most states divide murder into degrees by statute (the Pennsylvania model). First-degree murder typically covers (a) willful, deliberate, premeditated killings; (b) killings by specified means (poison, lying in wait, torture, explosives); and (c) felony murder during enumerated felonies. Everything else is second-degree murder. California follows this structure (Pen. Code § 189(a)) [S-357] [VERIFY V-253].

**MPC.** The MPC abandons "malice" and degrees: murder is a killing committed purposely, knowingly, or recklessly under circumstances manifesting extreme indifference to human life (MPC § 210.2); manslaughter is a reckless killing or a killing under extreme mental or emotional disturbance (§ 210.3); negligent homicide is a negligent killing (§ 210.4) (R-CRM-038).

**How tested.** MBE: classify a killing by mental state, often with a provocation or felony-murder twist. Essays: "What crimes can D be charged with?" calls require murder → degree → manslaughter alternatives for each death (Feb 2022 Q1: robbery deaths of a customer and a co-felon; Jul 2026 Q5: second-degree murder trial).

### I.A Intended killings

An intended killing is murder (express malice) unless a mitigating doctrine reduces it to voluntary manslaughter, and it is first-degree murder if premeditated and deliberate (R-CRM-032).

**Deadly-weapon inference.** The jury may infer intent to kill from the intentional use of a deadly weapon aimed at a vital part of the body. It is a permissible inference, not a mandatory presumption; a mandatory presumption that shifts the burden on intent violates due process (*Sandstrom v. Montana*, 442 U.S. 510 (1979)) (R-CRM-119).

**Intent vs. motive.** Motive (why) is not an element; intent (purpose or knowledge as to death) is. Mercy killing with intent to kill is murder.

#### I.A.1 Premeditation, deliberation

**Rule.** First-degree murder by premeditation requires that the defendant (1) **premeditated** — thought about the killing beforehand — and (2) **deliberated** — reflected on it with a cool mind, weighing the decision (R-CRM-033).

**How much time?** Jurisdictions differ:

| Approach | Rule | Authority |
|---|---|---|
| "No time is too short" | A killing can be premeditated even if reflection is nearly instantaneous | Pennsylvania line, e.g., *Commonwealth v. Carroll* (Pa. 1963) |
| Appreciable time | Some appreciable time for reflection is required; a quick decision is second degree | Many modern courts |
| California | Reflection matters, not duration; courts look to **planning**, **motive** and **manner** of killing | *People v. Anderson* (1968) 70 Cal.2d 15 |
| MPC | No premeditation category; all purposeful or knowing killings are murder | MPC § 210.2 |

**MBE vs. essay.** On the MBE, premeditation is a fact question: look for planning (buying a weapon, lying in wait), a prior relationship or motive, and a methodical manner. On an essay, use the Anderson categories as an organizing device under a general-principles rule.

**Evidence that tends to show premeditation:** acquiring a weapon in advance; luring the victim; lying in wait; prior threats; a killing method requiring several steps (strangling for minutes, multiple wounds to vital areas after the victim is helpless).

**Statutory first-degree categories (means).** Poison, lying in wait and torture are first-degree by statute in many states. In California, murder by destructive device or explosive, weapon of mass destruction, armor-penetrating ammunition, poison, lying in wait, torture, or discharging a firearm from a vehicle at a person outside with intent to kill is first-degree murder (Pen. Code § 189(a)) [S-357] [VERIFY V-253].

**Intoxication and mental disorder** can negate premeditation and deliberation (specific-intent-type elements) in most jurisdictions; see IV.C. In California, voluntary intoxication evidence is admissible on whether the defendant premeditated, deliberated or harbored express malice (Pen. Code § 29.4(b)) [S-361].

#### I.A.2 Provocation

**Rule.** An intentional killing is reduced from murder to **voluntary manslaughter** if the defendant killed in the **heat of passion** arising from **adequate provocation**, before a reasonable person would have cooled off and before the defendant actually cooled (R-CRM-034).

**Elements.**
1. **Adequate provocation:** conduct that would cause a reasonable person to lose self-control and act from passion rather than judgment.
2. **Actual provocation:** the defendant was in fact in the heat of passion.
3. **No reasonable cooling time** had passed.
4. **Defendant did not actually cool off.**

**Recognized categories at common law:** serious battery; mutual combat; discovering a spouse in the act of adultery; an unlawful arrest (some courts); a violent assault on a close relative. **Words alone** are not adequate provocation under the majority rule; some courts accept informational words (being told of a past assault on a family member).

**Cooling time** is objective: the jury asks whether a reasonable person would have cooled. A "rekindling" event (seeing the provoker again) may restart passion in some courts; long brooding generally defeats the doctrine, though California has accepted a long period of provocative conduct that culminated in a final triggering event (*People v. Berry* (1976) 18 Cal.3d 509).

**Comparison.**

| Issue | Common law / majority | MPC (EMED) | California |
|---|---|---|---|
| Trigger | Specific categories; words alone insufficient | Any "extreme mental or emotional disturbance for which there is reasonable explanation or excuse" | Provocation that would cause an ordinary person of average disposition to act rashly, from passion rather than judgment (not necessarily to kill) |
| Standard | Reasonable person | Reasonableness judged from the viewpoint of a person in the actor's situation under the circumstances as the actor believes them to be | Objective ordinary-person test |
| Cooling time | Bar if reasonable person would cool | No strict cooling bar | Bar if sufficient time for an ordinary person's passion to subside |
| Authority | — | MPC § 210.3(1)(b) | Pen. Code § 192(a); *People v. Beltran* (2013) 56 Cal.4th 935 |

**MBE vs. CA essay.** MBE: common-law categories, words alone insufficient, objective cooling time. Essay: state the common-law rule and add one line on the MPC's broader EMED test.

**Imperfect self-defense** (an honest but unreasonable belief in the need for deadly force) also reduces murder to voluntary manslaughter in many jurisdictions, including California (*In re Christian S.* (1994) 7 Cal.4th 768; *People v. Flannel* (1979) 25 Cal.3d 668). See R-CRM-023.

**Provocation by a person other than the victim.** If the defendant, provoked by A, kills B by mistake while trying to kill A, the mitigation transfers along with the intent (transferred intent, R-CRM-008). If the defendant intentionally kills an innocent bystander who did not provoke him, most courts deny mitigation.

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: Heat-of-passion manslaughter has no exact PRC counterpart; PRC courts treat a victim's serious fault as a sentencing mitigation within intentional homicide (approx.).

### I.B Unintended killings

An unintended killing is **murder** if the defendant intended serious bodily injury (I.B.1), acted with depraved-heart recklessness (I.B.2), or killed during a qualifying felony (I.B.3). It is **involuntary manslaughter** if the defendant was criminally negligent (I.B.2) or killed during an unlawful act not amounting to a qualifying felony (I.B.4).

#### I.B.1 Intent to injure

**Rule.** A killing is murder if the defendant intended to inflict **serious (grievous) bodily injury** and death resulted, even though the defendant did not intend death (R-CRM-035).

- Serious bodily injury = injury creating a substantial risk of death, or causing permanent disfigurement or loss of a bodily function.
- The MPC has no separate category; such killings are usually murder under the extreme-indifference standard (§ 210.2(1)(b)) or manslaughter as reckless killings.
- California treats this as implied malice (Pen. Code § 188(a)(2)).

**Example.** D beats V with a tire iron intending to break his legs; V dies of internal bleeding. Intent-to-inflict-serious-bodily-injury murder (second degree, absent premeditation of the killing).

#### I.B.2 Reckless and negligent killings

**Depraved-heart murder.** A killing is murder if the defendant acted with **extreme recklessness** — conscious disregard of a very high and unjustified risk of death, showing an "abandoned and malignant heart" (R-CRM-036). Classic examples: firing into an occupied room or a moving train; playing Russian roulette with another; dropping a heavy object onto a crowded street; keeping dangerous dogs with knowledge of the risk (*People v. Knoller* (2007) 41 Cal.4th 139: subjective awareness of a risk to human life suffices for implied malice).

**Involuntary manslaughter (criminal negligence).** A killing is involuntary manslaughter if the defendant acted with **criminal negligence** — a gross deviation from the standard of care that a reasonable person would observe, creating a substantial and unjustified risk of death or serious injury (R-CRM-037). Ordinary (civil) negligence is not enough. Many courts require awareness of the risk (recklessness); others accept an objective gross-negligence standard (*Commonwealth v. Welansky* (Mass. 1944) 316 Mass. 383: nightclub owner who locked fire exits).

**The line between them** is the degree of risk and the defendant's awareness:

| Mental state | Risk | Awareness | Homicide |
|---|---|---|---|
| Extreme recklessness | Very high risk of death | Aware and indifferent | Second-degree (depraved-heart) murder |
| Recklessness | Substantial and unjustified risk | Aware | Involuntary manslaughter (common law); manslaughter (MPC § 210.3(1)(a)) |
| Criminal negligence | Substantial and unjustified risk | Should have been aware | Involuntary manslaughter (common law); negligent homicide (MPC § 210.4) |
| Ordinary negligence | Unreasonable risk | Should have been aware | Not criminal homicide (civil tort only) |

**Drunk driving.** A drunk-driving death is usually vehicular or involuntary manslaughter; it can be depraved-heart murder if the facts show conscious disregard (prior DUI warnings, extreme speed). California: *People v. Watson* (1981) 30 Cal.3d 290 permits second-degree murder on implied malice.

**MBE vs. CA essay.** MBE: use the four-row table; the MBE often tests whether the defendant was "aware" of the risk. Essay: discuss both murder (depraved heart) and involuntary manslaughter, applying the facts on awareness and degree of risk, and conclude.

#### I.B.3 Felony murder

**Rule.** A killing committed during the perpetration or attempted perpetration of an **inherently dangerous felony** is murder, even if the killing was accidental; the intent to commit the felony supplies the malice (R-CRM-039).

**Qualifying felonies.**
- Common-law core: **burglary, arson, robbery, rape, kidnapping** (and mayhem, escape, sodomy in some lists). Statutes often list these as first-degree felony murder.
- Non-listed felonies support (second-degree) felony murder only if **inherently dangerous**. Courts test this either **in the abstract** (the elements of the felony, considered without the facts; California's approach in *People v. Phillips* (1966) 64 Cal.2d 574 and *People v. Burroughs* (1984) 35 Cal.3d 824) or **as committed** (on the facts of the case).

**Limitations (R-CRM-040).**
1. **Independent felony (merger).** The felony must be independent of the killing. Assault with a deadly weapon or aggravated battery "merges" with the homicide and cannot serve as the predicate; otherwise every assault-caused death would be murder (*People v. Ireland* (1969) 70 Cal.2d 522; *People v. Chun* (2009) 45 Cal.4th 1172).
2. **Defendant guilty of the felony.** A defense to the felony (duress, insanity, lack of intent) defeats felony murder.
3. **During the felony.** The killing must occur from the attempt through **immediate flight**; flight ends when the felon reaches a place of temporary safety.
4. **In furtherance / causation.** The death must be a foreseeable result of the felony, and the felon's act must cause it. Most courts hold a felon liable for a heart attack caused by the fright of the robbery if the victim's condition is taken as found (*People v. Stamp* (1969) 2 Cal.App.3d 203).
5. **Who did the killing** (R-CRM-041):

| Killer | Agency theory (majority) | Proximate-cause theory (minority) |
|---|---|---|
| A co-felon kills a victim or bystander | All felons guilty of felony murder | All felons guilty |
| A victim or police officer kills a bystander | No felony murder: the killing was not by a felon | Felons guilty if the killing was a foreseeable result of the felony |
| A victim or police officer kills a co-felon | No felony murder | Many proximate-cause courts still exclude a justifiable killing of a co-felon |
| Authority | *State v. Canola* (N.J. 1977) 73 N.J. 206; *People v. Washington* (1965) 62 Cal.2d 777 | Minority of states |

**Provocative-act doctrine (California and some other states).** Where a felon's own malicious conduct (for example, starting a gunfight) provokes a victim or officer to kill, the felon can be guilty of murder on a malice theory, not felony murder (*People v. Gilbert* (1965) 63 Cal.2d 690) [VERIFY V-251].

**Accomplices and the death penalty.** A non-killer accomplice may be executed only if he was a major participant in the felony and acted with reckless indifference to human life (*Tison v. Arizona*, 481 U.S. 137 (1987)); a minor participant who did not kill, attempt or intend to kill may not (*Enmund v. Florida*, 458 U.S. 782 (1982)) (R-CRM-118).

**MPC.** The MPC has no felony-murder rule; it creates a presumption of extreme indifference when the actor is engaged in robbery, rape, arson, burglary, kidnapping or felonious escape (MPC § 210.2(1)(b)).

**California (SB 1437).** Since 2019, a participant in an enumerated felony in which a death occurs is liable for murder only if he (1) was the actual killer, (2) with intent to kill aided or abetted the actual killer in first-degree murder, or (3) was a **major participant** in the felony and acted with **reckless indifference to human life** (Pen. Code § 189(e)) [S-357]; malice may not be imputed based solely on participation in a crime (§ 188(a)(3)) (R-CRM-043). The "major participant / reckless indifference" standard tracks *People v. Banks* (2015) 61 Cal.4th 788 and *People v. Clark* (2016) 63 Cal.4th 522, as *People v. Strong* (2022) explained [S-358] [VERIFY V-251]. Whether **second-degree** felony murder survives § 188(a)(3) is not settled in the sources checked for this repo [VERIFY V-252].

**MBE vs. CA essay.** MBE: common-law felony murder with the agency rule unless told otherwise. Essay: apply general felony-murder principles (enumerated or inherently dangerous felony, merger, timing, agency vs. proximate cause); add one line on § 189(e) only if the call invites California law. Feb 2022 Q1 tested felony murder for both a customer's death and a co-felon's death.

**Worked example.** During a liquor-store robbery, a clerk shoots at the robbers and kills a customer. Under the agency rule, the robbers are not guilty of felony murder for the customer's death (the clerk is not their agent); under the proximate-cause rule they are, because armed resistance was foreseeable.

#### I.B.4 Misdemeanor manslaughter

**Rule.** An unintended killing during the commission of an **unlawful act not amounting to a felony** (or a non-qualifying felony) is involuntary manslaughter (R-CRM-042).

**Limits.** Many courts restrict the rule to acts that are **malum in se** (inherently wrongful, like battery) rather than malum prohibitum (regulatory), or require that the unlawful act be the proximate cause of death or dangerous in the circumstances. The MPC rejects the doctrine; the killing is judged only for recklessness or negligence.

**California.** Involuntary manslaughter includes a killing "in the commission of an unlawful act, not amounting to a felony; or in the commission of a lawful act which might produce death, in an unlawful manner, or without due caution and circumspection" (Pen. Code § 192(b)).

**Example.** D slaps V (a misdemeanor battery); V falls, hits her head and dies. Misdemeanor manslaughter (battery is malum in se), unless the jurisdiction requires foreseeability of death and the court finds none.

## II Other crimes

Other crimes are tested on the MBE through their **elements** (often with one element missing) and on essays through "What crimes can D be charged with?" calls (theft, robbery, burglary, receiving and kidnapping have all appeared). For each crime, list the elements, apply each to the facts, then consider defenses.

### II.A Theft and receiving stolen goods

**Larceny (R-CRM-044).** The **trespassory taking and carrying away** of the **tangible personal property of another** with the **intent to permanently deprive** the owner of it.

| Element | Notes |
|---|---|
| Trespassory | Without consent. Consent obtained by fraud to **possession** only is larceny by trick (R-CRM-046) |
| Taking (caption) | Gaining control. An employee with mere **custody** (cashier, low-level employee using employer's property) who takes the item commits larceny, not embezzlement |
| Carrying away (asportation) | Any slight movement |
| Personal property | Not real property, services or intangibles at common law; modern statutes expand |
| Of another | Someone with a superior possessory interest (an owner can commit larceny of his own property from a bailee with a lien) |
| Intent to permanently deprive | At the time of the taking. Intent to borrow is not enough, unless the defendant intends to keep the property for an unreasonable time or to expose it to a substantial risk of loss (pawning, abandoning) |

**Continuing trespass.** If the defendant takes property wrongfully (trespassorily) without intent to steal and later forms the intent, the trespass "continues" and the later intent completes larceny. If the original taking was **not** wrongful (borrowing with permission), a later decision to keep it is not larceny (it may be embezzlement).

**Claim of right.** A good-faith belief that the property is one's own, or (in many jurisdictions) taking to satisfy a debt of a certain amount, negates intent to steal. Intent to repay **equivalent money** later is generally not a defense.

**Lost and mislaid property.** A finder commits larceny if, at the time of finding, he intends to keep it and knows or has reason to know how to find the owner.

**Embezzlement (R-CRM-045).** The **fraudulent conversion** of the **property of another** by a person **in lawful possession** of it. Conversion = serious interference with the owner's rights (selling, spending, keeping). Intent to restore **the exact property** is a defense; intent to repay with equivalent money is not (unless the property was money and the defendant had authority to use it). Typical defendants: trustees, agents, bank tellers handling deposits in their possession, bailees.

**False pretenses (R-CRM-046).** Obtaining **title** to property of another by a knowing **false representation** of a past or present **material fact**, with **intent to defraud**, which **causes** the victim to pass title.
- **Title vs. possession** is the line between false pretenses and **larceny by trick** (fraud obtaining possession only, such as "renting" a car never intended to be returned).
- A **false promise** about future conduct was not false pretenses at common law; the MPC includes it but does not let the jury infer deception from mere non-performance (MPC § 223.3(1)).
- The victim must actually be deceived (if not, attempt only).

**Consolidation and grading (R-CRM-047).** Modern codes merge larceny, embezzlement, false pretenses and related offenses into a single crime of **theft** (MPC § 223.1; Cal. Pen. Code § 484(a)). Grading turns on value: in California, theft of property worth more than $950 is generally grand theft (Pen. Code § 487), and Proposition 47 (2014) made most thefts of $950 or less petty theft (§ 490.2). Proposition 36 (2024) allows the value of property taken in multiple thefts to be **aggregated** to reach the felony threshold (§ 490.3) and makes petty theft or shoplifting with two prior theft-related convictions chargeable as a felony (§ 666.1) [S-362] [S-363] [VERIFY V-257].

**Receiving stolen property (R-CRM-048).** (1) Receiving **possession or control** of (2) **stolen** property (3) **knowing** it is stolen (4) with **intent to permanently deprive** the owner.
- Knowledge: actual knowledge or belief; some courts accept willful blindness. Mere suspicion or negligence is not enough.
- "Stolen" at the time of receipt: if police have **recovered** the goods and use them in a sting, they are no longer stolen. The defendant cannot be convicted of receiving but can be convicted of **attempted** receipt under the modern view, which rejects the "legal impossibility" defense (R-CRM-062; California: *People v. Rojas* (1961) 55 Cal.2d 252 [VERIFY V-251]). The Feb 2013 essay (stolen paintings, a copy, cooperating sons) turned on this.
- California: Pen. Code § 496.

**Comparison of theft crimes.**

| Crime | How property obtained | What victim gives up | Mental state |
|---|---|---|---|
| Larceny | Trespassory taking | Possession, no consent | Intent to permanently deprive at taking |
| Larceny by trick | Fraud | Possession only | Same |
| False pretenses | Fraud (false fact) | Title | Intent to defraud |
| Embezzlement | Already in lawful possession | — | Fraudulent conversion |
| Robbery | Larceny + force or fear from person or presence | Possession | Intent to permanently deprive |
| Extortion | Threat of future harm | Property | Intent to obtain property |
| Receiving | Receipt of stolen goods | — | Knowledge goods stolen |

**MBE vs. CA essay.** MBE: the common-law distinctions are tested constantly (custody vs. possession; title vs. possession). Essay: name the common-law crime precisely, then note that modern statutes consolidate theft.

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: PRC law separates theft (盗窃), fraud (诈骗) and embezzlement-type offenses (职务侵占/侵占) much like the common law, but grades them by "amount relatively large" thresholds set by judicial interpretation (approx.).

### II.B Robbery

**Rule (R-CRM-049).** Robbery is (1) a **larceny** (2) of property taken **from the victim's person or presence** (3) by **force or intimidation** (threat of immediate harm).

- **Force:** more than the force needed to move the property. A pickpocketing done without the victim's awareness is larceny, not robbery; a purse snatch that requires a struggle or overcomes resistance is robbery.
- **Intimidation:** a threat of **immediate** bodily harm to the victim, a family member or someone present. A toy gun or a bluff suffices if the victim is actually put in fear (Jul 2019 Q3).
- **Presence:** within the victim's reach or control, so that the victim could have prevented the taking but for the force or fear (a safe in the next room).
- **Timing:** at common law, the force or fear must be used to obtain the property, before or during the taking. Many modern statutes and California treat force used to **retain** the property or escape as robbery (*People v. Estes* (1983) 147 Cal.App.3d 23; *People v. Gomez* (2008) 43 Cal.4th 249) [VERIFY V-251].
- **Claim of right:** a good-faith claim to the specific property negates robbery in many jurisdictions.
- **Aggravated (armed) robbery:** with a deadly weapon; jurisdictions split on whether an unloaded or toy gun qualifies.
- California: Pen. Code § 211 ("felonious taking of personal property in the possession of another, from his person or immediate presence, and against his will, accomplished by means of force or fear"); carjacking (§ 215) is a separate offense.

**Extortion.** At common law, the corrupt collection of an unlawful fee by an officer. Modern extortion (blackmail) is obtaining property by threat of **future** harm (physical harm, exposure of a secret, accusation of a crime, damage to property); the property need not be obtained in the victim's presence. Some statutes complete the crime upon the threat. **Robbery = immediate threat; extortion = future threat.**

**Robbery is a lesser-included offense check.** Robbery contains larceny and assault; a defendant convicted of robbery cannot also be convicted of the included larceny for the same taking (double jeopardy, R-CRM-116).

### II.C Burglary

**Common-law burglary (R-CRM-050).** The (1) **breaking** and (2) **entering** of (3) the **dwelling** (4) **of another** (5) at **nighttime** (6) with **intent to commit a felony therein**.
- **Breaking:** actual (creating or enlarging an opening by even slight force: opening a closed but unlocked door or window) or constructive (entry by fraud, threat, or through a co-conspirator inside). Walking through an open door is not a breaking. Breaking out of a structure is not burglary at common law.
- **Entering:** any part of the body, or an instrument used to commit the felony (not merely to break in), crossing the threshold.
- **Dwelling:** a place regularly used for sleeping, even if temporarily unoccupied; includes structures within the curtilage.
- **Of another:** occupancy, not ownership, controls (a landlord can burglarize a tenant's apartment).
- **Nighttime:** between sunset and sunrise when a face cannot be discerned by natural light.
- **Intent** to commit a felony (or larceny) inside **at the time of entry**. Intent formed after entry is not burglary. The intended felony need not be completed.

**Modern statutes and MPC (R-CRM-051).** Most statutes drop the breaking, dwelling and nighttime elements, cover any building or structure (and often vehicles), and require intent to commit **any felony or theft**. Entry into premises open to the public during business hours is often excluded or treated as a lesser crime (MPC § 221.1: entry into a building or occupied structure with purpose to commit a crime therein, unless the premises are open to the public or the actor is licensed or privileged to enter).

**California.** Burglary is entry into a house, room, shop, store, vehicle (when locked) and other listed structures with intent to commit grand or petit larceny or any felony (Pen. Code § 459); no breaking or nighttime is required. Burglary of an inhabited dwelling is first degree (§ 460). Under Proposition 47, entering an open commercial establishment during regular business hours with intent to commit larceny of property worth $950 or less is **shoplifting** (a misdemeanor) and must be charged as such rather than as burglary (§ 459.5) [S-368].

| Element | Common law | Modern majority / MPC | California |
|---|---|---|---|
| Breaking | Required | Not required | Not required |
| Structure | Dwelling of another | Any building or occupied structure | Many listed structures, incl. locked vehicles |
| Time | Nighttime | Any time | Any time |
| Intent | Felony inside | Felony or theft (MPC: any crime) | Larceny or any felony |

**Burglary does not merge** with the felony committed inside: a defendant can be convicted of both burglary and the target crime.

**MBE vs. CA essay.** MBE: common-law elements unless a statute is given; the "intent at time of entry" and "breaking" elements are the usual traps. Essay: state the common-law elements and the modern relaxation; analyze intent at entry carefully (Oct 2020 Q4: house entered at night to steal; Jul 2023 Q4: breaking a garage window to shelter from extreme cold, where the intent element and a necessity defense both arise).

### II.D Assault and battery

**Battery (R-CRM-053).** The **unlawful application of force** to the person of another resulting in **bodily injury or an offensive touching**. It is a general-intent crime: intentional, reckless or (in many jurisdictions) criminally negligent conduct suffices. The force can be indirect (setting a dog on someone, poisoning).

**Assault (R-CRM-052).** Either (1) an **attempted battery** (a specific-intent crime: intent to commit battery plus an act beyond preparation), or (2) intentionally placing another in **reasonable apprehension of imminent bodily harm** (the tort-style assault). Words alone are not enough for the second form unless accompanied by conduct. For attempted-battery assault, the victim need not be aware; for apprehension assault, the victim must be aware.

**Aggravated assault and battery.** Felony versions involve a **deadly weapon**, **serious bodily injury**, intent to commit a further felony (assault with intent to kill or rape), or a protected victim (police officer, child). MPC § 211.1 grades simple and aggravated assault by injury and weapon.

**California.** Assault is "an unlawful attempt, coupled with a present ability, to commit a violent injury on the person of another" (Pen. Code § 240): only the attempted-battery form, and it requires **present ability**. The mental state is actual knowledge of facts sufficient to establish that the act by its nature will probably and directly result in a battery; specific intent to injure is not required (*People v. Williams* (2001) 26 Cal.4th 779). Battery is "any willful and unlawful use of force or violence upon the person of another" (§ 242). Assault with a deadly weapon or by force likely to produce great bodily injury is § 245.

**Mayhem** (common law): malicious dismemberment or disabling of a bodily member; modern statutes include permanent disfigurement.

**MBE vs. CA essay.** MBE: know both forms of assault and which requires the victim's awareness. Essay: charge battery, assault (both theories where facts fit), and the aggravated forms; general principles control.

### II.E Rape; statutory rape

**Rape (R-CRM-054).** At common law: unlawful sexual intercourse by a man with a woman not his wife, by **force** and **without her consent**. Modern statutes:
- are **gender-neutral** and define the act more broadly (sexual penetration);
- have **abolished the marital exemption** (California consolidated spousal rape into the general rape statute; see ca-distinctions [VERIFY V-258]);
- treat lack of consent as the core: many require only intercourse without consent; others still require force beyond the act itself or a threat;
- cover victims **incapable of consent** (unconscious, asleep, intoxicated to incapacity, mentally incapable) and intercourse procured by threats.

**Consent obtained by fraud.** Fraud **in the factum** (the victim does not know the act is sexual intercourse — "medical procedure") vitiates consent: rape. Fraud **in the inducement** (lying about wealth or love) generally does not, except where a statute covers specific frauds (for example, impersonating a spouse or partner).

**Mistake as to consent.** Rape is a general-intent crime; many jurisdictions allow an **honest and reasonable** mistake of fact about consent as a defense (California: *People v. Mayberry* (1975) 15 Cal.3d 143); an unreasonable belief is no defense.

**Statutory rape (R-CRM-055).** Sexual intercourse with a person under the age of consent (set by statute). It is a **strict-liability** crime in most jurisdictions: the victim's consent and a reasonable mistake about age are not defenses.

| Issue | Majority | MPC | California |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mistake of age | No defense (strict liability) | Defense if the critical age is above 10 and the actor proves by a preponderance that he reasonably believed the child was older (MPC § 213.6(1)) | Reasonable, good-faith mistake of age is a defense to unlawful sexual intercourse (Pen. Code § 261.5; *People v. Hernandez* (1964) 61 Cal.2d 529), but not to lewd acts with a child under 14 (*People v. Olsen* (1984) 36 Cal.3d 638) |

**Protected-class rule.** The underage victim cannot be convicted as an accomplice or co-conspirator to her own statutory rape (R-CRM-071; *Gebardi v. United States*, 287 U.S. 112 (1932), applies the same idea to the Mann Act).

### II.F Kidnapping

**Kidnapping (R-CRM-056).** At common law, the forcible abduction or stealing away of a person from his own country to another. Modern statutes: the **unlawful confinement** of a person **against his will** coupled with either (1) **moving** the person (asportation) or (2) **concealing** the person in a secret place.

**False imprisonment** is the lesser offense: unlawful confinement of a person without consent, with no movement or concealment required (California: Pen. Code § 236).

**Aggravated kidnapping (R-CRM-057).** Statutes increase the grade when the kidnapping is for **ransom**, to commit another felony (robbery, rape), to inflict injury or terrorize, to interfere with a governmental function, or when the victim is a **child**. MPC § 212.1: unlawfully removing a person from his residence or business or a substantial distance from where he is found, or confining him for a substantial period in a place of isolation, with a listed purpose (ransom or hostage; to facilitate a felony or flight; to inflict bodily injury or terrorize; to interfere with a governmental or political function).

**Incidental-movement limit.** Movement that is merely **incidental** to another crime (moving a robbery victim from the counter to the back room) is not kidnapping in many jurisdictions; the movement must be substantial and have independent significance, such as increasing the risk of harm or making detection less likely. California asks, for simple kidnapping, whether the movement was substantial in distance under the totality of circumstances, including increased risk of harm (Pen. Code § 207; *People v. Martinez* (1999) 20 Cal.4th 225), and for aggravated kidnapping whether the movement was more than incidental and substantially increased the risk of harm (§ 209(b); *People v. Daniels* (1969) 71 Cal.2d 1119).

**Consent and age.** Consent by a competent adult defeats kidnapping; consent obtained by fraud or force does not. A child's consent is generally ineffective; taking a child without a parent's consent can be kidnapping.

**Essay pattern.** Feb 2018 Q4 asked whether a defendant could be convicted of **attempted kidnapping**: combine the attempt analysis (specific intent to kidnap; substantial step or dangerous proximity) with the kidnapping elements.

### II.G Arson

**Common-law arson (R-CRM-058).** The **malicious burning** of the **dwelling of another**.
- **Burning:** some charring of the structure's material; scorching or smoke discoloration is not enough. Damage by explosion was not arson at common law unless a fire resulted.
- **Malice:** intent to burn or **reckless disregard** of an obvious risk that the structure will burn. Arson is not a specific-intent crime; voluntary intoxication is no defense (California: *People v. Atkins* (2001) 25 Cal.4th 76).
- **Dwelling of another:** includes outbuildings within the curtilage; occupancy, not title, controls. Burning one's own house was not arson at common law.

**Modern statutes and MPC.** Statutes extend arson to any **structure** (and often personal property and forest land), to burning one's own property to defraud an insurer, and to damage by explosion. MPC § 220.1: starting a fire or causing an explosion with purpose of destroying a building or occupied structure of another, or of damaging any property to collect insurance; reckless burning is a lesser offense.

**California.** Arson is willfully and maliciously setting fire to or burning (or causing to be burned) any structure, forest land or property (Pen. Code § 451); recklessly causing a fire is a separate, lesser crime (§ 452) [VERIFY V-260].

**Essay pattern (Jul 2023 Q4 type).** A defendant starts a fire for warmth inside a building she entered unlawfully; a spark later ignites oil. Analyze: burning (charring?); malice (intent or recklessness vs. mere negligence — negligence supports at most reckless/negligent burning statutes); "dwelling of another" (a garage within the curtilage); and necessity for the entry. Arson and burglary are separate crimes and do not merge.

### II.H Possession offenses

**Possession as the actus reus (R-CRM-059).** Possession is a sufficient act if the possessor **knowingly procured or received** the thing or was **aware of control** of it for a sufficient period to have been able to terminate possession (MPC § 2.01(4)).

- **Actual possession:** on the person. **Constructive possession:** dominion and control over the item or the place where it is found (a car, a room). Possession can be **joint**.
- **Knowledge:** the defendant must know of the item's presence and, in most jurisdictions, its illicit nature (knowing it is a drug, though not necessarily which drug). Mere proximity or presence where contraband is found is not enough.
- **Statutory presumptions** (for example, that all occupants of a car possess a gun found in it) are valid only as **permissive** inferences that are rational on the facts (*County Court of Ulster County v. Allen*, 442 U.S. 140 (1979)) (R-CRM-119).
- **Strict-liability possession statutes** are disfavored for serious crimes; courts read in a knowledge requirement (*Staples v. United States*, 511 U.S. 600 (1994): knowledge that a weapon had the characteristics bringing it within the statute). For federal felon-in-possession, the government must prove the defendant knew he belonged to the prohibited category (*Rehaif v. United States*, 588 U.S. 225 (2019)).
- **Second Amendment limits** on possession crimes are a Constitutional Law topic; see `03-subjects/constitutional-law/outline.md` (for example, an as-applied ruling on a drug-user firearm ban, *United States v. Hemani* (2026) [VERIFY V-250]).

**Essay pattern.** Drugs found in a car trunk (Jul 2021 Q4) or in a shared apartment: possession questions usually sit behind a Fourth Amendment suppression call; analyze standing and the search first, then (if asked) knowing possession.

## III Inchoate crimes; parties

Inchoate ("incomplete") crimes punish steps toward a crime: **attempt**, **solicitation** and **conspiracy**. Parties doctrine decides who besides the actual perpetrator is liable for a completed crime. California essays have tested attempt (3 times), conspiracy (twice) and accomplice liability; on the MBE these are among the most heavily tested topics in categories I–IV.

### III.A Inchoate offenses (State Bar essay outline III: Inchoate offenses)

**Merger summary (R-CRM-068).**

| Inchoate crime | Merges into the completed crime? | Can be punished with the completed crime? |
|---|---|---|
| Attempt | Yes | No |
| Solicitation | Yes (and into conspiracy if the person solicited agrees) | No |
| Conspiracy | **No** | **Yes**: conspiracy and the completed crime are separate offenses (*Callanan v. United States*, 364 U.S. 587 (1961)) |

The MPC, by contrast, generally bars conviction of both conspiracy and the completed object crime (MPC § 1.07(1)(b)).

#### III.A.1 Attempts

**Rule.** Attempt requires (1) the **specific intent** to commit the target crime and (2) an **act beyond mere preparation** toward its commission (R-CRM-060, R-CRM-061).

**Mental state (R-CRM-060).**
- Attempt is always a specific-intent crime, even if the target crime is not: attempted murder requires **intent to kill**; intent to inflict serious bodily injury or depraved-heart recklessness is not enough.
- There is no attempt to commit a crime defined by recklessness or negligence (no "attempted involuntary manslaughter") and no attempted felony murder.
- For attendant circumstances, the MPC requires only the culpability required for the completed crime (MPC § 5.01(1)); so attempted statutory rape may be strict liability as to age where the completed crime is.

**Act (R-CRM-061).**

| Test | Rule | Authority |
|---|---|---|
| Dangerous proximity (common law) | Conduct so near completion that, but for interruption, the crime would have been committed | *People v. Rizzo*, 246 N.Y. 334 (1927) (defendants driving around looking for a payroll clerk they never found: no attempt) |
| Equivocality / res ipsa | Conduct that unequivocally shows criminal intent on its own | Older approach |
| Substantial step (MPC; modern majority; federal courts) | An act or omission constituting a substantial step in a course of conduct planned to culminate in the crime, **strongly corroborative** of criminal purpose | MPC § 5.01(1)(c), (2) |
| California | Specific intent plus a direct but ineffectual act toward commission, going beyond preparation; slight acts suffice once the design is clear | Pen. Code § 21a; *People v. Superior Court (Decker)* (2007) 41 Cal.4th 1 [VERIFY V-251] |

MPC § 5.01(2) lists conduct that may be a substantial step: lying in wait or following the victim; enticing the victim to the scene; reconnoitering the place; unlawfully entering the structure; possessing materials specially designed for the crime; possessing or collecting materials at or near the scene; soliciting an innocent agent.

**MBE vs. CA essay.** MBE: use the substantial-step test unless the question says common law. Essay: state both tests and apply each (Feb 2018 Q4 attempted kidnapping; Feb 2022 Q1 attempted murder of a witness by shooting and missing).

**Impossibility (R-CRM-062).**

| Type | Example | Common law | Modern / MPC |
|---|---|---|---|
| Factual impossibility | Pickpocket reaches into an empty pocket; shoots at a bed where victim is not | No defense | No defense |
| "Legal" (hybrid) impossibility | Receiving goods the defendant thinks are stolen but are not (police recovered them); shooting a corpse believed alive | Traditionally a defense (*People v. Jaffe*, 185 N.Y. 497 (1906)) | **No defense**: guilt judged on the circumstances as the defendant believed them (MPC § 5.01(1)(a)); most jurisdictions |
| Pure legal impossibility | Doing something the defendant thinks is a crime but is not (bringing in "contraband" that is legal) | Defense | Defense (legality principle) |

**Abandonment (R-CRM-063).** At common law, once the attempt is complete (the act beyond preparation has occurred), abandonment is no defense. Under the MPC (and some states), **complete and voluntary renunciation** of criminal purpose is an affirmative defense if the defendant abandons the effort or prevents the crime; it is not voluntary if motivated by an increased risk of detection or a decision to postpone or pick another victim (MPC § 5.01(4)).

**Punishment.** Attempt is usually punished less severely than the completed crime (California: generally one-half the term, Pen. Code § 664). The MPC grades attempt the same as the completed crime except for capital crimes and first-degree felonies (MPC § 5.05(1)).

#### III.A.2 Conspiracy

**Rule.** Conspiracy is (1) an **agreement** between two or more persons, (2) the **intent to agree**, (3) the **intent to achieve the unlawful objective**, and (4) in most jurisdictions, an **overt act** in furtherance by any conspirator (R-CRM-065, R-CRM-066).

**Agreement.** It may be inferred from concerted action; no formal or written agreement is needed. Mere knowledge, presence or association is not enough.

**Bilateral vs. unilateral.**

| Issue | Common law (bilateral) | MPC and modern majority (unilateral) |
|---|---|---|
| Agreement with an undercover officer or a feigning partner | No conspiracy: two guilty minds required | Conspiracy: the defendant agreed (MPC § 5.03(1)) |
| All other alleged co-conspirators acquitted in the same trial | Remaining defendant cannot be convicted | Remaining defendant may be convicted |
| Federal law | Generally bilateral: no conspiracy with only a government agent | — |

**Overt act.** Not required at common law (the agreement was the crime). Required by the federal general conspiracy statute (18 U.S.C. § 371), California (Pen. Code § 184) and most states. Any act in furtherance by any conspirator suffices, even a lawful act (a phone call, renting a car). The MPC requires an overt act except for conspiracies to commit first- or second-degree felonies (MPC § 5.03(5)).

**Mental state (R-CRM-066).** Conspiracy is a specific-intent crime: intent to agree and intent that the object crime be committed. There can be no conspiracy to commit a crime of recklessness or negligence (no conspiracy to commit involuntary manslaughter; California holds there is no conspiracy to commit implied-malice murder, *People v. Swain* (1996) 12 Cal.4th 593 [VERIFY V-251]).

**Suppliers.** A seller of lawful goods who knows the buyer will use them for a crime is a conspirator only if he **intends** to further the crime; intent may be inferred from a **stake in the venture**: inflated prices, goods with no legitimate use, a large share of business with the criminal, or (for serious crimes) knowledge alone in some courts (*United States v. Falcone*, 311 U.S. 205 (1940); *Direct Sales Co. v. United States*, 319 U.S. 703 (1943); *People v. Lauria* (1967) 251 Cal.App.2d 471).

**Pinkerton liability (R-CRM-067).** Each conspirator is liable for crimes committed by co-conspirators that are (1) **in furtherance** of the conspiracy and (2) **reasonably foreseeable** as a natural consequence of it, even if the conspirator did not participate (*Pinkerton v. United States*, 328 U.S. 640 (1946)). The MPC rejects Pinkerton; a conspirator is liable for other crimes only as an accomplice (MPC § 2.06).

**Scope and structure.** One agreement with multiple criminal objects is one conspiracy (*Braverman v. United States*, 317 U.S. 49 (1942); MPC § 5.03(3)). In a **chain** (manufacturer → wholesaler → retailer) members may be part of one conspiracy because each depends on the others; in a **hub-and-spoke** ("wheel") structure, spokes who deal only with the hub and have no common interest form separate conspiracies (*Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750 (1946)).

**Defenses and limits (R-CRM-068).**
- **Withdrawal:** at common law, no withdrawal from the conspiracy itself once the agreement is made (in jurisdictions with an overt-act requirement, withdrawal before any overt act may avoid liability). Withdrawal does cut off **Pinkerton** liability for co-conspirators' later crimes if the conspirator takes an affirmative act: timely notice to **every** co-conspirator, or notice to law enforcement. The MPC allows renunciation as a complete defense only if the defendant **thwarts** the conspiracy's success (MPC § 5.03(6)). Once a defendant raises withdrawal, he may bear the burden of proving it (*Smith v. United States*, 568 U.S. 106 (2013)).
- **Impossibility** is no defense.
- **Wharton's rule:** where the substantive crime requires two participants (adultery, bigamy, dueling, bribery between briber and official), there is no conspiracy unless more than the necessary number agree (*Iannelli v. United States*, 420 U.S. 770 (1975)).
- **Protected class:** a person the statute protects (the minor in statutory rape; the woman transported under the Mann Act) cannot be a conspirator to that crime, and if the only other party is protected, there is no conspiracy (*Gebardi v. United States*, 287 U.S. 112 (1932)).
- **Duration:** the conspiracy ends when its objectives are achieved or abandoned; acts of concealment after the objective is achieved are not part of it unless concealment was itself agreed to. A conspiracy does not end merely because police have secretly defeated its object (*United States v. Jimenez Recio*, 537 U.S. 270 (2003)).
- **Spousal immunity** (husband and wife cannot conspire) is abolished almost everywhere.

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: PRC law punishes joint crime (共同犯罪) and preparatory acts, but has no general stand-alone "conspiracy" offense separate from the completed crime; US conspiracy is a separate crime even when the object is achieved (approx.).

#### III.A.3 Solicitation

**Rule (R-CRM-064).** Solicitation is **asking, inducing, encouraging or commanding** another person to commit a crime, with the **intent that the person commit it**. The crime is complete when the request is made; the other person need not agree or act.

- **Merger:** if the person solicited agrees, solicitation merges into conspiracy; if the crime is committed, the solicitor is an accomplice and the solicitation merges.
- **Defenses:** impossibility is no defense; the solicited person's refusal is no defense. At common law, withdrawal is no defense once the request is made; the MPC allows a defense of complete and voluntary renunciation that **prevents** the crime (MPC § 5.02(3)).
- **Uncommunicated solicitation:** under the MPC, it is still solicitation if the actor's conduct was designed to communicate the request (a letter intercepted) (§ 5.02(2)).
- **The solicited party** who refuses commits no crime. A person who solicits an undercover officer is guilty of solicitation (and, in unilateral jurisdictions, of nothing more until an agreement).
- California: solicitation of specified serious crimes is a separate offense (Pen. Code § 653f).

**Essay pattern (Feb 2017 Q6 type).** A spouse offers money to an informant to find a hit man: solicitation of murder (and, if the informant feigns agreement, a bilateral/unilateral conspiracy question), plus entrapment.

### III.B Parties to crime

**Common-law categories and modern merger.**

| Common-law label | Who | Modern treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Principal in the first degree | Actually commits the crime (or uses an innocent agent) | Principal |
| Principal in the second degree | Aids or abets, present actually or constructively (lookout, getaway driver) | Accomplice, liable as a principal |
| Accessory before the fact | Aids or counsels before the crime, not present | Accomplice, liable as a principal |
| Accessory after the fact | Knowingly helps a felon avoid arrest after the crime | Separate, lesser crime (obstruction) |

Federal law (18 U.S.C. § 2) and California (Pen. Code § 31) treat everyone who aids and abets as a principal; California treats accessories after the fact separately (Pen. Code § 32).

**Accomplice liability — elements (R-CRM-069).** A person is liable as an accomplice if he (1) **aided, counseled, encouraged or induced** the principal (2) with the **intent to aid** and the **intent that the principal commit the crime** (the "dual intent").
- **Act:** any aid or encouragement, including words, supplying tools, acting as lookout or getaway driver, or a prior promise to help. **Mere presence**, even with approval, is not enough unless the defendant had a duty to act or his presence was pre-arranged encouragement.
- **Mental state:** the majority (and MPC § 2.06(3)) require **purpose** to promote or facilitate the crime. Knowledge that one's help will be used is not enough in most jurisdictions (a vendor of ordinary goods), though some courts accept knowledge for serious crimes. For an armed offense, the accomplice must have had advance knowledge of the weapon (*Rosemond v. United States*, 572 U.S. 65 (2014)).
- **Crimes of recklessness or negligence:** an accomplice who intentionally aids the conduct is liable if he had the culpability required for the result (MPC § 2.06(4)) (a passenger urging a driver to race, killing a pedestrian).
- **Principal's conviction not required:** an accomplice can be convicted even if the principal is acquitted or not prosecuted (*Standefer v. United States*, 447 U.S. 10 (1980)), though the prosecution must still prove that the crime was committed.

**Scope (R-CRM-070).** An accomplice is liable for the crime he intended to aid **and** (under the common-law **natural and probable consequences** doctrine) any other crime by the principal that was a foreseeable result of the intended crime. The MPC rejects the foreseeability extension (liability only for crimes the accomplice purposely aided). California no longer allows the natural and probable consequences doctrine to support **murder** (*People v. Chiu* (2014) 59 Cal.4th 155 for first-degree premeditated murder; Pen. Code § 188(a)(3) and *People v. Gentile* (2020) for second-degree murder [S-359] [VERIFY V-251]) or attempted murder (SB 775, see § 1172.6 [S-360]).

**Limits and withdrawal (R-CRM-071).**
- **Withdrawal** before the crime becomes unstoppable: (1) repudiate encouragement; (2) neutralize or retrieve material aid; or (3) give timely warning to police or otherwise make a proper effort to prevent the crime (MPC § 2.06(6)(c)).
- **Protected-class victims** and those whose conduct is inevitably incident to the crime (the buyer in an illegal sale, in some statutes) are not accomplices (MPC § 2.06(6)(a)–(b)).
- **Feigned accomplice:** a person who appears to help but intends to frustrate the crime (an undercover officer) is not an accomplice.
- **Accessory after the fact:** (1) a completed felony; (2) knowledge of it; (3) aid to the felon (2) with intent to help him avoid arrest, trial or punishment. Punished less severely; some statutes exempt close relatives. **Misprision** (failure to report) is not generally a crime; federal misprision requires active concealment (18 U.S.C. § 4).

**Essay pattern.** For multiple-defendant calls (Feb 2013 Q1: four defendants; Oct 2020 Q4: two burglars, one of whom took a wallet), analyze each defendant separately: own acts → conspiracy → Pinkerton → accomplice liability → defenses.

## IV General principles (State Bar essay outline I: General principles)

The State Bar's essay outline opens with general principles: sources of law, actus reus, mens rea, causation, parties and scope of liability, withdrawal and abandonment (CRM-CBX-I; wording paraphrased [VERIFY V-254]). This category covers the building blocks of every crime.

**Sources of law and legality (R-CRM-001).** There are no federal common-law crimes; federal crimes are statutory. States define crimes by statute, using common-law terms that courts interpret by their common-law meaning. **Legality** principles: no punishment without a pre-existing law (Ex Post Facto Clauses, U.S. Const. art. I, §§ 9–10; *Rogers v. Tennessee*, 532 U.S. 451 (2001) for judicial enlargement); statutes must give fair notice and must not invite arbitrary enforcement (void for vagueness); the **rule of lenity** resolves genuine ambiguity in a criminal statute in the defendant's favor.

### IV.A Acts and omissions

**Voluntary act (R-CRM-002).** Criminal liability requires conduct that includes a **voluntary act** (or an omission to perform an act the person was physically capable of performing). Involuntary movements are not acts: reflexes and convulsions, movements during unconsciousness or sleep, conduct under hypnosis, or movements not the product of the actor's effort or determination (MPC § 2.01(1)–(2)).
- **Prior voluntary act:** liability may rest on an earlier voluntary act. A driver who knows he is subject to seizures and drives anyway is liable when a seizure causes a crash (*People v. Decina*, 2 N.Y.2d 133 (1956)).
- **Status crimes:** a state cannot punish a person for a status (being an addict) without any act (*Robinson v. California*, 370 U.S. 660 (1962)); it may punish conduct such as public drunkenness (*Powell v. Texas*, 392 U.S. 514 (1968)) and generally applicable public-camping rules (*City of Grants Pass v. Johnson* (2024) [S-366]) (R-CRM-118).
- **Thoughts alone** are never punished; speech can be an act (solicitation, threats).
- **Possession** is an act if knowing (II.H).

**Omissions (R-CRM-003).** A failure to act is a crime only if (1) there is a **legal duty** to act, (2) the defendant **knew** the facts giving rise to the duty, and (3) it was **reasonably possible** to perform. A moral duty is not enough; there is no general duty to rescue a stranger.

**Sources of a legal duty:**
1. **Statute** (filing a tax return; reporting an accident; mandated reporters).
2. **Contract** (a lifeguard, nurse or caregiver).
3. **Status relationship** (parent to minor child; spouse to spouse; employer to employee in some contexts).
4. **Voluntary assumption of care**, especially where the defendant secludes the victim from other help.
5. **Creation of the peril** by the defendant (even innocently).
6. **Duty to control** others (parent over child; employer over employee) and landowner duties to invitees in some jurisdictions.

*Jones v. United States*, 308 F.2d 307 (D.C. Cir. 1962), lists the four classic duty situations (statute, status, contract, voluntary assumption with seclusion).

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: PRC theory also recognizes "omission" crimes (不作为犯罪) grounded in legal, contractual, professional or prior-conduct duties; the categories are close (approx.).

### IV.B State of mind

#### IV.B.1 Required mental state

**Common-law categories (R-CRM-004).**

| Category | Meaning | Crimes (common law) | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|---|
| Specific intent | An intent or objective beyond the act itself | Solicitation, conspiracy, attempt; first-degree premeditated murder; assault (attempted battery); larceny, robbery, burglary, embezzlement, false pretenses, forgery | Voluntary intoxication and **any** honest mistake of fact (even unreasonable) can negate it |
| Malice | Intent or reckless disregard of a known risk | Common-law murder; arson | Treated like general intent: intoxication no defense; mistake must be reasonable |
| General intent | Intent to do the act (awareness of the factors constituting the crime) | Battery, rape, kidnapping, false imprisonment | Only a reasonable mistake of fact is a defense; voluntary intoxication is not |
| Strict liability | No mental state for one or more elements | Public-welfare offenses; statutory rape (majority); selling liquor to minors | Mistake of fact is no defense |

**MPC culpability (R-CRM-005).**

| Level | Result / conduct element | Attendant-circumstance element |
|---|---|---|
| **Purposely** | Conscious object to engage in the conduct or cause the result | Aware of the circumstances, or believes or hopes they exist |
| **Knowingly** | Aware the conduct is of that nature; practically certain it will cause the result | Aware the circumstances exist (or aware of a high probability, unless he actually believes they do not — "willful blindness," § 2.02(7)) |
| **Recklessly** | Consciously disregards a substantial and unjustifiable risk; a gross deviation from the standard of a law-abiding person | Same |
| **Negligently** | Should be aware of a substantial and unjustifiable risk; a gross deviation from the standard of a reasonable person | Same |

**MPC rules of construction:**
- **Element analysis:** culpability is assessed separately for each material element (§ 2.02(1)).
- **Default:** if a statute is silent, at least **recklessness** is required (§ 2.02(3)).
- **Distribution:** a culpability term stated without distinguishing among elements applies to **all** material elements unless a contrary purpose plainly appears (§ 2.02(4)).
- **Higher satisfies lower:** proof of purpose satisfies knowledge, recklessness or negligence (§ 2.02(5)).
- **Willful blindness** satisfies knowledge (§ 2.02(7); *Global-Tech Appliances, Inc. v. SEB S.A.*, 563 U.S. 754 (2011) describes the doctrine).

**Transferred intent (R-CRM-008).** If the defendant intends harm to one person and causes the same kind of harm to another, the intent transfers (D shoots at A, misses and kills B: murder of B, plus attempted murder of A). It does not transfer across kinds of harm (intent to break a window does not supply intent to injure a person) and does not apply to attempt against the unintended victim. MPC § 2.03(2)(a).

**Concurrence (R-CRM-009).** The mental state must **concur** with the act that causes the harm: the intent must actuate the act. A defendant who plans to kill V tomorrow but kills him accidentally today is not guilty of murder. If the defendant, believing V dead after an attack, disposes of the "body" and V dies from the disposal, most courts still convict (the series of acts is treated as one transaction). California: "In every crime or public offense there must exist a union, or joint operation of act and intent, or criminal negligence" (Pen. Code § 20).

**Vicarious and corporate liability (R-CRM-007).** Vicarious liability (an employer punished for an employee's act without personal fault) is permitted for regulatory offenses with light penalties; imprisonment on a purely vicarious basis raises due-process concerns. A **responsible corporate officer** may be liable for public-welfare violations he had authority to prevent (*United States v. Park*, 421 U.S. 658 (1975); *United States v. Dotterweich*, 320 U.S. 277 (1943)). A **corporation** is liable for acts of agents within the scope of employment (federal *respondeat superior*); the MPC limits corporate liability for true crimes to offenses authorized, commanded or recklessly tolerated by the board or a high managerial agent (MPC § 2.07).

**MBE vs. CA essay.** MBE: expect an MPC culpability question at least occasionally ("which mental state does the statute require?"), and common-law specific vs. general intent for intoxication and mistake. Essay: name the mental state for each element you analyze.

#### IV.B.2 Strict liability

**Rule (R-CRM-006).** A strict-liability offense requires no culpable mental state as to one or more elements. Courts find strict liability mainly for **public-welfare (regulatory) offenses**: light penalties, conduct a reasonable person would know is regulated, and a danger to the public (adulterated food or drugs, traffic and liquor violations). Statutory rape and bigamy are traditional exceptions with heavier penalties.

**Presumption against strict liability.** For offenses derived from the common law, or with serious penalties, courts presume Congress or the legislature intended a mens rea requirement, even if the statute is silent (*Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246 (1952): conversion of government property; *Staples v. United States*, 511 U.S. 600 (1994); *Elonis v. United States*, 575 U.S. 723 (2015): threats require more than negligence; *Rehaif v. United States*, 588 U.S. 225 (2019)). True threats require at least recklessness under the First Amendment (*Counterman v. Colorado* (2023) [VERIFY V-035]).

**MPC.** Strict liability is limited to **violations** (non-criminal offenses punishable only by fine) unless the legislature plainly intends otherwise (MPC § 2.05).

**Defenses.** Mistake of fact is no defense to the strict-liability element. A voluntary act is still required.

**Due process.** Punishing a passive failure to register without notice can violate due process if the defendant had no knowledge of the duty and no probability of such knowledge (*Lambert v. California*, 355 U.S. 225 (1957)).

#### IV.B.3 Mistake of fact or law

**Mistake of fact (R-CRM-010).**

| Crime type | Mistake must be | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Specific intent | **Honest** (even if unreasonable) | D takes another's umbrella honestly believing it his own: no larceny |
| General intent / malice | **Honest and reasonable** | D believes, reasonably, that a sexual partner consents (jurisdictions vary) |
| Strict liability | No defense | Statutory rape (majority) |

**MPC.** A mistake is a defense if it negates the required culpability for an element; if the defendant would be guilty of a lesser offense had the facts been as he believed, he can be convicted of that lesser offense (MPC § 2.04(1)–(2)). California: Pen. Code § 26 (persons who acted "under an ignorance or mistake of fact, which disproves any criminal intent").

**Mistake of law (R-CRM-011).** Ignorance or mistake of law is generally **no defense**. Exceptions:
1. **Mistake negating a mental element** that includes a legal concept: a good-faith belief that property legally belongs to the defendant negates the intent to steal; a "willful" tax violation requires knowledge of the legal duty, and even an unreasonable good-faith belief negates it (*Cheek v. United States*, 498 U.S. 192 (1991)).
2. **Reasonable reliance on an official statement of law** later found invalid or erroneous: a statute, a judicial decision, an administrative order, or an official interpretation by the public officer charged with interpreting the law. Reliance on a private lawyer's advice does not count (MPC § 2.04(3)(b)).
3. **Law not reasonably made available** before the conduct (MPC § 2.04(3)(a)); *Lambert* (registration statute).

### IV.C Responsibility

#### IV.C.1 Mental disorder

**Insanity tests (R-CRM-015).**

| Test | Rule | Where |
|---|---|---|
| **M'Naghten** | At the time of the act, because of a disease of the mind, the defendant did not know (1) the nature and quality of the act, or (2) that it was wrong | *M'Naghten's Case* (1843); majority of states; **California** (Pen. Code § 25(b), read disjunctively in *People v. Skinner* (1985) 39 Cal.3d 765) [S-150] |
| **Irresistible impulse** | Because of mental illness, the defendant was unable to control his actions or conform his conduct to the law | Added to M'Naghten in a minority of states |
| **Durham (product)** | The crime was the product of mental disease or defect | *Durham v. United States*, 214 F.2d 862 (D.C. Cir. 1954); now only New Hampshire |
| **MPC (ALI)** | As a result of mental disease or defect, the defendant lacked **substantial capacity** either to **appreciate the criminality** [wrongfulness] of his conduct or to **conform** his conduct to the law | MPC § 4.01; many states |
| **Federal** | As a result of a **severe** mental disease or defect, the defendant was **unable to appreciate** the nature and quality or the wrongfulness of his acts; defendant proves by **clear and convincing** evidence | 18 U.S.C. § 17 (Insanity Defense Reform Act of 1984) |

**Constitutional floor.** Due process does not require any particular test; a state need not adopt a moral-incapacity prong (*Kahler v. Kansas* (2020) [VERIFY V-250]) and may channel mental-disease evidence to the insanity issue (*Clark v. Arizona*, 548 U.S. 735 (2006)).

**Procedure (R-CRM-016).** Defendants are presumed sane. States may place the burden of proving insanity on the defendant (*Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790 (1952)); most require a preponderance, the federal statute clear and convincing evidence. California tries guilt and sanity in **bifurcated** phases (Pen. Code § 1026) and requires the defendant to prove insanity by a preponderance (§ 25(b)) [S-150]. A defendant found not guilty by reason of insanity may be committed until no longer mentally ill or dangerous, even beyond the maximum sentence (*Jones v. United States*, 463 U.S. 354 (1983)).

**Competency is different.** Insanity concerns the defendant's state at the **time of the crime**; **competency to stand trial** concerns his **present** ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding and to understand the proceedings rationally and factually (*Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402 (1960)). Trying an incompetent defendant violates due process (*Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. 375 (1966)). See V.E (R-CRM-114).

**Diminished capacity (R-CRM-017).** Some jurisdictions allow evidence of a mental defect short of insanity to show that the defendant did not form a specific intent (it is not a complete defense; it typically reduces the crime). The MPC admits mental-disease evidence whenever relevant to whether the defendant had a required state of mind (MPC § 4.02(1)). **California abolished** the diminished-capacity defense (Pen. Code § 25(a)) [S-150], but evidence of mental disorder is admissible on whether the defendant **actually formed** a required specific intent, premeditated, deliberated or harbored malice aforethought (Pen. Code § 28(a)).

**Infancy (R-CRM-019).** At common law: under 7, conclusively incapable of crime; 7 to 14, rebuttably presumed incapable; 14 and over, treated as adults. Modern statutes give juvenile courts jurisdiction over most offenses by minors. California: children under 14 are incapable of committing crimes "in the absence of clear proof that at the time of committing the act charged against them, they knew its wrongfulness" (Pen. Code § 26).

#### IV.C.2 Intoxication

**Rule (R-CRM-018).**

| Type | Defense to | Standard |
|---|---|---|
| **Voluntary** (knowing ingestion of a substance known to be intoxicating) | **Specific-intent** crimes only, if it prevented formation of the intent | Not a defense to general-intent or malice crimes, or to crimes of recklessness (MPC § 2.08(2): unawareness of a risk due to self-induced intoxication is immaterial to recklessness) |
| **Involuntary** (unknowing ingestion; ingestion under duress; unexpected reaction to a prescribed medication) | **All** crimes, if it produces an insanity-type incapacity under the jurisdiction's test, and also negates any mental state | MPC § 2.08(4) |

- Voluntary intoxication that causes a settled mental illness may support insanity.
- **Due process** does not require that intoxication evidence be considered at all; a state may abolish voluntary intoxication as a defense (*Montana v. Egelhoff*, 518 U.S. 37 (1996)).
- Alcoholism or addiction does not make drinking involuntary (*Powell v. Texas*, 392 U.S. 514 (1968)).
- **"Dutch courage":** a defendant who becomes intoxicated in order to commit a crime he already intended cannot rely on the intoxication.

**California.** Voluntary intoxication evidence is admissible solely on whether the defendant actually formed a required specific intent or, for murder, whether he premeditated, deliberated or harbored **express** malice; it cannot negate **implied** malice (Pen. Code § 29.4) [S-361].

### IV.D Causation

**Rule.** For result crimes (homicide above all), the defendant's act must be both the **actual cause** and the **proximate cause** of the result (R-CRM-012, R-CRM-013).

**Actual cause (cause in fact).**
- **But-for test:** the result would not have occurred when and as it did but for the act.
- **Acceleration** counts: hastening the death of a dying person causes death.
- **Multiple sufficient causes:** where two independent acts would each have caused the result (two simultaneous mortal wounds), each is a cause (substantial-factor test).

**Proximate cause (legal cause).** The result must be a natural and probable (foreseeable) consequence of the act, not too remote or accidental to have a just bearing on liability (MPC § 2.03).
- **Eggshell victim:** the defendant takes the victim as he finds her (hemophilia, weak heart).
- **Dependent intervening causes** (responses to the defendant's act) usually do **not** break the chain: ordinary medical negligence in treating the wound; the victim's foreseeable attempt to escape or protect herself; a rescuer's response; disease from the injury.
- **Independent, unforeseeable intervening causes** **do** break the chain: an unrelated free, deliberate act of a third party (a second attacker who independently kills the victim); an act of God; in many courts, grossly reckless or intentional medical maltreatment that is the sole cause of death.
- **Victim's refusal of treatment** (including on religious grounds) does not break the chain.
- **Victim's own voluntary act** — supplying drugs that the victim knowingly takes — is treated differently across jurisdictions; many deny homicide liability where the victim's free, informed choice intervenes.

**MBE vs. CA essay.** MBE: classic patterns are medical negligence (no break) vs. an unrelated new attack (break). Essay: briefly prove actual and proximate cause for every homicide charge, even when obvious.

### IV.E Justification and excuse (State Bar essay outline V: Exculpation)

The State Bar's "Exculpation" section lists justification and excuse, mistake, self-defense, defense of others, duress, necessity, entrapment, consent and intoxication [VERIFY V-254]. Mistake and intoxication are covered in IV.B.3 and IV.C.2.

**Justification vs. excuse.** A **justification** makes conduct right or tolerable (self-defense, necessity, law enforcement); an **excuse** concedes wrongdoing but relieves the actor of blame (insanity, duress, infancy). The distinction matters for accomplices (one may aid a justified act) and for resisting (one may not resist justified force).

**Self-defense (R-CRM-020).** A person may use **non-deadly force** when he reasonably believes it necessary to protect himself from the imminent use of unlawful force, and **deadly force** only when he reasonably believes he faces imminent **death or serious bodily injury** (the MPC adds kidnapping and forcible sexual intercourse, § 3.04(2)(b)).
- **Reasonable belief:** honest and reasonable; the threat need not be real if the belief was reasonable.
- **Imminence:** the threat must be immediate; fear of a future attack is not enough. **Battered spouses** who kill a sleeping abuser generally do not meet imminence, though many courts admit expert testimony on battered-person syndrome to show the reasonableness of the belief in a confrontation.
- **Proportionality:** deadly force cannot answer non-deadly force.
- **Not the initial aggressor** (R-CRM-022).

**Retreat (R-CRM-021).**

| Rule | Content | Where |
|---|---|---|
| No duty to retreat ("stand your ground") | A non-aggressor may use deadly force without retreating | Majority of states; **California** (CALCRIM No. 505 includes optional no-retreat language) [S-367] |
| Retreat rule | Before using **deadly** force, retreat if it can be done with complete safety | Minority; MPC § 3.04(2)(b)(ii) |
| Castle exception | No duty to retreat in one's own home (MPC: unless initial aggressor or attacked by a co-dweller at work) | Applies even in retreat jurisdictions |
| Non-deadly force | No duty to retreat before using non-deadly force | Everywhere |

California also presumes that a resident who uses deadly force against an unlawful and forcible intruder into the residence reasonably feared imminent death or great bodily injury (Pen. Code § 198.5).

**Initial aggressor (R-CRM-022).** An initial aggressor may use force in self-defense only if (1) he **withdraws** in good faith and **communicates** the withdrawal, or (2) the victim of his non-deadly aggression **escalates** suddenly to deadly force, and the aggressor cannot safely withdraw.

**Imperfect self-defense (R-CRM-023).** An honest but unreasonable belief in the need for deadly force (or an aggressor's use of excessive force in response) is not a complete defense, but in many jurisdictions it reduces murder to voluntary manslaughter. The MPC handles it through § 3.09(2): a reckless or negligent belief supports conviction for a crime of recklessness or negligence (manslaughter or negligent homicide).

**Defense of others (R-CRM-024).** A person may use force to defend a third person when he **reasonably believes** the third person would be entitled to use that force in self-defense (majority and MPC § 3.05). Under the older minority "alter ego" rule, the defender stands in the shoes of the person defended and has no defense if that person had no right of self-defense. No special relationship is required under the modern rule.

**Defense of property and dwelling (R-CRM-025).**
- **Property:** reasonable **non-deadly** force to prevent or end an unlawful interference with property, after a request to stop unless a request would be futile or dangerous. **Deadly force is never justified to protect property alone**; spring guns and similar devices are not permitted (*People v. Ceballos* (1974) 12 Cal.3d 470).
- **Recapture:** force may be used to recover property only in immediate (hot) pursuit, and not deadly force.
- **Dwelling:** deadly force is permitted to prevent an unlawful entry where the occupant reasonably believes it necessary to prevent a violent entry and that the intruder intends to commit a felony or personal attack inside (formulations vary; many states have "castle" statutes with presumptions).

**Law enforcement and crime prevention (R-CRM-026).**
- **Police:** reasonable force to arrest; **deadly force** only to prevent escape where the officer has probable cause to believe the suspect poses a significant threat of death or serious physical injury to the officer or others, after a warning where feasible (*Tennessee v. Garner*, 471 U.S. 1 (1985)).
- **Private persons:** may use non-deadly force to prevent a felony or breach of the peace and to arrest; deadly force only to prevent a dangerous felony involving risk to human life, and (for arrest) only if the person arrested actually committed the felony.
- **Resisting an unlawful arrest:** the common law allowed reasonable non-deadly force; the modern majority and MPC prohibit force to resist an arrest the actor knows is being made by a police officer, even if the arrest is unlawful (MPC § 3.04(2)(a)(i); California: Pen. Code § 834a).

**Necessity (R-CRM-027).** Conduct otherwise criminal is justified if (1) the defendant reasonably believed it necessary to avoid **imminent harm**; (2) the harm avoided was **greater** than the harm caused; (3) there was **no reasonable legal alternative**; (4) the defendant did not create the danger (MPC: not available for crimes of recklessness or negligence if he recklessly or negligently created it); and (5) the legislature has not precluded the defense (MPC § 3.02).
- Traditionally, the pressure came from **natural forces** (storms, fire, cold); pressure from a human threat was duress. The MPC drops that line.
- **Homicide:** the common law denies necessity as a defense to intentional killing (*Regina v. Dudley and Stephens*, 14 Q.B.D. 273 (1884)); the MPC's choice-of-evils provision could in principle justify a killing that saves more lives.
- **Prison escape** to avoid intolerable conditions requires a bona fide effort to surrender once the necessity ends (*United States v. Bailey*, 444 U.S. 394 (1980)).
- Economic necessity and political protest (civil disobedience) generally fail.

**Duress (R-CRM-028).** An excuse where the defendant committed the crime because of (1) a **threat of imminent death or serious bodily injury** to himself or another (many jurisdictions limit it to family members; modern law covers anyone); (2) a person of **reasonable firmness** would have yielded; (3) no reasonable opportunity to escape; and (4) the defendant did not recklessly place himself in the situation.
- **Not a defense to intentional murder** at common law and in most states (California: Pen. Code § 26 (persons under threats "unless the crime be punishable with death"); *People v. Anderson* (2002) 28 Cal.4th 767). Duress can be a defense to the felony underlying felony murder in some jurisdictions.
- MPC § 2.09: a defense when the actor was coerced by the use or threat of unlawful force against himself or another that a person of reasonable firmness in his situation would have been unable to resist; it applies even to murder.
- Federal defendants bear the burden of proving duress by a preponderance (*Dixon v. United States*, 548 U.S. 1 (2006)).

**Entrapment (R-CRM-029).**

| Approach | Test | Where |
|---|---|---|
| **Subjective (majority, federal)** | (1) The government **induced** the crime and (2) the defendant was **not predisposed** to commit it. Predisposition must exist before government contact | *Sorrells v. United States*, 287 U.S. 435 (1932); *Sherman v. United States*, 356 U.S. 369 (1958); *Jacobson v. United States*, 503 U.S. 540 (1992) |
| **Objective (MPC, minority, California)** | Whether the police conduct was likely to induce a **normally law-abiding person** to commit the crime; the defendant's predisposition is irrelevant | MPC § 2.13; *People v. Barraza* (1979) 23 Cal.3d 675 |

- **Mere opportunity** (posing as a buyer) is not inducement.
- Inducement must come from the **government** or its agent (an informant acting at police direction); private inducement is not entrapment.
- A federal defendant may deny the crime and still claim entrapment (*Mathews v. United States*, 485 U.S. 58 (1988)).
- **Outrageous government conduct** violating due process is recognized in theory but almost never succeeds (*United States v. Russell*, 411 U.S. 423 (1973); *Hampton v. United States*, 425 U.S. 484 (1976)).

**MBE vs. CA essay.** MBE: subjective test unless told otherwise; defendants with prior similar crimes or quick acceptance are predisposed. Essay: state the majority subjective test and the objective alternative, apply both, and conclude (Feb 2017 Q6 asked whether entrapment would succeed).

**Consent (R-CRM-030).** Consent is a defense only where (1) lack of consent is an element (rape, larceny, kidnapping) or (2) the harm is minor or part of a lawful activity (contact sports, medical treatment). Consent is no defense to murder or serious bodily injury, and is ineffective if given by a person legally incapable or obtained by force or fraud in the factum. The victim's forgiveness (condonation) or contributory negligence is no defense.

### IV.F Jurisdiction

**Territorial jurisdiction (R-CRM-014).** A state may prosecute a crime if **any element** (the conduct or the result) occurs within its territory; an attempt or conspiracy outside the state to commit a crime inside, and conduct outside the state intended to produce and producing harmful effects inside (*Strassheim v. Daily*, 221 U.S. 280 (1911)), may also be prosecuted. MPC § 1.03 lists these bases. California: Pen. Code § 27 (persons liable to punishment) and § 778a (an act in California in execution of an intent that culminates in a crime inside or outside the state).

**Federal jurisdiction.** Federal crimes need a constitutional hook (commerce, taxing, federal property, federal officers); see Constitutional Law.

**Separate sovereigns.** The state and federal governments (and two states) may each prosecute the same conduct without double jeopardy (*Gamble v. United States*, 587 U.S. 678 (2019); *Heath v. Alabama*, 474 U.S. 82 (1985)); a city is not a separate sovereign from its state (*Waller v. Florida*, 397 U.S. 387 (1970)) (R-CRM-117).

**Venue and vicinage.** Federal trials must be held in the state and district where the crime was committed (U.S. Const. art. III, § 2, cl. 3; Sixth Amendment). Trial in the wrong venue does not bar retrial in the right one (*Smith v. United States* (2023) [VERIFY V-250]).

**Statutes of limitations.** Most crimes have limitation periods; murder has none (California: Pen. Code § 799).

## V Constitutional protection of accused persons

**Weight.** About half of the MBE Criminal Law and Procedure questions come from this category (about 12–13 of 25 scored) [S-040]. On California essays, Fourth Amendment search and seizure is the single most tested Criminal issue (9 tagged appearances), followed by confessions (4) [VERIFY V-071].

**Incorporation.** Nearly all Bill of Rights criminal-procedure guarantees apply to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment Due Process Clause, including the exclusionary rule (*Mapp v. Ohio*, 367 U.S. 643 (1961)), the privilege against self-incrimination (*Malloy v. Hogan*, 378 U.S. 1 (1964)), counsel (*Gideon v. Wainwright*, 372 U.S. 335 (1963)), jury trial (*Duncan v. Louisiana*, 391 U.S. 145 (1968)), double jeopardy (*Benton v. Maryland*, 395 U.S. 784 (1969)), jury unanimity (*Ramos v. Louisiana* (2020) [VERIFY V-250]) and excessive fines (*Timbs v. Indiana*, 586 U.S. 146 (2019)). The **grand-jury** indictment requirement is **not** incorporated (*Hurtado v. California*, 110 U.S. 516 (1884)).

### V.A Arrest, search and seizure (State Bar outline: Criminal Procedure I. Fourth Amendment)

The State Bar's Criminal Procedure section I covers the Fourth Amendment: reasonableness of searches and seizures, reasonable expectation of privacy, and detention [VERIFY V-254]. The Fourth Amendment protects "the right of the people to be secure in their persons, houses, papers, and effects, against unreasonable searches and seizures," and requires that warrants issue only on probable cause, supported by oath, particularly describing the place and things.

**Analysis order (use on every essay).**
1. **Government action** (R-CRM-072).
2. **Standing:** the defendant's own reasonable expectation of privacy or property interest (R-CRM-075).
3. **Search or seizure?** (R-CRM-073, R-CRM-074, R-CRM-076).
4. **Justification:** warrant with probable cause, or a warrant exception, or reasonable suspicion for a stop (R-CRM-077–R-CRM-091).
5. **Remedy:** exclusionary rule, fruit of the poisonous tree, and its limits (R-CRM-090–R-CRM-092).

#### Government action

The Fourth Amendment restricts only government agents: police, other public officials (school officials, building inspectors), and private persons acting at police direction or as police instruments. A **private search** is not covered, and police may replicate (but not exceed) a private search without a warrant (*United States v. Jacobsen*, 466 U.S. 109 (1984)) (R-CRM-072).

#### What is a "search"

**Two tests (R-CRM-073).**
1. **Reasonable expectation of privacy (Katz):** a search occurs when the government intrudes on an expectation of privacy that the person actually (subjectively) holds and that society is prepared to recognize as reasonable (*Katz v. United States*, 389 U.S. 347 (1967) (Harlan, J., concurring): eavesdropping device on a public phone booth).
2. **Trespass (property-based):** a search also occurs when the government physically intrudes on a constitutionally protected area (persons, houses, papers, effects) to obtain information (*United States v. Jones*, 565 U.S. 400 (2012): GPS on a car; *Florida v. Jardines*, 569 U.S. 1 (2013): drug dog on the front porch).

**Technology.** Using sense-enhancing technology **not in general public use** to obtain information about the interior of a home that could not otherwise be obtained without physical intrusion is a search (*Kyllo v. United States*, 533 U.S. 27 (2001): thermal imager). Acquiring **historical cell-site location information** covering an extended period is a search requiring a warrant (*Carpenter v. United States*, 585 U.S. 296 (2018)). Accessing a person's cell-phone **location history through a geofence warrant** is a search (*Chatrie v. United States* (2026) [S-353] [S-354] [VERIFY V-034]); the Court left the validity of the multi-step warrant to the lower court. Essays: Jul 2019 Q3 involved a store-bought parabolic microphone used to overhear a suspect's payphone call in an alley — analyze Katz (phone booth/payphone privacy), whether the device is in general public use (Kyllo), and the place (an alley, not a home).

**No reasonable expectation of privacy (R-CRM-074):**
- **Open fields** — land outside the curtilage, even if fenced and posted (*Oliver v. United States*, 466 U.S. 170 (1984)). **Curtilage** (the area immediately around the home, judged by proximity, enclosure, use and steps taken to shield it from view, *United States v. Dunn*, 480 U.S. 294 (1987)) is protected like the home; a driveway area next to the house is curtilage (*Collins v. Virginia*, 584 U.S. 586 (2018)).
- **Aerial observation** from public navigable airspace, with the naked eye (*California v. Ciraolo*, 476 U.S. 207 (1986); *Florida v. Riley*, 488 U.S. 445 (1989)).
- **Garbage** left at the curb for collection (*California v. Greenwood*, 486 U.S. 35 (1988)).
- **Third-party records** voluntarily conveyed: bank records (*United States v. Miller*, 425 U.S. 435 (1976)); numbers dialed via a pen register (*Smith v. Maryland*, 442 U.S. 735 (1979)) — but not long-term cell-site location data (Carpenter).
- **What is knowingly exposed to the public:** physical characteristics (voice, handwriting, face), the exterior of a car, paint scrapings, open conversations in public, items in plain view from a lawful vantage point.
- **Movements on public roads** tracked by a beeper (*United States v. Knotts*, 460 U.S. 276 (1983)), but monitoring a beeper inside a home is a search (*United States v. Karo*, 468 U.S. 705 (1984)).
- **Canine sniffs** of luggage in a public place (*United States v. Place*, 462 U.S. 696 (1983)) or a car during a lawful stop (*Illinois v. Caballes*, 543 U.S. 405 (2005)) — but not at the front door of a home (Jardines).
- **Misplaced trust:** a person who speaks to an informant or undercover officer assumes the risk that the listener will repeat or record the conversation (*Hoffa v. United States*, 385 U.S. 293 (1966); *United States v. White*, 401 U.S. 745 (1971)).
- **Manipulating luggage:** squeezing a bus passenger's soft bag to explore its contents is a search (*Bond v. United States*, 529 U.S. 334 (2000)).

#### Standing

**Rule (R-CRM-075).** Fourth Amendment rights are personal: a defendant may challenge a search only if **his own** reasonable expectation of privacy (or property interest) was violated (*Rakas v. Illinois*, 439 U.S. 128 (1978)).
- **Owners and possessors** of the place or item have standing.
- **Overnight guests** have standing in the host's home (*Minnesota v. Olson*, 495 U.S. 91 (1990)); a short-term visitor present for a commercial transaction does not (*Minnesota v. Carter*, 525 U.S. 83 (1998)).
- **Passengers** have no standing in a car they do not own or possess (Rakas) but may challenge the **stop** of the car, because they are seized (*Brendlin v. California*, 551 U.S. 249 (2007)).
- A **driver in sole possession** of a rental car has standing even if not listed on the rental agreement (*Byrd v. United States*, 584 U.S. 395 (2018)).
- **No standing** to challenge a search of a co-defendant's property or of items abandoned or disclaimed.

**Essay technique.** Address standing first, in one or two sentences, for each defendant and each place searched ("Fourth Amendment standing before suppression" is a selected-answer pattern, grader-expectations §5).

#### Seizures of persons; investigatory stops

**What is a seizure (R-CRM-076).** A person is seized when, by **physical force** or a **show of authority** to which he **submits**, his freedom of movement is restrained. Test: whether a reasonable person would feel free to leave or to decline the officers' requests and terminate the encounter (*United States v. Mendenhall*, 446 U.S. 544 (1980); *Florida v. Bostick*, 501 U.S. 429 (1991): bus sweep). A show of authority without submission is not a seizure (*California v. Hodari D.*, 499 U.S. 621 (1991): fleeing suspect who drops drugs), but the application of physical force with intent to restrain is a seizure even if the person escapes (*Torres v. Madrid* (2021) [VERIFY V-250]).

**Consensual encounters** (approaching a person and asking questions) need no justification.

**Terry stops (R-CRM-077).** Police may briefly detain a person for investigation if they have **reasonable suspicion**, based on specific and articulable facts, that criminal activity is afoot (*Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968)). Reasonable suspicion is less than probable cause and is judged on the **totality of the circumstances** (*United States v. Sokolow*, 490 U.S. 1 (1989)).
- **Unprovoked flight** from police in a high-crime area supports reasonable suspicion (*Illinois v. Wardlow*, 528 U.S. 119 (2000)); presence in a high-crime area alone, or looking "nervous," does not (Feb 2022 Q1: stop of a man for looking nervous).
- **Anonymous tips:** a bare anonymous tip that a described person is carrying a gun is not enough (*Florida v. J.L.*, 529 U.S. 266 (2000)); a tip with predictive details corroborated by police can be (*Alabama v. White*, 496 U.S. 325 (1990)); a 911 call reporting a contemporaneous eyewitnessed dangerous-driving incident can be (*Navarette v. California*, 572 U.S. 393 (2014)).
- **Duration and scope:** the stop must last no longer than necessary for its purpose; a stop that becomes too long or intrusive (moving the suspect to a police station) becomes an arrest requiring probable cause (*Florida v. Royer*, 460 U.S. 491 (1983)).
- **Identification:** a state may require a detained suspect to state his name (*Hiibel v. Sixth Judicial District Court*, 542 U.S. 177 (2004)).

**Frisks and protective searches (R-CRM-078).** During a lawful stop, an officer who reasonably believes the suspect is **armed and dangerous** may pat down the outer clothing for weapons (*Terry*). The officer may seize contraband whose identity is **immediately apparent** by touch (plain feel), but may not manipulate an object to identify it (*Minnesota v. Dickerson*, 508 U.S. 366 (1993)). Police may search the **passenger compartment** of a car for weapons if they reasonably believe the suspect is dangerous and may gain access to a weapon (*Michigan v. Long*, 463 U.S. 1032 (1983)). In an in-home arrest, officers may make a **protective sweep** of spaces where a dangerous person may hide, on reasonable suspicion (*Maryland v. Buie*, 494 U.S. 325 (1990)).

**Traffic stops (R-CRM-079).**
- A stop requires reasonable suspicion of a violation or probable cause of a traffic infraction. The officer's **actual motive is irrelevant** if objective grounds exist (pretext stops are valid) (*Whren v. United States*, 517 U.S. 806 (1996)). A reasonable mistake of law can supply reasonable suspicion (*Heien v. North Carolina*, 574 U.S. 54 (2014)); running a plate and learning the owner's license is revoked supports a stop absent contrary information (*Kansas v. Glover* (2020) [VERIFY V-250]).
- Officers may order the **driver** (*Pennsylvania v. Mimms*, 434 U.S. 106 (1977)) and **passengers** (*Maryland v. Wilson*, 519 U.S. 408 (1997)) out of the car, and may frisk a passenger reasonably believed armed and dangerous (*Arizona v. Johnson*, 555 U.S. 323 (2009)).
- A **dog sniff** is permitted, but police may not **prolong** the stop beyond the time reasonably required for its mission to conduct one without reasonable suspicion (*Rodriguez v. United States*, 575 U.S. 348 (2015)).
- **Random** stops to check a license are invalid (*Delaware v. Prouse*, 440 U.S. 648 (1979)); fixed checkpoints are governed by special-needs rules (R-CRM-088).

#### Arrest

**Rule (R-CRM-080).** An arrest is a seizure requiring **probable cause**: facts and circumstances sufficient for a reasonable person to believe that the suspect committed a crime (*Maryland v. Pringle*, 540 U.S. 366 (2003): drugs in a car supported arrest of all three occupants).
- **Public places:** no warrant needed for a felony arrest on probable cause (*United States v. Watson*, 423 U.S. 411 (1976)), or for a misdemeanor, even a fine-only one, committed in the officer's presence (*Atwater v. City of Lago Vista*, 532 U.S. 318 (2001)). An arrest that violates state law but rests on probable cause does not violate the Fourth Amendment (*Virginia v. Moore*, 553 U.S. 164 (2008)).
- **Home:** absent exigency or consent, police need an **arrest warrant** to enter the suspect's home to arrest him (*Payton v. New York*, 445 U.S. 573 (1980)) and a **search warrant** to enter a third party's home to arrest a guest (*Steagald v. United States*, 451 U.S. 204 (1981)). A Payton violation does not require suppression of a later station-house statement if police had probable cause (*New York v. Harris*, 495 U.S. 14 (1990)).
- **Prompt probable-cause determination:** a person arrested without a warrant is entitled to a prompt judicial determination of probable cause (*Gerstein v. Pugh*, 420 U.S. 103 (1975)), presumptively within **48 hours** (*County of Riverside v. McLaughlin*, 500 U.S. 44 (1991)).
- **Illegal arrest** is not a defense to prosecution and does not void the conviction; it only triggers exclusion of evidence obtained by exploiting it.
- **Arrest under a later-invalidated law:** an arrest made in good-faith reliance on a statute or ordinance later held unconstitutional is valid, unless the law was so grossly and flagrantly unconstitutional that any reasonable person would see its flaws (*Michigan v. DeFillippo*, 443 U.S. 31 (1979)); evidence found in the search incident to that arrest is admissible (R-CRM-092).

**Excessive force (R-CRM-081).** Force used in an arrest or stop is judged by Fourth Amendment **objective reasonableness**, considering the severity of the crime, whether the suspect posed an immediate threat, and whether he was actively resisting or fleeing (*Graham v. Connor*, 490 U.S. 386 (1989)); deadly force to stop a fleeing suspect requires probable cause of a significant threat of death or serious injury (*Garner*). The analysis looks at the **totality of the circumstances**, not only the moment of the threat (*Barnes v. Felix* (2025) [VERIFY V-035]).

#### Search warrants

**Requirements (R-CRM-082).**
1. **Probable cause:** a fair probability that contraband or evidence of a crime will be found in the place to be searched, judged by the **totality of the circumstances** (*Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213 (1983)). An informant's tip is assessed by its **veracity/reliability** and **basis of knowledge** as part of the totality, and police **corroboration** can make up for weaknesses.
2. **Issued by a neutral and detached magistrate** (not a prosecutor or police officer, *Coolidge v. New Hampshire*, 403 U.S. 443 (1971); not one paid per warrant issued).
3. **Particularity:** the warrant must particularly describe the place to be searched and the things to be seized; a warrant that fails to describe the items is invalid even if the application did (*Groh v. Ramirez*, 540 U.S. 551 (2004)).
4. **Oath or affirmation.**

**Franks challenge.** A defendant who makes a substantial preliminary showing that the affiant included a **deliberately false** statement, or one made with **reckless disregard** for the truth, which was **necessary** to the finding of probable cause, is entitled to a hearing; if proved, the warrant is void and the evidence excluded (*Franks v. Delaware*, 438 U.S. 154 (1978)).

**Execution.**
- **Knock and announce:** officers must generally knock, announce their purpose and wait a reasonable time before forcible entry (*Wilson v. Arkansas*, 514 U.S. 927 (1995)), unless they reasonably suspect danger, futility or destruction of evidence (*Richards v. Wisconsin*, 520 U.S. 385 (1997)). Violation does **not** trigger the exclusionary rule (*Hudson v. Michigan*, 547 U.S. 586 (2006)).
- **Detaining occupants:** officers executing a search warrant for contraband may detain occupants of the premises (*Michigan v. Summers*, 452 U.S. 692 (1981)) but not persons who have left the immediate vicinity (*Bailey v. United States*, 568 U.S. 186 (2013)).
- **Persons on the premises:** a warrant to search a place does not authorize searching every person present (*Ybarra v. Illinois*, 444 U.S. 85 (1979)).
- **Scope:** the search may extend to any place where the items could be found; seizure of other items is valid under plain view.
- **Anticipatory warrants** are valid if there is probable cause that the triggering condition will occur and that, if it does, evidence will be found (*United States v. Grubbs*, 547 U.S. 90 (2006)).

#### Warrant exceptions

**Search incident to lawful arrest (R-CRM-083).**
- **Person and wingspan:** after a **lawful custodial arrest**, police may search the arrestee's person and the area within his **immediate control** (*Chimel v. California*, 395 U.S. 752 (1969)), automatically, without suspicion (*United States v. Robinson*, 414 U.S. 218 (1973)). The search must be **contemporaneous** with the arrest.
- **Vehicles:** if the arrestee is an occupant or **recent occupant** of the vehicle (*Thornton v. United States*, 541 U.S. 615 (2004)), police may search the passenger compartment incident to arrest only if (1) the arrestee is **unsecured and within reaching distance** of the compartment at the time of the search, or (2) it is reasonable to believe **evidence of the offense of arrest** might be found in the vehicle (*Arizona v. Gant*, 556 U.S. 332 (2009)).
- **Cell phones:** police generally need a **warrant** to search the digital contents of a phone seized incident to arrest (*Riley v. California*, 573 U.S. 373 (2014)). Where a suppression item is a text message or other phone data (Jul 2021 Q4 asked about a text message), consider Riley.
- **Breath vs. blood:** a breath test is permitted incident to a DUI arrest; a blood test is not (*Birchfield v. North Dakota*, 579 U.S. 438 (2016)).
- **Citation only:** no search incident to a mere traffic citation (*Knowles v. Iowa*, 525 U.S. 113 (1998)).
- **DNA** cheek swabs of persons arrested for serious offenses are a legitimate booking procedure (*Maryland v. King*, 569 U.S. 435 (2013)).

**Automobile exception (R-CRM-084).** If police have **probable cause** to believe a vehicle contains contraband or evidence of a crime, they may search it without a warrant (*Carroll v. United States*, 267 U.S. 132 (1925)), including any **container** in the vehicle that could hold the object of the search (*California v. Acevedo*, 500 U.S. 565 (1991)) and passengers' belongings (*Wyoming v. Houghton*, 526 U.S. 295 (1999)). The search may happen later at the station (*Chambers v. Maroney*, 399 U.S. 42 (1970)). The exception does **not** permit entry into a home's curtilage to search a vehicle there (*Collins v. Virginia*, 584 U.S. 586 (2018)). Probable cause limited to a container found in the car allows searching that container, not the whole car (Acevedo). Drugs found in a car trunk (Jul 2021 Q4) call for automobile-exception analysis alongside Gant.

**Plain view (R-CRM-085).** Police may seize an item without a warrant if (1) they are **lawfully present** at the vantage point, (2) they have **lawful access** to the item, and (3) its **incriminating character is immediately apparent** (probable cause) (*Coolidge*; *Horton v. California*, 496 U.S. 128 (1990): inadvertence not required). Moving an item even slightly to see a serial number is a search requiring probable cause (*Arizona v. Hicks*, 480 U.S. 321 (1987)).

**Consent (R-CRM-086).**
- **Voluntary** under the totality of circumstances; knowledge of the right to refuse is a factor but not required (*Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218 (1973)). Consent following a false claim of a warrant is not voluntary (*Bumper v. North Carolina*, 391 U.S. 543 (1968)).
- **Scope:** what a reasonable person would understand from the exchange (*Florida v. Jimeno*, 500 U.S. 248 (1991): consent to search a car for drugs includes a closed bag on the floor). Consent may be withdrawn.
- **Third-party consent:** a person with **common authority** (joint access or control) may consent (*United States v. Matlock*, 415 U.S. 164 (1974)); **apparent authority** suffices if police reasonably believe the person has authority (*Illinois v. Rodriguez*, 497 U.S. 177 (1990)).
- **Co-occupant objection:** a physically present co-occupant's express objection overrides another occupant's consent as to him (*Georgia v. Randolph*, 547 U.S. 103 (2006)); if the objector is lawfully removed (arrested), the remaining occupant may later consent (*Fernandez v. California*, 571 U.S. 292 (2014)).
- **Parents** can usually consent to search of a child's room; a landlord or hotel clerk cannot consent to search a tenant's or guest's room (*Stoner v. California*, 376 U.S. 483 (1964)).

**Exigent circumstances (R-CRM-087).** A warrantless search or entry is reasonable when an emergency makes obtaining a warrant impracticable:
- **Hot pursuit** of a fleeing felon (*Warden v. Hayden*, 387 U.S. 294 (1967)); pursuit of a fleeing **misdemeanant** does not categorically justify home entry and is judged case by case (*Lange v. California* (2021) [VERIFY V-250]).
- **Imminent destruction of evidence**, including where police did not create the exigency by violating or threatening to violate the Fourth Amendment (*Kentucky v. King*, 563 U.S. 452 (2011)). The natural dissipation of alcohol in blood does not create a per se exigency for a warrantless blood draw (*Missouri v. McNeely*, 569 U.S. 141 (2013)).
- **Emergency aid:** police may enter a home without a warrant when they have an **objectively reasonable basis for believing** that an occupant is seriously injured or imminently threatened with such injury (*Brigham City v. Stuart*, 547 U.S. 398 (2006)). The standard is objective reasonableness, **not probable cause** (*Case v. Montana* (2026) [S-351] [S-352] [VERIFY V-034]: 911 call and visible signs of a threatened suicide justified entry). There is **no free-standing "community caretaking" exception** for the home (*Caniglia v. Strom* (2021) [VERIFY V-250]).
- **Danger to police or the public** (a suspect with a gun; a fire — officials may remain a reasonable time to investigate its cause, *Michigan v. Tyler*, 436 U.S. 499 (1978)).
- **Essay pattern:** Feb 2018 Q4 (search for a missing person, then a warrant obtained only afterward): analyze emergency aid for the first entry, plain view, and the **independent-source** doctrine for evidence found under the later warrant.

**Administrative, special-needs and other searches (R-CRM-088).**
- **Inventory** searches of an impounded car or an arrestee's belongings, under standardized police procedures, are valid without probable cause (*Colorado v. Bertine*, 479 U.S. 367 (1987); *Illinois v. Lafayette*, 462 U.S. 640 (1983)); opening closed containers requires a standard policy covering them (*Florida v. Wells*, 495 U.S. 1 (1990)).
- **Checkpoints:** sobriety checkpoints (*Michigan Dept. of State Police v. Sitz*, 496 U.S. 444 (1990)), border-area checkpoints (*United States v. Martinez-Fuerte*, 428 U.S. 543 (1976)) and information-seeking checkpoints (*Illinois v. Lidster*, 540 U.S. 419 (2004)) are valid; a checkpoint whose primary purpose is general crime control (drug interdiction) is not (*City of Indianapolis v. Edmond*, 531 U.S. 32 (2000)).
- **Border searches:** routine border searches need no suspicion (*United States v. Ramsey*, 431 U.S. 606 (1977); *United States v. Flores-Montano*, 541 U.S. 149 (2004)).
- **Schools:** a school official may search a student on **reasonable grounds** that the search will turn up evidence of a violation of law or school rules, if reasonable in scope (*New Jersey v. T.L.O.*, 469 U.S. 325 (1985)); a strip search requires more (*Safford Unified School District v. Redding*, 557 U.S. 364 (2009)). Random drug testing of student athletes and students in extracurricular activities is valid (*Vernonia School District 47J v. Acton*, 515 U.S. 646 (1995); *Board of Education v. Earls*, 536 U.S. 822 (2002)).
- **Probationers and parolees:** a probationer subject to a search condition may be searched on reasonable suspicion (*United States v. Knights*, 534 U.S. 112 (2001)); a parolee may be searched without any suspicion (*Samson v. California*, 547 U.S. 843 (2006)).
- **Closely regulated businesses** (junkyards, liquor, firearms) may be inspected without a warrant under a statutory scheme that limits discretion (*New York v. Burger*, 482 U.S. 691 (1987)); other administrative inspections require an administrative warrant on reasonable legislative or administrative standards (*Camara v. Municipal Court*, 387 U.S. 523 (1967)).

**Electronic and digital surveillance (R-CRM-089).**
- **Wiretapping** and electronic eavesdropping on private conversations are searches (*Katz*), and federal law (Title III, 18 U.S.C. §§ 2510–2522) requires a court order with probable cause, particularity as to the conversations and persons, limited duration and minimization.
- **No warrant** where one party consents (an informant wearing a wire, *White*) or the speaker has no reasonable expectation of privacy (loud speech audible to the public).
- **Location data:** short-term public movements (Knotts) vs. GPS installation (Jones) vs. historical cell-site records over an extended period (Carpenter) vs. geofence location history (Chatrie [S-353] [VERIFY V-034]).
- **Phones:** Riley requires a warrant for the contents of a phone seized incident to arrest.

#### The exclusionary rule and its limits

**Rule and scope (R-CRM-090).** Evidence obtained in violation of the Fourth Amendment is generally inadmissible in the prosecution's case-in-chief in federal (*Weeks v. United States*, 232 U.S. 383 (1914)) and state (*Mapp v. Ohio*, 367 U.S. 643 (1961)) courts. The rule is a judicially created remedy designed to **deter** police misconduct, applied only where its deterrence benefits outweigh its substantial social costs (*Herring v. United States*, 555 U.S. 135 (2009); *Davis v. United States*, 564 U.S. 229 (2011)).

**Where it does not apply:** grand-jury proceedings (*United States v. Calandra*, 414 U.S. 338 (1974)); civil proceedings, including deportation; parole revocation hearings (*Pennsylvania Board of Probation and Parole v. Scott*, 524 U.S. 357 (1998)); federal habeas review of Fourth Amendment claims that received a full and fair hearing in state court (*Stone v. Powell*, 428 U.S. 465 (1976)); and **impeachment** of the defendant's own testimony (*Walder v. United States*, 347 U.S. 62 (1954); *United States v. Havens*, 446 U.S. 620 (1980)) — but not impeachment of other defense witnesses (*James v. Illinois*, 493 U.S. 307 (1990)).

**California.** Under the Truth-in-Evidence provision (Proposition 8, 1982), "relevant evidence shall not be excluded in any criminal proceeding," subject to listed exceptions (Cal. Const. art. I, § 28(f)(2)) [S-145]. California courts therefore exclude evidence for unlawful searches **only when the federal Constitution requires** (*In re Lance W.* (1985) 37 Cal.3d 873). The motion is made under Pen. Code § 1538.5. On an essay, the federal analysis is the California analysis.

**Fruit of the poisonous tree (R-CRM-091).** Evidence derived from an illegal search, seizure, arrest or (for actual coercion) interrogation is also excluded (*Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471 (1963)), unless the prosecution shows:
1. **Independent source:** the evidence was also obtained through a source independent of the illegality, including a later valid warrant not prompted by and not based on what was seen in the illegal entry (*Murray v. United States*, 487 U.S. 533 (1988); *Segura v. United States*, 468 U.S. 796 (1984)).
2. **Inevitable discovery:** the evidence would inevitably have been discovered by lawful means (*Nix v. Williams*, 467 U.S. 431 (1984): search party about to reach the body), proved by a preponderance.
3. **Attenuation:** the connection is so attenuated that the taint is dissipated, considering (a) **temporal proximity**, (b) **intervening circumstances**, and (c) the **purpose and flagrancy** of the misconduct (*Brown v. Illinois*, 422 U.S. 590 (1975)); Miranda warnings alone do not purge an illegal arrest (Brown). The discovery of a valid, pre-existing arrest warrant is an intervening circumstance (*Utah v. Strieff*, 579 U.S. 232 (2016)). A defendant's independent act of free will (returning voluntarily days later to confess) can break the chain (*Wong Sun*). Live-witness testimony is rarely suppressed as fruit (*United States v. Ceccolini*, 435 U.S. 268 (1978)).

**Good faith and related limits (R-CRM-092).** Evidence is not excluded when police acted in **objectively reasonable reliance** on:
- a **warrant** later found invalid (*United States v. Leon*, 468 U.S. 897 (1984); *Massachusetts v. Sheppard*, 468 U.S. 981 (1984)), **unless** (1) the affidavit is so lacking in indicia of probable cause that reliance is unreasonable ("bare bones"); (2) the warrant is so facially deficient (particularity) that officers cannot reasonably presume it valid; (3) the affiant misled the magistrate (Franks); or (4) the magistrate wholly abandoned the judicial role;
- a **statute** authorizing the search, later held unconstitutional (*Illinois v. Krull*, 480 U.S. 340 (1987)); compare an arrest under a later-invalidated ordinance (*Michigan v. DeFillippo*, 443 U.S. 31 (1979); see Arrest above);
- **binding appellate precedent** later overruled (*Davis v. United States*, 564 U.S. 229 (2011));
- **database errors** by court clerks (*Arizona v. Evans*, 514 U.S. 1 (1995)) or isolated negligent police record-keeping errors (*Herring*). Deliberate, reckless, grossly negligent or systemic errors are not protected.

**Knock-and-announce** violations do not lead to exclusion (*Hudson v. Michigan*, 547 U.S. 586 (2006)).

**MBE vs. CA essay (V.A).** MBE and essay apply the same federal rules. Essay technique: for **each item of evidence**, write a heading, then standing → search? → warrant or exception → exclusion and limits. Two selected answers can reach opposite conclusions (Feb 2025 Q1); the points are in the analysis.

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: PRC Criminal Procedure Law excludes illegally obtained confessions (torture) and some physical evidence that cannot be "corrected or reasonably explained," but there is no general deterrence-based exclusionary rule or fruit doctrine like the US one (approx.).

### V.B Confessions and privilege against self-incrimination (State Bar outline: Criminal Procedure II. Fifth Amendment)

A confession can be challenged on four separate grounds; analyze each that the facts raise:

| Ground | Source | Applies when |
|---|---|---|
| Due-process voluntariness | Fourteenth (and Fifth) Amendment due process | Any police-coerced statement, custody or not |
| Miranda | Fifth Amendment privilege (as applied to custodial interrogation) | Custodial interrogation without warnings or valid waiver, or after invocation |
| Sixth Amendment (Massiah) | Right to counsel | After formal charges, deliberate elicitation about the charged offense (V.D) |
| Fruit of an illegal arrest or search | Fourth Amendment | Statement exploits an unlawful seizure (R-CRM-091) |

**Due-process voluntariness (R-CRM-093).** A confession is inadmissible if, under the **totality of the circumstances**, it was **involuntary** — the defendant's will was overborne by **police coercion** (physical abuse, threats, prolonged incommunicado interrogation, promises of leniency in some cases). Coercive police activity is a necessary predicate; a confession driven by the defendant's own mental illness is not involuntary (*Colorado v. Connelly*, 479 U.S. 157 (1986)). Relevant factors: age, education, mental condition, length of questioning, deprivation of food or sleep, threats, promises. An involuntary confession cannot be used **even for impeachment** (*Mincey v. Arizona*, 437 U.S. 385 (1978)); its admission is reviewed for harmless error (*Arizona v. Fulminante*, 499 U.S. 279 (1991)). The trial court must determine voluntariness outside the jury's presence (*Jackson v. Denno*, 378 U.S. 368 (1964)); the prosecution proves voluntariness by a preponderance (*Lego v. Twomey*, 404 U.S. 477 (1972)).

**Miranda (R-CRM-094–R-CRM-100).** Before **custodial interrogation**, police must warn the suspect that (1) he has the right to remain silent, (2) anything he says can be used against him in court, (3) he has the right to the presence of an attorney, and (4) if he cannot afford one, an attorney will be appointed before questioning (*Miranda v. Arizona*, 384 U.S. 436 (1966)). Miranda is a constitutional rule that Congress cannot overrule (*Dickerson v. United States*, 530 U.S. 428 (2000)), but a Miranda violation alone does not support a civil-rights damages claim (*Vega v. Tekoh* (2022) [VERIFY V-250]).

**Custody (R-CRM-094).** Custody exists when, under the objective circumstances, a reasonable person would have felt he was not free to terminate the interrogation and leave, **and** the environment presents the inherently coercive pressures of a station-house interrogation (*Stansbury v. California*, 511 U.S. 318 (1994); *Howes v. Fields*, 565 U.S. 499 (2012)).
- **Not custody:** a routine **traffic stop** or Terry stop (*Berkemer v. McCarty*, 468 U.S. 420 (1984)) — but Miranda applies once the suspect is in custody, whatever the offense; a voluntary station-house interview where the suspect is told he is not under arrest and leaves afterwards (*Oregon v. Mathiason*, 429 U.S. 492 (1977); *California v. Beheler*, 463 U.S. 1121 (1983)); a prisoner questioned about an unrelated matter in a setting where he was told he could leave the interview (*Howes*).
- **Factors:** location, duration, statements made, physical restraints, release at the end, number of officers, whether told free to leave.
- The officer's **undisclosed** suspicions are irrelevant (Stansbury). A child's **age**, if known or objectively apparent, is part of the analysis (*J.D.B. v. North Carolina*, 564 U.S. 261 (2011)).

**Interrogation (R-CRM-095).** Express questioning **or its functional equivalent**: words or actions that the police **should know are reasonably likely to elicit an incriminating response** (*Rhode Island v. Innis*, 446 U.S. 291 (1980): officers' remarks about a missing shotgun and handicapped children were not interrogation on the facts).
- **Volunteered statements** are not the product of interrogation and are admissible.
- **Booking questions** (name, address, height) are within a routine-booking exception (*Pennsylvania v. Muniz*, 496 U.S. 582 (1990) — the exception was recognized in a plurality opinion; eight Justices agreed the answers were admissible).
- **Undercover agents:** questioning by an undercover officer posing as a cellmate is not Miranda interrogation, because there is no coercive police-dominated atmosphere (*Illinois v. Perkins*, 496 U.S. 292 (1990)) — but see Massiah after charges.

**Warnings and waiver (R-CRM-096).**
- Warnings need not track Miranda's exact words if they reasonably convey the rights (*Duckworth v. Eagan*, 492 U.S. 195 (1989); *Florida v. Powell*, 559 U.S. 50 (2010)).
- **Waiver** must be **knowing, intelligent and voluntary**, proved by a preponderance. It may be **implied** from a suspect's understanding of the warnings followed by an uncoerced statement (*North Carolina v. Butler*, 441 U.S. 369 (1979); *Berghuis v. Thompkins*, 560 U.S. 370 (2010): answering after nearly three hours of mostly silence was an implied waiver).
- Police failure to tell the suspect that a lawyer retained by family is trying to reach him does not invalidate the waiver (*Moran v. Burbine*, 475 U.S. 412 (1986)); nor does failing to tell him all the crimes to be discussed (*Colorado v. Spring*, 479 U.S. 564 (1987)).

**Invoking the right to remain silent (R-CRM-097).** The suspect must invoke **unambiguously**; mere silence is not invocation (*Berghuis v. Thompkins*). Once invoked, police must **scrupulously honor** it; they may resume questioning only after a significant time has passed, with fresh warnings, typically about a different crime (*Michigan v. Mosley*, 423 U.S. 96 (1975): two hours, new warnings, different robbery).

**Invoking the right to counsel (R-CRM-098).** The request must be **unambiguous** — a statement a reasonable officer would understand as a request for an attorney; "Maybe I should talk to a lawyer" is not enough (*Davis v. United States*, 512 U.S. 452 (1994)). Once the suspect unambiguously invokes counsel, **all** interrogation must stop until counsel is present, unless the **suspect himself reinitiates** communication (*Edwards v. Arizona*, 451 U.S. 477 (1981)).
- The Edwards bar covers questioning about **any** crime, not just the one under investigation (*Arizona v. Roberson*, 486 U.S. 675 (1988)).
- Once counsel is requested, police may not reinitiate even after the suspect has consulted counsel (*Minnick v. Mississippi*, 498 U.S. 146 (1990)).
- The Edwards protection **ends 14 days after release from custody** (return to normal life), after which police may approach and seek a new waiver (*Maryland v. Shatzer*, 559 U.S. 98 (2010)).
- **Reinitiation** requires the suspect to show a willingness for a generalized discussion of the investigation, not just a request for water or a bathroom (*Oregon v. Bradshaw*, 462 U.S. 1039 (1983)).

**Public-safety exception (R-CRM-099).** Police may ask questions reasonably prompted by an **immediate concern for public safety** (where is the gun?) before giving warnings, and the answers are admissible (*New York v. Quarles*, 467 U.S. 649 (1984)). The test is objective; the exception covers only questions necessary to secure the officers' or the public's safety, not to gather evidence. Typical essay pattern: an officer asks where a gun is right after an arrest in a public place.

**Fruits, two-step interrogation and impeachment (R-CRM-100).**
- **Physical fruits** of a voluntary but unwarned statement are admissible (*United States v. Patane*, 542 U.S. 630 (2004): gun found from an unwarned statement; plurality opinion with a two-Justice concurrence in the judgment).
- **Later warned statement:** if the first unwarned statement was voluntary, a later statement after proper warnings and waiver is admissible (*Oregon v. Elstad*, 470 U.S. 298 (1985)), **unless** police used a **deliberate two-step** "question first, warn later" strategy that made the midstream warnings ineffective (*Missouri v. Seibert*, 542 U.S. 600 (2004)). Under Justice Kennedy's controlling concurrence, if the two-step was deliberate, the later statement is excluded unless curative measures (a substantial break in time and circumstances, or a warning that the earlier statement is inadmissible) were taken.
- **Impeachment:** a voluntary statement taken in violation of Miranda may be used to **impeach** the defendant's inconsistent trial testimony (*Harris v. New York*, 401 U.S. 222 (1971)); an **involuntary** one may not (Mincey). California follows Harris (*People v. May* (1988) 44 Cal.3d 309, applying Truth-in-Evidence) [S-364].
- **Silence:** the prosecution may not use a defendant's **post-Miranda-warning** silence to impeach him (*Doyle v. Ohio*, 426 U.S. 610 (1976)); pre-arrest silence by a suspect who did not expressly invoke the privilege during a non-custodial interview may be used as evidence of guilt (*Salinas v. Texas*, 570 U.S. 178 (2013)).

**The privilege against self-incrimination generally (R-CRM-101).** "No person … shall be compelled in any criminal case to be a witness against himself" (U.S. Const. amend. V).
- Protects only **testimonial** communications (*Schmerber v. California*, 384 U.S. 757 (1966): blood sample not testimonial). Non-testimonial: fingerprints, blood, handwriting and voice exemplars, lineups, physical evidence.
- Must be **compelled** by the government, and **incriminating** (a real risk of criminal prosecution).
- **Documents:** contents of voluntarily prepared business records are not protected, but the **act of producing** them may be testimonial (existence, possession, authenticity) (*Fisher v. United States*, 425 U.S. 391 (1976); *United States v. Hubbell*, 530 U.S. 27 (2000)). A custodian of corporate records cannot invoke the privilege for the entity's records (*Braswell v. United States*, 487 U.S. 99 (1988)); corporations and other collective entities have no privilege.
- **Who may invoke:** any witness, in any proceeding (civil, criminal, administrative, legislative), where the answer could incriminate. A criminal **defendant** may refuse to take the stand at all.
- **Comment:** the prosecution may not comment on the defendant's failure to testify (*Griffin v. California*, 380 U.S. 609 (1965)); on request, the court must instruct the jury not to draw an adverse inference (*Carter v. Kentucky*, 450 U.S. 288 (1981)).
- **Immunity:** the government can compel testimony by granting **use and derivative-use** immunity, which is coextensive with the privilege; transactional immunity is not required (*Kastigar v. United States*, 406 U.S. 441 (1972)).
- **Waiver:** a defendant who testifies waives the privilege as to cross-examination within the scope of direct.

**MBE vs. CA essay (V.B).** Same federal rules on both. Essay structure for each statement: (1) voluntariness; (2) Miranda: custody → interrogation → warnings → waiver → invocation; (3) Sixth Amendment if charges were filed; (4) fruits and impeachment. Feb 2017 Q6, Jul 2023 Q4 and Feb 2025 Q1 each asked whether to suppress a statement.

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: The PRC Criminal Procedure Law bars compelling self-incrimination but also requires suspects to answer investigators' relevant questions truthfully; there is no Miranda-style warning-and-waiver regime (approx.).

### V.C Lineups and other forms of identification

**Right to counsel (R-CRM-106).** A defendant has a Sixth Amendment right to counsel at a **post-charge, in-person** lineup or show-up (*United States v. Wade*, 388 U.S. 218 (1967); *Gilbert v. California*, 388 U.S. 263 (1967)), but **not** at a pre-charge lineup (*Kirby v. Illinois*, 406 U.S. 682 (1972)) or at a **photographic** array, before or after charges (*United States v. Ash*, 413 U.S. 300 (1973)). If counsel was wrongly absent, the lineup identification is excluded, and an **in-court** identification is allowed only if the prosecution shows by clear and convincing evidence that it rests on an **independent source** (the witness's own observation at the crime) (Wade).

**Due process (R-CRM-107).** Regardless of counsel, an identification procedure violates due process if it was (1) **unnecessarily suggestive** and (2) created a **substantial likelihood of misidentification** (*Stovall v. Denno*, 388 U.S. 293 (1967); *Neil v. Biggers*, 409 U.S. 188 (1972)). **Reliability is the linchpin**: even a suggestive identification is admissible if reliable under the totality of the circumstances (*Manson v. Brathwaite*, 432 U.S. 98 (1977)). The **Biggers factors**:
1. the witness's **opportunity to view** the criminal at the time of the crime;
2. the witness's **degree of attention**;
3. the **accuracy** of the witness's prior description;
4. the witness's level of **certainty** at the confrontation;
5. the **time** between the crime and the confrontation.

- **Suggestive examples:** a lineup in which only the suspect matches the description (*Foster v. California*, 394 U.S. 440 (1969)); a single-photo display; a one-person show-up without exigency (Stovall allowed one because the only witness might die).
- The due-process check applies only to suggestiveness **arranged by police**; an identification made under suggestive circumstances not arranged by police is tested by ordinary trial safeguards (cross-examination, jury instructions) (*Perry v. New Hampshire*, 565 U.S. 228 (2012)).
- **Remedy:** exclusion of the out-of-court identification and of any in-court identification tainted by it, unless there is an independent source.
- **Other identification evidence:** compelled exemplars (handwriting, voice, appearing in a lineup, wearing clothing) do not violate the Fifth Amendment because they are not testimonial (Wade; Schmerber). An arrest without probable cause does not bar the victim's in-court identification based on her own memory (*United States v. Crews*, 445 U.S. 463 (1980)).

**Essay pattern (Oct 2020 Q4).** The call limited the defendant to the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments for a pre-arraignment lineup (so no Sixth Amendment issue): analyze suggestiveness (all six men bearded and long-haired, which points to a fair composition), then reliability with each Biggers factor (a witness who needed 20 minutes at the lineup raises a certainty question).

### V.D Right to counsel

**When it attaches (R-CRM-102).** The Sixth Amendment right to counsel attaches at the initiation of **adversary judicial proceedings** — formal charge, preliminary hearing, indictment, information or arraignment (*Kirby*), including the first appearance before a magistrate where the defendant learns the charge and liberty is restricted (*Rothgery v. Gillespie County*, 554 U.S. 191 (2008)). It applies at every **critical stage** after attachment: post-charge lineups, preliminary hearings (*Coleman v. Alabama*, 399 U.S. 1 (1970)), plea negotiations and pleas, trial, sentencing, and the first appeal as of right. It does not apply to pre-charge investigation, photo arrays, taking handwriting or blood samples, or most post-conviction proceedings.

**Deliberate elicitation (Massiah).** After the right attaches, the government may not **deliberately elicit** incriminating statements about the **charged offense** from the defendant without counsel or a valid waiver (*Massiah v. United States*, 377 U.S. 201 (1964); *Brewer v. Williams*, 430 U.S. 387 (1977): "Christian burial speech"). An informant placed in a cell who actively stimulates conversation violates the right (*United States v. Henry*, 447 U.S. 264 (1980)); a passive "listening post" does not (*Kuhlmann v. Wilson*, 477 U.S. 436 (1986)).
- **Offense-specific:** the right covers only the charged offense and offenses that are the "same offense" under Blockburger (*McNeil v. Wisconsin*, 501 U.S. 171 (1991); *Texas v. Cobb*, 532 U.S. 162 (2001)). Police may question a represented defendant about **uncharged**, different crimes (subject to Miranda).
- **Waiver:** a defendant may waive the Sixth Amendment right after Miranda warnings, even if he has a lawyer, and police may initiate the approach (*Patterson v. Illinois*, 487 U.S. 285 (1988); *Montejo v. Louisiana*, 556 U.S. 778 (2009)) — but if he invokes counsel during custodial interrogation, Edwards applies.
- **Impeachment:** a statement obtained in violation of Massiah may be used to impeach the defendant's contrary testimony (*Kansas v. Ventris*, 556 U.S. 586 (2009)).

**Miranda counsel vs. Sixth Amendment counsel.**

| | Miranda / Edwards (Fifth) | Sixth Amendment |
|---|---|---|
| Triggers | Custodial interrogation | Formal charges; critical stage |
| Scope | All crimes once invoked (Roberson) | Offense-specific (McNeil, Cobb) |
| Undercover agent | Not interrogation (Perkins) | Violation if deliberate elicitation (Henry) |
| Invocation required? | Yes, unambiguous (Davis) | Attaches automatically, but waivable (Montejo) |
| Ends | 14 days after release (Shatzer) | Continues through the case |

**Appointed counsel (R-CRM-103).** An indigent defendant has a right to appointed counsel in **any felony** prosecution (*Gideon v. Wainwright*, 372 U.S. 335 (1963)) and in any misdemeanor case in which **imprisonment is actually imposed** (*Argersinger v. Hamlin*, 407 U.S. 25 (1972); *Scott v. Illinois*, 440 U.S. 367 (1979)), including a suspended sentence that may be activated (*Alabama v. Shelton*, 535 U.S. 654 (2002)). The right extends to the **first appeal as of right** (*Douglas v. California*, 372 U.S. 353 (1963)) but not to discretionary appeals (*Ross v. Moffitt*, 417 U.S. 600 (1974)) or habeas.

**Self-representation and counsel of choice (R-CRM-104).**
- A defendant has a right to represent himself if his waiver of counsel is **knowing and intelligent** and he is **competent** to waive (*Faretta v. California*, 422 U.S. 806 (1975)); the request must be timely and unequivocal. Technical legal knowledge is irrelevant. The competency standard to waive counsel is the same as to stand trial (*Godinez v. Moran*, 509 U.S. 389 (1993)), but a state may insist on counsel for a defendant competent to stand trial but too mentally ill to conduct the trial himself (*Indiana v. Edwards*, 554 U.S. 164 (2008)). The court may appoint standby counsel over objection (*McKaskle v. Wiggins*, 465 U.S. 168 (1984)). A self-represented defendant cannot later claim his own ineffectiveness. Jul 2014 Q4 tested a request to self-represent made a week before trial (timeliness; knowing waiver).
- A defendant with retained counsel has a right to **counsel of choice**; wrongful denial is structural error requiring reversal without a showing of prejudice (*United States v. Gonzalez-Lopez*, 548 U.S. 140 (2006)). The court may refuse a waiver of conflict-free counsel (*Wheat v. United States*, 486 U.S. 153 (1988)). Pretrial restraint of untainted assets needed to retain counsel violates the Sixth Amendment (*Luis v. United States*, 578 U.S. 5 (2016)).
- **Consultation during trial:** a court may not bar all consultation between a testifying defendant and counsel during an overnight recess (*Geders v. United States*, 425 U.S. 80 (1976)), but may bar consultation during a short recess (*Perry v. Leeke*, 488 U.S. 272 (1989)), and may bar discussion of the defendant's **ongoing testimony** during an overnight recess if other consultation remains allowed (*Villarreal v. Texas* (2026) [S-355] [VERIFY V-034]).
- **Client autonomy:** counsel may not concede guilt over the defendant's express objection (*McCoy v. Louisiana*, 584 U.S. 414 (2018)). Decisions to plead guilty, waive a jury, testify and appeal belong to the client.

**Ineffective assistance (R-CRM-105).** A defendant must show (1) **deficient performance** — below an objective standard of reasonableness, under highly deferential review of strategic choices — and (2) **prejudice** — a reasonable probability that, but for the errors, the result would have been different (*Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668 (1984)).
- **Plea context:** prejudice means a reasonable probability the defendant would not have pleaded guilty and would have gone to trial (*Hill v. Lockhart*, 474 U.S. 52 (1985)); counsel must advise of clear deportation consequences (*Padilla v. Kentucky*, 559 U.S. 356 (2010)); counsel must communicate formal plea offers (*Missouri v. Frye*, 566 U.S. 134 (2012)); bad advice leading to rejection of a plea offer can be prejudicial (*Lafler v. Cooper*, 566 U.S. 156 (2012)).
- **Presumed prejudice:** complete denial of counsel at a critical stage, or counsel who entirely fails to subject the case to adversarial testing (*United States v. Cronic*, 466 U.S. 648 (1984)); failing to file a requested appeal, even after an appeal waiver (*Garza v. Idaho*, 586 U.S. 232 (2019)).
- **Conflicts of interest:** if the trial court forces joint representation over a timely objection, reversal is automatic (*Holloway v. Arkansas*, 435 U.S. 475 (1978)); otherwise the defendant must show an **actual conflict** that **adversely affected** counsel's performance (*Cuyler v. Sullivan*, 446 U.S. 335 (1980)).
- **Client perjury:** a lawyer's refusal to assist a client's planned perjury is not ineffective assistance (*Nix v. Whiteside*, 475 U.S. 157 (1986)). (Jul 2014 Q4 involved a client who wanted to testify falsely.)

### V.E Fair trial and guilty pleas

**Pretrial (R-CRM-108).**
- **Bail:** the Eighth Amendment bars excessive bail (bail set higher than reasonably necessary to assure appearance, *Stack v. Boyle*, 342 U.S. 1 (1951)); preventive detention of dangerous arrestees after an adversary hearing is constitutional (*United States v. Salerno*, 481 U.S. 739 (1987)).
- **Probable-cause hearing** (Gerstein; 48 hours, McLaughlin).
- **Grand jury:** required for federal felonies (Fifth Amendment), not incorporated (Hurtado). Grand juries may hear inadmissible and illegally obtained evidence (*Costello v. United States*, 350 U.S. 359 (1956); *Calandra*), and the prosecutor need not present exculpatory evidence (*United States v. Williams*, 504 U.S. 36 (1992)). Discrimination in grand-jury selection violates equal protection. California may proceed by **information** after a preliminary hearing.
- **Preliminary hearing:** a critical stage requiring counsel (*Coleman v. Alabama*).

**Speedy trial (R-CRM-109).** Sixth Amendment speedy-trial claims are judged by the **Barker factors**: (1) length of delay, (2) reason for delay, (3) the defendant's assertion of the right, and (4) prejudice (*Barker v. Wingo*, 407 U.S. 514 (1972)). The right attaches at arrest or formal charge, whichever comes first; the remedy is **dismissal with prejudice**. Excessive delay can make prejudice presumptive (*Doggett v. United States*, 505 U.S. 647 (1992): eight and a half years). It does not cover the period between conviction and sentencing (*Betterman v. Montana*, 578 U.S. 437 (2016)). Pre-charge delay is governed by statutes of limitations and due process (actual prejudice plus improper reason).

**Jury trial (R-CRM-110).**
- **When:** for any offense with an authorized penalty of **more than six months'** imprisonment (*Baldwin v. New York*, 399 U.S. 66 (1970)); offenses with six months or less are presumptively petty (*Blanton v. City of North Las Vegas*, 489 U.S. 538 (1989)), and aggregate sentences for multiple petty offenses do not create a right (*Lewis v. United States*, 518 U.S. 322 (1996)).
- **Size and unanimity:** at least six jurors (*Williams v. Florida*, 399 U.S. 78 (1970); *Ballew v. Georgia*, 435 U.S. 223 (1978)); a six-person jury must be unanimous (*Burch v. Louisiana*, 441 U.S. 130 (1979)); verdicts for serious offenses must be **unanimous** in state and federal courts (*Ramos v. Louisiana* (2020) [VERIFY V-250]). California requires 12 jurors and unanimity in felony cases (Cal. Const. art. I, § 16).
- **Fair cross-section** of the community in the venire (not the petit jury itself) (*Taylor v. Louisiana*, 419 U.S. 522 (1975); *Duren v. Missouri*, 439 U.S. 357 (1979)).
- **Peremptory challenges** may not be based on **race** (*Batson v. Kentucky*, 476 U.S. 79 (1986)) or **sex** (*J.E.B. v. Alabama ex rel. T.B.*, 511 U.S. 127 (1994)). Three steps: (1) prima facie showing; (2) a neutral explanation; (3) the court decides whether purposeful discrimination is proved (*Flowers v. Mississippi*, 588 U.S. 284 (2019)). The defendant need not share the juror's race (*Powers v. Ohio*, 499 U.S. 400 (1991)); the defense is also bound (*Georgia v. McCollum*, 505 U.S. 42 (1992)). California adds its own procedure (*People v. Wheeler* (1978) 22 Cal.3d 258; Code Civ. Proc. § 231.7 for criminal trials since 2022 [S-365]).
- **Death qualification:** jurors whose views would prevent or substantially impair their duties may be excused for cause (*Wainwright v. Witt*, 469 U.S. 412 (1985)).
- **Juror racial bias in deliberations:** the no-impeachment rule yields to evidence that a juror relied on racial stereotypes or animus (*Peña-Rodriguez v. Colorado*, 580 U.S. 206 (2017)).
- **Impartiality and publicity:** prejudicial pretrial publicity may require a change of venue, continuance or careful voir dire (*Sheppard v. Maxwell*, 384 U.S. 333 (1966)).

**Apprendi (R-CRM-111).** Other than the fact of a prior conviction, any fact that increases the penalty beyond the **statutory maximum** must be submitted to a jury and proved **beyond a reasonable doubt** (*Apprendi v. New Jersey*, 530 U.S. 466 (2000)). The "statutory maximum" is the maximum the judge may impose based solely on facts reflected in the verdict or admitted (*Blakely v. Washington*, 542 U.S. 296 (2004)). The rule covers facts increasing a mandatory minimum (*Alleyne v. United States*, 570 U.S. 99 (2013)) and aggravating facts for the death penalty (*Ring v. Arizona*, 536 U.S. 584 (2002); *Hurst v. Florida*, 577 U.S. 92 (2016)); the federal Sentencing Guidelines are advisory to avoid the problem (*United States v. Booker*, 543 U.S. 220 (2005)). Whether prior offenses were committed on separate "occasions" under the Armed Career Criminal Act is for the jury (*Erlinger v. United States* (2024) [VERIFY V-035]). California's former determinate sentencing law violated Apprendi (*Cunningham v. California*, 549 U.S. 270 (2007)); under the current Pen. Code § 1170(b), the middle term is the presumptive maximum and aggravating facts for an upper term must be stipulated, found true beyond a reasonable doubt, or (for prior convictions) shown by certified records [S-369], and the California Supreme Court requires a jury finding (or admission) for such aggravating facts (*People v. Lynch* (2024) 16 Cal.5th 730) [S-133].

**Confrontation (R-CRM-112).** The Sixth Amendment Confrontation Clause bars admission of **testimonial** hearsay of a witness who does not testify at trial unless the witness is **unavailable** and the defendant had a **prior opportunity to cross-examine** (*Crawford v. Washington*, 541 U.S. 36 (2004)).
- **Testimonial:** statements whose primary purpose is to establish past facts for later prosecution — police interrogation after an emergency has ended, affidavits, prior testimony, forensic reports (*Melendez-Diaz v. Massachusetts*, 557 U.S. 305 (2009); *Bullcoming v. New Mexico*, 564 U.S. 647 (2011)).
- **Non-testimonial:** statements to police whose primary purpose is to meet an **ongoing emergency** (*Davis v. Washington*, 547 U.S. 813 (2006); *Michigan v. Bryant*, 562 U.S. 344 (2011)); statements to private persons, such as a child to a teacher (*Ohio v. Clark*, 576 U.S. 237 (2015)); business records not made for prosecution; co-conspirator statements.
- An expert who relays an absent analyst's statements as the **basis** for the expert's opinion offers them for their truth (*Smith v. Arizona* (2024) [VERIFY V-035]).
- **Forfeiture by wrongdoing:** the defendant forfeits the right if he wrongfully caused the witness's absence **intending** to prevent testimony (*Giles v. California*, 554 U.S. 353 (2008)).
- **Codefendant confessions:** a non-testifying codefendant's confession that facially incriminates the defendant cannot be admitted at a joint trial, even with a limiting instruction (*Bruton v. United States*, 391 U.S. 123 (1968)); redactions that obviously refer to the defendant fail (*Gray v. Maryland*, 523 U.S. 185 (1998)); a confession redacted to avoid naming the defendant, with a limiting instruction, is permitted (*Richardson v. Marsh*, 481 U.S. 200 (1987); *Samia v. United States* (2023) [VERIFY V-035]).
- **Face-to-face confrontation** may yield to a case-specific finding that a child witness would be traumatized (*Maryland v. Craig*, 497 U.S. 836 (1990)); a generalized statutory presumption is not enough (*Coy v. Iowa*, 487 U.S. 1012 (1988); *Pitts v. Mississippi* (2025) [VERIFY V-034]).
- Evidence and Criminal crossovers (Feb 2024 Q4; Jul 2026 Q5) combine hearsay rules with confrontation; see `03-subjects/evidence/outline.md` (Phase 4, group 3).

**Disclosure and false evidence (R-CRM-113).** Due process requires the prosecution to disclose evidence **favorable** to the accused (exculpatory or impeachment) that is **material** — a reasonable probability that disclosure would have changed the result (*Brady v. Maryland*, 373 U.S. 83 (1963); *Giglio v. United States*, 405 U.S. 150 (1972); *United States v. Bagley*, 473 U.S. 667 (1985); *Kyles v. Whitley*, 514 U.S. 419 (1995): materiality judged cumulatively, and the prosecutor is responsible for evidence known to police). Good or bad faith is irrelevant. Knowing use of **false testimony**, or failure to correct it, requires a new trial if there is any reasonable likelihood it affected the verdict (*Napue v. Illinois*, 360 U.S. 264 (1959); *Glossip v. Oklahoma* (2025) [VERIFY V-250]). Failure to preserve **potentially useful** evidence violates due process only if done in **bad faith** (*Arizona v. Youngblood*, 488 U.S. 51 (1988)).

**Other fair-trial guarantees (R-CRM-114).**
- **Competency to stand trial** (Dusky; Pate). A state may presume competence and place the burden on the defendant by a preponderance (*Medina v. California*, 505 U.S. 437 (1992)) but not by clear and convincing evidence (*Cooper v. Oklahoma*, 517 U.S. 348 (1996)). A defendant who is unlikely to regain competency cannot be held indefinitely (*Jackson v. Indiana*, 406 U.S. 715 (1972)). Execution of a person who is insane (unable to understand the punishment and its reason) is barred (*Ford v. Wainwright*, 477 U.S. 399 (1986)).
- **Impartial judge:** a judge with a direct financial interest in the outcome (*Tumey v. Ohio*, 273 U.S. 510 (1927)) or a serious probability of actual bias (*Caperton v. A.T. Massey Coal Co.*, 556 U.S. 868 (2009)) violates due process.
- **Appearance:** compelling the defendant to wear prison clothes before the jury (*Estelle v. Williams*, 425 U.S. 501 (1976)) or visible shackles without case-specific justification (*Deck v. Missouri*, 544 U.S. 622 (2005)) violates due process.
- **Right to present a defense, testify and compel witnesses:** *Washington v. Texas*, 388 U.S. 14 (1967); *Chambers v. Mississippi*, 410 U.S. 284 (1973); *Rock v. Arkansas*, 483 U.S. 44 (1987).
- **Public trial:** closure requires an overriding interest, narrow tailoring, consideration of alternatives and findings (*Waller v. Georgia*, 467 U.S. 39 (1984)); it extends to voir dire (*Presley v. Georgia*, 558 U.S. 209 (2010)).
- **Prosecutorial vindictiveness:** raising charges in retaliation for exercising a right (trial de novo appeal) is presumptively vindictive (*Blackledge v. Perry*, 417 U.S. 21 (1974)); a harsher sentence after a successful appeal must be justified by objective information (*North Carolina v. Pearce*, 395 U.S. 711 (1969)); but threatening more serious charges during plea bargaining is permissible (*Bordenkircher v. Hayes*, 434 U.S. 357 (1978)).

**Guilty pleas (R-CRM-115).**
- A plea must be **knowing, intelligent and voluntary**, shown **on the record** (*Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238 (1969)). The judge must personally address the defendant and determine that he understands the **nature of the charge** (elements) (*Henderson v. Morgan*, 426 U.S. 637 (1976)), the **maximum** possible penalty and any mandatory minimum, and the **rights waived** (jury trial, confrontation, privilege against self-incrimination), and must find a **factual basis** (Fed. R. Crim. P. 11).
- A defendant may plead guilty while protesting innocence if the record shows strong evidence of guilt (*North Carolina v. Alford*, 400 U.S. 25 (1970)).
- **Plea bargains** are enforceable: if the prosecutor breaks a promise, the remedy is specific performance or withdrawal of the plea (*Santobello v. New York*, 404 U.S. 257 (1971)); a defendant who breaches may lose the benefit (*Ricketts v. Adamson*, 483 U.S. 1 (1987)). A plea is not involuntary merely because it was entered to avoid a harsher penalty (*Brady v. United States*, 397 U.S. 742 (1970)).
- **Judges** are not bound by the parties' sentencing recommendation unless they accept a binding agreement (Fed. R. Crim. P. 11(c)).
- **Effect:** a valid plea waives non-jurisdictional defects before the plea (*Tollett v. Henderson*, 411 U.S. 258 (1973)) but not a claim that the statute of conviction is unconstitutional (*Class v. United States*, 583 U.S. 174 (2018)).
- **Appeal waivers** in plea agreements are generally enforceable; a sentencing judge's statement that the defendant "has the right to appeal" did not cancel a written waiver that could be modified only in writing (*Hunter v. United States* (2026) [S-356] [VERIFY V-255]).
- **Collateral attack:** a plea can be attacked for lack of jurisdiction, ineffective assistance (Hill v. Lockhart; Padilla), failure to understand the charge, or a broken bargain.
- **Essay pattern:** Jul 2025 Q5 asked whether a judge properly accepted a guilty plea and whether the sentence was proper (with a PR crossover).

### V.F Double jeopardy

**Attachment (R-CRM-116).** Jeopardy attaches in a **jury trial** when the jury is **empaneled and sworn** (*Crist v. Bretz*, 437 U.S. 28 (1978)); in a **bench trial** when the first witness is sworn (*Serfass v. United States*, 420 U.S. 377 (1975)); on a **guilty plea** when the court accepts it. It does not attach in civil proceedings (unless the sanction is punitive in purpose or effect, *Hudson v. United States*, 522 U.S. 93 (1997)) or civil commitment (*Kansas v. Hendricks*, 521 U.S. 346 (1997)).

**Same offense.** Two offenses are the same unless **each requires proof of an element the other does not** (*Blockburger v. United States*, 284 U.S. 299 (1932); *United States v. Dixon*, 509 U.S. 688 (1993)).
- A **lesser-included offense** and the greater offense are the same offense: conviction or acquittal of one bars later prosecution for the other (*Brown v. Ohio*, 432 U.S. 161 (1977): joyriding and auto theft). **Exception:** if the greater offense (murder) was not complete when the lesser was prosecuted (the victim dies later), or the state could not proceed on it with due diligence, a later prosecution is allowed.
- **Multiple punishments in one trial:** cumulative punishment for the same offense in a single trial is permitted only if the legislature clearly authorized it (*Missouri v. Hunter*, 459 U.S. 359 (1983)). Congress did not clearly authorize convictions under both 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(1)(A)(i) and § 924(j) for a single act (*Barrett v. United States* (2026) [S-350] [VERIFY V-255]).

**Retrial (R-CRM-117).**
- **Acquittal** bars retrial, even if based on an egregiously erroneous legal ruling (*Evans v. Michigan*, 568 U.S. 313 (2013)). A conviction of a lesser offense is an **implied acquittal** of the greater (*Green v. United States*, 355 U.S. 184 (1957); *Price v. Georgia*, 398 U.S. 323 (1970)).
- **Hung jury:** retrial permitted. **Mistrial** declared over the defendant's objection: retrial only if there was **manifest necessity** (*Illinois v. Somerville*, 410 U.S. 458 (1973); *Arizona v. Washington*, 434 U.S. 497 (1978)). **Mistrial at the defendant's request:** retrial permitted unless the prosecution **intended to goad** the defendant into moving for a mistrial (*Oregon v. Kennedy*, 456 U.S. 667 (1982)).
- **Successful appeal** by the defendant: retrial permitted, except where reversal is for **insufficient evidence** (*Burks v. United States*, 437 U.S. 1 (1978)). Reversal because the verdict was against the weight of the evidence does not bar retrial (*Tibbs v. Florida*, 457 U.S. 31 (1982)).
- **Separate sovereigns** (state and federal; two states) may each prosecute (*Gamble*; *Heath*); a municipality is not separate from its state (*Waller v. Florida*).
- **Collateral estoppel:** once an issue of ultimate fact has been determined by a valid final judgment of acquittal, it cannot be relitigated between the same parties (*Ashe v. Swenson*, 397 U.S. 436 (1970): poker-game robbery of several victims).
- **Government appeals:** the prosecution may appeal pretrial rulings and post-verdict rulings that can be reversed without a new trial (reinstating a guilty verdict).

### V.G Cruel and unusual punishment

**Rule (R-CRM-118).** The Eighth Amendment bars cruel and unusual punishments, excessive fines and excessive bail; it applies to the states (*Robinson v. California*; *Timbs v. Indiana*, 586 U.S. 146 (2019) for fines).

**Proportionality — non-capital.** Only an extreme sentence **grossly disproportionate** to the crime violates the Amendment; long recidivist sentences have been upheld (*Harmelin v. Michigan*, 501 U.S. 957 (1991): life without parole for drug possession; *Ewing v. California*, 538 U.S. 11 (2003): California three-strikes sentence; but see *Solem v. Helm*, 463 U.S. 277 (1983)).

**Capital punishment.**
- The death penalty is not per se unconstitutional, but requires guided discretion and individualized consideration of mitigating evidence (*Gregg v. Georgia*, 428 U.S. 153 (1976); *Woodson v. North Carolina*, 428 U.S. 280 (1976): mandatory death sentences invalid; *Lockett v. Ohio*, 438 U.S. 586 (1978)).
- **Disproportionate for:** rape of an adult (*Coker v. Georgia*, 433 U.S. 584 (1977)); rape of a child where the victim did not die, for crimes against individuals (*Kennedy v. Louisiana*, 554 U.S. 407 (2008)); a felony-murder accomplice who did not kill, attempt or intend to kill (*Enmund*), unless a major participant acting with reckless indifference (*Tison*).
- **Categorical exemptions:** intellectually disabled offenders (*Atkins v. Virginia*, 536 U.S. 304 (2002); *Hall v. Florida*, 572 U.S. 701 (2014)); offenders under 18 at the time of the crime (*Roper v. Simmons*, 543 U.S. 551 (2005)); the insane (*Ford*).

**Juveniles — life without parole.** Barred for non-homicide offenses (*Graham v. Florida*, 560 U.S. 48 (2010)); **mandatory** LWOP for juvenile homicide is barred (*Miller v. Alabama*, 567 U.S. 460 (2012)), retroactively (*Montgomery v. Louisiana*, 577 U.S. 190 (2016)), but no separate finding of permanent incorrigibility is required (*Jones v. Mississippi* (2021) [VERIFY V-250]).

**Status vs. conduct.** Punishing a status (addiction) violates the Eighth Amendment (*Robinson*); punishing conduct does not, even if related to a condition (*Powell*), and enforcing generally applicable public-camping laws against homeless persons is not cruel and unusual punishment (*City of Grants Pass v. Johnson* (2024) [S-366]).

**Methods and conditions.** A method of execution is cruel only if it presents a substantial risk of severe pain compared with a known, available alternative (*Baze v. Rees*, 553 U.S. 35 (2008); *Glossip v. Gross*, 576 U.S. 863 (2015); *Bucklew v. Precythe*, 587 U.S. 119 (2019)). Deliberate indifference to a prisoner's serious medical needs violates the Amendment (*Estelle v. Gamble*, 429 U.S. 97 (1976)).

**Excessive fines.** A forfeiture is an excessive fine if grossly disproportional to the gravity of the offense (*United States v. Bajakajian*, 524 U.S. 321 (1998)).

### V.H Burdens of proof and persuasion

**Rule (R-CRM-119).** Due process requires the prosecution to prove **every element** of the crime **beyond a reasonable doubt** (*In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358 (1970)). An instruction that misdescribes reasonable doubt is structural error (*Sullivan v. Louisiana*, 508 U.S. 275 (1993)); a conviction stands on appeal if, viewing the evidence most favorably to the prosecution, **any rational trier of fact** could have found the elements beyond a reasonable doubt (*Jackson v. Virginia*, 443 U.S. 307 (1979)).

**Affirmative defenses.** The state may place the burden of proving an affirmative defense on the defendant (insanity, *Leland*; extreme emotional disturbance, *Patterson v. New York*, 432 U.S. 197 (1977); self-defense, *Martin v. Ohio*, 480 U.S. 228 (1987); duress, *Dixon*) so long as the defense does not negate an element. It may not shift to the defendant the burden of disproving an element, such as requiring him to prove heat of passion when malice (absence of provocation) is an element (*Mullaney v. Wilbur*, 421 U.S. 684 (1975)).

**Presumptions.**
- A **mandatory** presumption that shifts the burden of persuasion on an element (or a conclusive presumption) is unconstitutional (*Sandstrom v. Montana*, 442 U.S. 510 (1979); *Francis v. Franklin*, 471 U.S. 307 (1985)).
- A **permissive** inference is valid if the presumed fact is more likely than not to flow from the proved fact on the facts of the case (*County Court of Ulster County v. Allen*, 442 U.S. 140 (1979)).

**Burden of production vs. persuasion.** A defendant typically bears the burden of **producing** some evidence of a defense; once produced, the prosecution may bear the burden of persuasion (depending on the defense and jurisdiction). Federal: defendant proves insanity by clear and convincing evidence (18 U.S.C. § 17(b)).

**Sentencing facts** that raise the maximum must be found by a jury beyond a reasonable doubt (Apprendi, R-CRM-111).

### V.I Appeal and error

**Rule (R-CRM-120).**
- **No constitutional right to appeal** (*McKane v. Durston*, 153 U.S. 684 (1894)), but where a state provides appeals, it must not discriminate against indigents: free transcripts (*Griffin v. Illinois*, 351 U.S. 12 (1956)) and counsel for the first appeal as of right (*Douglas v. California*).
- **Harmless error:** most constitutional errors do not require reversal if the government proves them **harmless beyond a reasonable doubt** (*Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18 (1967)), including admission of a coerced confession (*Fulminante*) and omission of an element from jury instructions (*Neder v. United States*, 527 U.S. 1 (1999)).
- **Structural errors** require automatic reversal: complete denial of counsel (Gideon); a biased judge (Tumey); racial discrimination in grand or petit jury selection; denial of self-representation (Faretta); denial of a public trial (Waller); a defective reasonable-doubt instruction (Sullivan); denial of counsel of choice (Gonzalez-Lopez) (*Arizona v. Fulminante*; *Weaver v. Massachusetts*, 582 U.S. 286 (2017) (public-trial violation raised by ineffective-assistance claim requires prejudice)).
- **Plain error:** an unpreserved error may be corrected if it is (1) an error, (2) plain, (3) affecting substantial rights, and (4) seriously affecting the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings (Fed. R. Crim. P. 52(b); *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725 (1993)).
- **Retroactivity:** new rules apply to all cases on direct review (*Griffith v. Kentucky*, 479 U.S. 314 (1987)); new procedural rules do not apply on federal collateral review (*Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288 (1989); *Edwards v. Vannoy* (2021) [VERIFY V-250] abandoned the "watershed" exception).
- **Federal habeas corpus** for state prisoners (28 U.S.C. § 2254): requires exhaustion of state remedies; relief only if the state decision was contrary to, or an unreasonable application of, clearly established Supreme Court law (§ 2254(d)); one-year limitation period (§ 2244(d)); Fourth Amendment claims fully litigated in state court are not cognizable (*Stone v. Powell*); on collateral review, an error is harmless unless it had a substantial and injurious effect on the verdict (*Brecht v. Abrahamson*, 507 U.S. 619 (1993)).
- **Double jeopardy limits on government appeals** (V.F).

## Specific crimes and remaining criminal procedure (outline gap: State Bar essay sections not captured)

The State Bar essay outline has sections beyond those captured in Phase 0 (likely specific crimes against the person and property, and further procedure); this section collects crimes and procedure points not covered above [VERIFY V-254]. Depth is limited because these appear rarely on essays.

**Crimes against the person.**
- **Mayhem:** malicious dismemberment or disfigurement (II.D).
- **False imprisonment:** unlawful confinement without consent (II.F).
- **Carjacking** (California Pen. Code § 215): taking a motor vehicle from the person or immediate presence of another by force or fear (a robbery variant).
- **Stalking and criminal threats** are statutory; true-threat prosecutions require at least recklessness as to the threatening character (*Counterman* [VERIFY V-035]).

**Crimes against property and justice.**
- **Forgery:** making or altering a writing with legal significance so that it is false, with intent to defraud. **Uttering:** offering a forged instrument as genuine with intent to defraud. A genuine instrument obtained by fraud is not forged (it may be false pretenses).
- **Malicious mischief:** malicious destruction of or damage to another's property.
- **Bad checks:** issuing a check knowing there are insufficient funds, with intent to defraud.
- **Perjury:** a false material statement under oath, made knowingly. **Subornation of perjury:** procuring another to commit perjury.
- **Bribery:** at common law, corruptly paying or receiving something of value to influence an official's discretion; modern statutes cover commercial bribery. Wharton's rule limits conspiracy charges between briber and official.
- **Obstruction of justice; misprision** (III.B).

**Remaining procedure.**
- **Charging:** indictment (grand jury) or information (prosecutor after preliminary hearing).
- **Joinder and severance:** a court may sever defendants where a joint trial would compromise a specific trial right (Bruton).
- **Statutes of limitations** (IV.F).
- **Sentencing:** Apprendi line (V.E); ex post facto limits on retroactive increases (restitution under the federal MVRA is criminal punishment for Ex Post Facto purposes, *Ellingburg v. United States* (2026) [VERIFY V-034]); California upper-term rules (*People v. Lynch* [S-133]).
- **Post-conviction:** appeals and habeas (V.I); California resentencing petitions under Pen. Code § 1172.6 for persons convicted under felony-murder or natural-and-probable-consequences theories no longer valid after SB 1437 and SB 775 [S-360].
- **California procedure points:** suppression motion (§ 1538.5); accomplice testimony requires corroboration (§ 1111); creative-expression evidence limits (Evid. Code § 352.2) [S-116]; see [ca-distinctions.md](ca-distinctions.md).
