# Criminal Law and Procedure — rule statements

**How to use.** Each block gives a 1–3 sentence rule you can write verbatim, then elements, exceptions, authority and a short example. The full discussion is in [outline.md](outline.md); California-specific points are collected in [ca-distinctions.md](ca-distinctions.md).

**Which rule on which exam.** For substantive crimes (R-CRM-001 to R-CRM-071), the MBE uses the generally accepted common-law or majority rule unless the question names a statute or the MPC; a California essay uses general principles — the common law, with the MPC noted where it differs — unless the call asks for California law [S-077]. For criminal procedure (R-CRM-072 to R-CRM-120), both the MBE and the essays apply the U.S. Constitution, and California's Truth-in-Evidence clause makes the federal exclusion rules the California rules (R-CRM-090).

**Citation conventions.** U.S. Reports citations are given where certain. October Term 2025 decisions listed in `00-exam-intel/recent-changes.md` carry `[VERIFY V-034]`; October Terms 2022–2024 decisions listed there carry `[VERIFY V-035]`. Other recent decisions cited from memory carry `[VERIFY V-250]`; California cases of moderate certainty carry `[VERIFY V-251]`.

## A. Foundations and general principles (outline IV)

### R-CRM-001 — Sources of criminal law and legality
**Rule (write this):** Crimes are defined by statute, read in light of their common-law meaning; there are no federal common-law crimes. A person may be punished only under a law that existed when he acted, that gives fair notice of what is forbidden, and that does not invite arbitrary enforcement; genuine ambiguity is resolved in the defendant's favor (rule of lenity).

**Elements:** 1. A pre-existing statute defines the crime (no ex post facto law). 2. The statute is sufficiently definite (no vagueness). 3. Its terms are construed by their common-law meaning unless the legislature says otherwise. 4. Real ambiguity → lenity.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Judicial enlargement of a crime that is unexpected and indefensible violates due process; abolishing an outdated common-law rule (year-and-a-day) is not.

**Authority:** U.S. Const. art. I, §§ 9–10 (Ex Post Facto Clauses); amend. XIV (due process); *Rogers v. Tennessee*, 532 U.S. 451 (2001); MPC § 1.05(1).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule. On essays, state the common-law elements and note the MPC where it differs, unless the call asks for California law.

**Example:** A state supreme court abolishes the year-and-a-day rule and applies the change to D, whose victim died 15 months after the attack. Under Rogers, the retroactive abolition does not violate due process.

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: Comparable to the PRC principle "no crime or punishment without a law" (罪刑法定), but US crimes are mostly state-law creatures read against the common law (approx.).

### R-CRM-002 — Voluntary act requirement
**Rule (write this):** Criminal liability requires conduct that includes a voluntary act, or an omission to perform an act the defendant was physically capable of performing. Reflexes, convulsions, movements during sleep or unconsciousness, and conduct under hypnosis are not voluntary acts, but liability may rest on an earlier voluntary act that created the risk.

**Elements:** 1. A bodily movement (or legally required omission). 2. That was the product of the actor's conscious effort or determination. 3. That caused the social harm.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Prior voluntary act (driving while knowing one is prone to seizures). Status alone cannot be punished (Robinson). Possession is an act if knowing (R-CRM-059).

**Authority:** MPC § 2.01(1)–(2), (4); *People v. Decina*, 2 N.Y.2d 133 (1956); *Robinson v. California*, 370 U.S. 660 (1962).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** D, an epileptic warned not to drive, has a seizure at the wheel and kills a pedestrian. The seizure is not a voluntary act, but choosing to drive was; D can be liable for involuntary manslaughter.

### R-CRM-003 — Omission liability
**Rule (write this):** A failure to act is criminal only if the defendant had a legal duty to act, knew the facts giving rise to the duty, and could reasonably have performed it. A legal duty arises from statute, contract, a special relationship, voluntary assumption of care, or the defendant's creation of the peril; there is no general duty to rescue a stranger.

**Elements:** 1. Legal duty (statute; contract; parent–child or spouse relationship; voluntary assumption, especially secluding the victim; creation of peril; duty to control). 2. Knowledge of the facts creating the duty. 3. Physical capability. 4. Causation of the harm.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A moral duty is not enough. A duty from voluntary assumption may end if the defendant does not leave the victim worse off (did not cut off other help).

**Authority:** MPC § 2.01(3); *Jones v. United States*, 308 F.2d 307 (D.C. Cir. 1962).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** D, an Olympic swimmer, watches a stranger drown and does nothing. No crime: no legal duty. If D had pushed the stranger in, even accidentally, D would have a duty to attempt a rescue.

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: Similar to the PRC sources of a duty to act in omission crimes (statute, profession, contract, prior conduct) (approx.).

### R-CRM-004 — Common-law mental states
**Rule (write this):** At common law, specific-intent crimes require an intent or objective beyond the act itself (attempt, conspiracy, solicitation, first-degree murder, assault, larceny, robbery, burglary, embezzlement, false pretenses, forgery); general-intent crimes require only intent to do the act (battery, rape, kidnapping); malice crimes (murder, arson) require intent or reckless disregard of a known risk; and strict-liability crimes require no mental state as to some element.

**Elements:** 1. Identify the crime's category. 2. Specific intent: voluntary intoxication and any honest mistake of fact can negate it. 3. General intent and malice: only a reasonable mistake of fact negates; voluntary intoxication does not. 4. Strict liability: no mistake defense.

**Exceptions / defenses:** The MPC replaces these categories with purpose, knowledge, recklessness and negligence (R-CRM-005).

**Authority:** Common law; *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246 (1952).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE tests the categories mainly through intoxication and mistake. On essays, identify the category when raising those defenses.

**Example:** Drunk, D takes a coat believing it is his. Larceny is specific intent; D's honest (even unreasonable) belief negates the intent to steal.

### R-CRM-005 — MPC culpability levels
**Rule (write this):** Under the MPC, a person acts purposely when it is his conscious object to engage in conduct or cause a result; knowingly when he is aware of the nature of his conduct or practically certain it will cause the result; recklessly when he consciously disregards a substantial and unjustifiable risk; and negligently when he should be aware of such a risk, each being a gross deviation from the applicable standard. Culpability is assessed element by element, and if a statute is silent, recklessness is the default.

**Elements:** 1. Identify each material element (conduct, result, attendant circumstance). 2. Assign the required level. 3. Silent statute → at least recklessness (§ 2.02(3)). 4. A level stated without distinction applies to all material elements (§ 2.02(4)). 5. A higher level satisfies a lower (§ 2.02(5)).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Willful blindness (awareness of a high probability, unless actual belief otherwise) counts as knowledge (§ 2.02(7)). Strict liability limited to violations (§ 2.05).

**Authority:** MPC § 2.02; *Global-Tech Appliances, Inc. v. SEB S.A.*, 563 U.S. 754 (2011).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE may ask which mental state a statute requires; apply the MPC rules of construction. Essays: mention the MPC level as an alternative to the common-law label.

**Example:** A statute punishes "knowingly selling a firearm to a minor." Under § 2.02(4), "knowingly" applies to the buyer's age too; a seller who reasonably believed the buyer was 21 is not guilty.

### R-CRM-006 — Strict liability
**Rule (write this):** A strict-liability offense requires no mental state as to one or more elements; courts recognize it mainly for public-welfare offenses with light penalties. For serious or common-law-derived crimes, courts presume a mens rea requirement even when the statute is silent.

**Elements:** 1. Regulatory or public-welfare purpose. 2. Light penalty, little stigma. 3. Legislative intent to dispense with mens rea. 4. A voluntary act is still required.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Mistake of fact is no defense to the strict-liability element. Due process may bar punishing a passive failure to register without notice (Lambert). MPC limits strict liability to violations.

**Authority:** *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246 (1952); *Staples v. United States*, 511 U.S. 600 (1994); *Rehaif v. United States*, 588 U.S. 225 (2019); *Lambert v. California*, 355 U.S. 225 (1957); MPC § 2.05.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule. Statutory rape is strict liability as to age on the MBE (majority); California allows a reasonable mistake-of-age defense (R-CRM-055).

**Example:** A grocer sells contaminated meat without knowing it. Under a public-welfare statute with a fine, he is liable even though careful.

### R-CRM-007 — Vicarious and corporate liability
**Rule (write this):** Vicarious liability for another's act is permitted for regulatory offenses with light penalties, and a responsible corporate officer may be convicted of a public-welfare violation he had authority to prevent or correct. A corporation is criminally liable for acts of its agents within the scope of their employment, and the MPC limits corporate liability for true crimes to conduct authorized or recklessly tolerated by the board or a high managerial agent.

**Elements:** 1. Regulatory offense or officer with responsibility and power to prevent. 2. Agent acting within scope (corporation). 3. MPC: high managerial agent involvement for serious crimes.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Imprisonment on a purely vicarious basis raises due-process concerns; an officer may show he was powerless to prevent the violation.

**Authority:** *United States v. Park*, 421 U.S. 658 (1975); *United States v. Dotterweich*, 320 U.S. 277 (1943); MPC § 2.07.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule; rarely on essays.

**Example:** A food chain's CEO received reports of rodent infestation at a warehouse and did nothing; he can be convicted under the food-adulteration statute (Park).

### R-CRM-008 — Transferred intent
**Rule (write this):** When a defendant intends to harm one person and instead causes the same kind of harm to another, his intent transfers to the actual victim. The defendant is also liable for attempt against the intended victim.

**Elements:** 1. Intent to cause a harm to X. 2. The same type of harm results to Y. 3. Defenses and mitigations (provocation, self-defense) transfer too.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Intent does not transfer between different kinds of harm (intent to damage property ≠ intent to injure a person). There is no "transferred" attempt against the unintended victim.

**Authority:** Common law; MPC § 2.03(2)(a).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** D shoots at A intending to kill him, misses and kills B. D is guilty of murder of B and attempted murder of A.

### R-CRM-009 — Concurrence
**Rule (write this):** The defendant's mental state must concur with the act that causes the harm: the intent must actuate the conduct. A later-formed intent does not convert an earlier innocent act into a crime, except under the continuing-trespass doctrine for larceny.

**Elements:** 1. Mental state present at the time of the act. 2. The mental state motivated the act that caused the result.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Continuing trespass (R-CRM-044). Most courts treat a series of acts aimed at the same goal (attack, then disposal of a "corpse" who was still alive) as one transaction.

**Authority:** Common law; Cal. Pen. Code § 20.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** D enters a house to shelter from a storm and, once inside, decides to steal a radio. No burglary: the intent to steal did not exist at entry.

### R-CRM-010 — Mistake of fact
**Rule (write this):** A mistake of fact is a defense if it negates the mental state required for the crime. For specific-intent crimes any honest mistake suffices, even an unreasonable one; for general-intent and malice crimes the mistake must be honest and reasonable; and for strict-liability elements mistake is no defense.

**Elements:** 1. Defendant actually held the mistaken belief. 2. The belief, if true, would negate the required mental state. 3. Reasonableness required for general intent/malice.

**Exceptions / defenses:** MPC: a mistake is a defense if it negates the culpability required; if the defendant would be guilty of a lesser offense on the facts as he believed them, he can be convicted of that lesser offense.

**Authority:** MPC § 2.04(1)–(2); Cal. Pen. Code § 26 (mistake of fact disproving criminal intent).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** D takes V's identical suitcase from a carousel, unreasonably believing it is his own. Not larceny: specific intent, so an unreasonable but honest belief is a defense.

### R-CRM-011 — Mistake of law
**Rule (write this):** Ignorance or mistake of law is generally no defense. It excuses only when (1) it negates a mental element that includes a legal concept, (2) the defendant reasonably relied on an official statement of the law later found erroneous, or (3) the law was not reasonably made available before the conduct.

**Elements:** For reliance: 1. An official statement — statute, judicial decision, administrative order, or interpretation by the official charged with interpreting the law. 2. Reasonable reliance. 3. The statement is later held invalid or erroneous.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Reliance on a private lawyer's advice is not enough. "Willful" tax crimes require knowledge of the legal duty (Cheek). Claim of right negates larceny.

**Authority:** MPC § 2.04(3); *Cheek v. United States*, 498 U.S. 192 (1991); *Lambert v. California*, 355 U.S. 225 (1957).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** D's lawyer tells her that a new gambling machine is legal; it is not. No defense. Had a state supreme court decision held the machine legal (later overruled), reliance would excuse her.

### R-CRM-012 — Actual causation
**Rule (write this):** For result crimes, the defendant's conduct must be the actual cause of the harm: the result would not have occurred when and as it did but for the conduct. Hastening an inevitable death is causation, and where two independent acts would each have caused the result, each is a cause.

**Elements:** 1. But-for test. 2. Acceleration counts. 3. Multiple sufficient causes → substantial factor.

**Exceptions / defenses:** If the victim would have died at the same moment anyway from an unrelated cause, there is no actual causation.

**Authority:** Common law; MPC § 2.03(1).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** D poisons V's coffee; before the poison acts, V dies of an unrelated heart attack. D is not the actual cause of death (attempted murder only).

### R-CRM-013 — Proximate causation and intervening causes
**Rule (write this):** The defendant's conduct must also be the proximate cause of the result: the result must be a natural and foreseeable consequence, not too remote or accidental to have a just bearing on liability. Foreseeable (dependent) intervening causes do not break the chain, but an unforeseeable, independent intervening cause does.

**Elements:** 1. Foreseeable type of harm. 2. Eggshell victim taken as found. 3. Dependent intervening causes (ordinary medical negligence, the victim's escape attempt, rescue efforts) do not break the chain. 4. Independent, unforeseeable causes (a new attack by a stranger; an act of God; gross medical malpractice that is the sole cause) do.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Victim's refusal of medical treatment does not break the chain.

**Authority:** Common law; MPC § 2.03(2)–(3).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** D stabs V, who dies after a surgeon negligently nicks an artery during treatment. D caused the death; ordinary malpractice is foreseeable.

### R-CRM-014 — Jurisdiction and venue
**Rule (write this):** A state may prosecute a crime if any element — the conduct or the result — occurs within its territory, or if conduct outside the state is intended to and does produce harmful effects inside it. Federal and state governments (and two states) are separate sovereigns that may each prosecute the same conduct, and trial must be in the state and district where the crime was committed.

**Elements:** 1. Conduct or result element in the state; or attempt/conspiracy outside aimed at a crime inside; or effects. 2. Sovereign's authority (federal crimes need a constitutional basis). 3. Venue and vicinage.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A city is not a separate sovereign from its state. Wrong venue does not bar retrial in the proper venue [VERIFY V-250].

**Authority:** MPC § 1.03; *Strassheim v. Daily*, 221 U.S. 280 (1911); *Gamble v. United States*, 587 U.S. 678 (2019); U.S. Const. art. III, § 2, cl. 3; amend. VI; Cal. Pen. Code §§ 27, 778a.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** D, in Nevada, mails a poisoned package to V in California, where V dies. California has jurisdiction because the result occurred there.

### R-CRM-015 — Insanity tests
**Rule (write this):** Under the M'Naghten test, a defendant is not guilty by reason of insanity if, because of a disease of the mind, he did not know the nature and quality of his act or did not know that it was wrong. Other jurisdictions add irresistible impulse (inability to control conduct), use the MPC test (lack of substantial capacity to appreciate wrongfulness or to conform conduct to law), or, in federal court, require a severe mental disease that made the defendant unable to appreciate the nature, quality or wrongfulness of his acts.

**Elements:** 1. Mental disease or defect at the time of the crime. 2. The applicable incapacity: cognitive (M'Naghten; federal), volitional (irresistible impulse), or either (MPC "substantial capacity"). 3. Causal link between the disease and the incapacity.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Voluntary intoxication alone is not insanity; settled insanity from long-term use may be. The Durham "product" test survives only in New Hampshire.

**Authority:** *M'Naghten's Case* (1843); MPC § 4.01; 18 U.S.C. § 17; *Kahler v. Kansas* (2020) [VERIFY V-250]; *Clark v. Arizona*, 548 U.S. 735 (2006); Cal. Pen. Code § 25(b) and *People v. Skinner* (1985) 39 Cal.3d 765 [S-150].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: M'Naghten unless the question names another test. Essay: state M'Naghten, then the irresistible-impulse and MPC alternatives, and apply each. California uses M'Naghten read disjunctively (either prong).

**Example:** D kills his neighbor believing God ordered him to and that the killing was morally right, though he knew it was illegal. Jurisdictions split on whether "wrong" means legal or moral wrong; under the MPC, the volitional prong may also apply.

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: PRC law exempts a mentally ill person who could not recognize or control his conduct, confirmed by legal procedure — a combined cognitive/volitional test closer to the MPC than to M'Naghten (approx.).

### R-CRM-016 — Insanity procedure; competency distinguished
**Rule (write this):** Defendants are presumed sane, and a state may require the defendant to prove insanity, usually by a preponderance (clear and convincing evidence in federal court). Insanity concerns the defendant's mental state at the time of the crime; competency to stand trial concerns his present ability to consult with counsel and to understand the proceedings.

**Elements:** 1. Burden on defendant permitted. 2. Insanity acquittee may be civilly committed until no longer mentally ill or dangerous. 3. Competency (Dusky) is a separate due-process requirement at the time of trial.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Execution of a prisoner who cannot understand the punishment is barred (Ford).

**Authority:** *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790 (1952); 18 U.S.C. § 17(b); *Jones v. United States*, 463 U.S. 354 (1983); *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402 (1960); Cal. Pen. Code §§ 25(b), 1026 [S-150].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule; California bifurcates guilt and sanity phases.

**Example:** D was psychotic when he killed V but has recovered by trial. He is competent to stand trial, and may still raise insanity.

### R-CRM-017 — Diminished capacity
**Rule (write this):** In some jurisdictions, evidence of a mental defect short of insanity is admissible to show that the defendant did not form a specific intent required for the crime, which typically reduces the offense rather than acquits. The MPC admits such evidence whenever relevant to any required mental state.

**Elements:** 1. Mental disease or defect. 2. Offered to negate a specific mental element (premeditation, intent to kill). 3. Not a complete defense in most states.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Many states reject the doctrine. California abolished the diminished-capacity defense but admits mental-disorder evidence on whether the defendant actually formed a required specific intent, premeditated, deliberated or harbored malice.

**Authority:** MPC § 4.02(1); Cal. Pen. Code §§ 25(a), 28(a) [S-150].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE rarely tests it. Essays: mention as a partial defense to first-degree murder; note California's "actual formation" rule only if asked about California law.

**Example:** D, severely depressed, shoots V. Evidence that his depression prevented reflection may reduce first-degree to second-degree murder where diminished capacity is recognized.

### R-CRM-018 — Intoxication
**Rule (write this):** Voluntary intoxication is a defense only to specific-intent crimes, and only if it prevented the defendant from forming the required intent; it is no defense to general-intent, malice or reckless crimes. Involuntary intoxication — unknowing ingestion, ingestion under duress, or an unexpected reaction to prescribed medication — is a defense to any crime if it causes an insanity-type incapacity or negates a required mental state.

**Elements:** 1. Voluntary or involuntary? 2. Voluntary: specific-intent crime + intent actually not formed. 3. Involuntary: incapacity under the jurisdiction's insanity test, or negation of mens rea.

**Exceptions / defenses:** "Dutch courage" (drinking to steel oneself for a planned crime) is no defense. MPC: self-induced unawareness of risk does not negate recklessness. A state may abolish the voluntary-intoxication defense (Egelhoff). California: voluntary intoxication can negate specific intent, premeditation, deliberation or express malice, but not implied malice.

**Authority:** MPC § 2.08; *Montana v. Egelhoff*, 518 U.S. 37 (1996); Cal. Pen. Code § 29.4 [S-361].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule. On essays, flag the crime category before applying intoxication.

**Example:** Drunk, D breaks into a house intending to sleep, then takes a TV. Intoxication may negate the intent required for burglary (specific intent at entry), but voluntary intoxication is no defense to a battery he commits there.

### R-CRM-019 — Infancy
**Rule (write this):** At common law, a child under 7 is conclusively incapable of crime, a child from 7 to 14 is rebuttably presumed incapable, and a person 14 or older is treated as an adult. Modern statutes send most offenses by minors to juvenile court.

**Elements:** 1. Age at the time of the act. 2. For 7–14, the prosecution must rebut the presumption with proof the child understood wrongfulness.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Statutes may allow transfer of older juveniles to adult court.

**Authority:** Common law; Cal. Pen. Code § 26 (under 14 incapable absent clear proof the child knew the act's wrongfulness).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** A 12-year-old hides his tracks after taking a bicycle. The concealment helps rebut the presumption of incapacity.

### R-CRM-020 — Self-defense
**Rule (write this):** A person may use non-deadly force when he reasonably believes it is necessary to protect himself from the imminent use of unlawful force, and may use deadly force only when he reasonably believes he faces imminent death or serious bodily injury. The force used must be proportional, and an initial aggressor generally may not claim self-defense.

**Elements:** 1. Honest and reasonable belief in an imminent threat of unlawful force. 2. Force reasonably necessary and proportional (deadly only against death or serious bodily injury; MPC adds kidnapping and forcible sexual intercourse). 3. Not the initial aggressor (R-CRM-022). 4. Retreat where required (R-CRM-021).

**Exceptions / defenses:** An honest but unreasonable belief → imperfect self-defense (R-CRM-023). Fear of a future (non-imminent) attack is not enough.

**Authority:** Common law; MPC § 3.04; Cal. Pen. Code § 197.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** V lunges at D with a knife; D shoots V. Deadly force is justified: D reasonably feared imminent death.

### R-CRM-021 — Duty to retreat and castle doctrine
**Rule (write this):** Under the majority rule, a non-aggressor need not retreat before using deadly force. In a minority of jurisdictions and under the MPC, a person must retreat before using deadly force if he knows he can do so in complete safety, except that he need not retreat from his own home.

**Elements:** 1. Deadly force (no retreat duty ever applies to non-deadly force). 2. Retreat jurisdiction? 3. Complete safety known to be available. 4. Not in one's home (castle exception).

**Exceptions / defenses:** MPC removes the castle exception if the defender was the initial aggressor or is attacked at work by a co-worker. California does not require retreat and presumes reasonable fear against an unlawful, forcible intruder into the residence.

**Authority:** MPC § 3.04(2)(b)(ii); Cal. Pen. Code § 198.5; CALCRIM No. 505 [S-367].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: no duty to retreat unless the question says the jurisdiction requires it. Essay: state the majority no-retreat rule and the minority/MPC retreat rule, and apply both.

**Example:** In a parking lot, V pulls a knife on D; D could safely step into his car and drive off but shoots V. Justified under the majority rule; not justified in a retreat jurisdiction.

### R-CRM-022 — Initial aggressor
**Rule (write this):** An initial aggressor may use force in self-defense only if he withdraws in good faith and communicates the withdrawal to the other party, or if the victim of his non-deadly aggression suddenly escalates to deadly force and the aggressor cannot safely withdraw.

**Elements:** 1. Who started the conflict? 2. Withdrawal + communication; or 3. Sudden escalation by the victim to deadly force.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Words alone generally do not make one an aggressor; provoking a fight with the intent to kill (manufactured self-defense) forfeits the defense.

**Authority:** Common law; MPC § 3.04(2)(b)(i).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** D punches V; V pulls a gun. D, unable to flee, stabs V. D may claim self-defense because V escalated to deadly force.

### R-CRM-023 — Imperfect self-defense
**Rule (write this):** A defendant who kills with an honest but unreasonable belief that deadly force was necessary, or who was the initial aggressor, cannot claim complete self-defense, but in many jurisdictions the killing is reduced from murder to voluntary manslaughter.

**Elements:** 1. Actual belief in the need for deadly force. 2. Belief unreasonable (or defendant at fault in creating the situation). 3. Result: voluntary manslaughter.

**Exceptions / defenses:** The MPC reaches a similar result through § 3.09(2): a reckless or negligent belief supports conviction only for crimes of recklessness or negligence.

**Authority:** Common law; MPC § 3.09(2); *In re Christian S.* (1994) 7 Cal.4th 768; *People v. Flannel* (1979) 25 Cal.3d 668.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same approach; the MBE tests it as the reason a killing is manslaughter rather than murder.

**Example:** D, frightened by a neighbor's long feud, shoots him when he reaches into his pocket for keys. If D honestly but unreasonably feared a gun, the killing is voluntary manslaughter.

### R-CRM-024 — Defense of others
**Rule (write this):** A person may use force to defend another when he reasonably believes the other person would be justified in using that force in self-defense. No special relationship is required under the modern rule.

**Elements:** 1. Reasonable belief that the third person faces imminent unlawful force. 2. Force the third person could use. 3. Proportionality.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Under the older "alter ego" rule, the defender has no defense if the person defended had no right of self-defense (for example, was the aggressor), however reasonable the defender's belief.

**Authority:** MPC § 3.05; common law.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Majority reasonable-belief rule on both.

**Example:** D sees a man in plain clothes wrestling a teenager to the ground and pulls him off; the man was an undercover officer making a lawful arrest. Under the majority rule, D has a defense if his belief was reasonable.

### R-CRM-025 — Defense of property and dwelling
**Rule (write this):** A person may use reasonable non-deadly force to prevent or end an unlawful interference with property, but never deadly force solely to protect property. Deadly force may be used to prevent an unlawful entry into a dwelling only where the occupant reasonably believes it necessary to prevent a violent entry and that the intruder intends a felony or personal attack inside.

**Elements:** 1. Unlawful interference. 2. Request to desist unless futile or dangerous. 3. Non-deadly force for property; deadly force only under the dwelling or self-defense rules. 4. Recapture only in hot pursuit.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Spring guns and other deadly mechanical devices are not permitted.

**Authority:** Common law; MPC § 3.06; *People v. Ceballos* (1974) 12 Cal.3d 470.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** D rigs a shotgun to fire at anyone opening his garage; it wounds a teenage burglar. D is guilty of assault (or battery): deadly force cannot protect property.

### R-CRM-026 — Law enforcement, arrest and crime prevention
**Rule (write this):** A police officer may use reasonable force to make an arrest, but deadly force against a fleeing suspect only if necessary to prevent escape and the officer has probable cause to believe the suspect poses a significant threat of death or serious physical injury. A private person may use reasonable non-deadly force to prevent a felony or breach of the peace, and deadly force only to prevent a dangerous felony or to arrest one who actually committed it.

**Elements:** 1. Lawful purpose (arrest, preventing crime). 2. Force reasonably necessary. 3. Deadly-force limits (Garner for police).

**Exceptions / defenses:** The modern rule bars using force to resist an arrest by a known police officer, even if the arrest is unlawful.

**Authority:** *Tennessee v. Garner*, 471 U.S. 1 (1985); MPC §§ 3.04(2)(a)(i), 3.07; Cal. Pen. Code § 834a.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** An officer shoots an unarmed teenager fleeing a burglary. Under Garner, the force was unreasonable; the officer has no justification.

### R-CRM-027 — Necessity
**Rule (write this):** Conduct that would otherwise be criminal is justified if the defendant reasonably believed it was necessary to avoid an imminent harm greater than the harm caused, there was no reasonable legal alternative, and the defendant did not create the danger. Necessity is not a defense to intentional homicide at common law.

**Elements:** 1. Imminent threatened harm. 2. Harm avoided > harm caused. 3. No reasonable legal alternative. 4. Defendant not at fault in creating the situation. 5. Legislature has not precluded the defense.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Traditionally the threat must come from natural forces (human threats = duress); the MPC drops this. Economic necessity and civil disobedience fail. Escapees must try to surrender once the necessity ends.

**Authority:** MPC § 3.02; *Regina v. Dudley and Stephens*, 14 Q.B.D. 273 (1884); *United States v. Bailey*, 444 U.S. 394 (1980).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** Caught in a blizzard, D breaks into a vacant cabin to avoid freezing. Necessity justifies the entry (but D may owe compensation for the damage).

### R-CRM-028 — Duress
**Rule (write this):** Duress excuses a crime committed because of a threat of imminent death or serious bodily injury to the defendant or another person, where a person of reasonable firmness would have yielded and the defendant had no reasonable opportunity to escape. At common law and in most states, duress is not a defense to intentional murder.

**Elements:** 1. Threat by a human of imminent death or serious bodily harm. 2. Reasonable fear. 3. No reasonable escape. 4. Defendant did not recklessly place himself in the situation. 5. Crime is not intentional killing (majority).

**Exceptions / defenses:** MPC: a person of reasonable firmness standard, available for all crimes including murder. Duress may defeat the felony underlying felony murder in some jurisdictions. Federal defendants prove duress by a preponderance.

**Authority:** MPC § 2.09; *Dixon v. United States*, 548 U.S. 1 (2006); Cal. Pen. Code § 26; *People v. Anderson* (2002) 28 Cal.4th 767.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: no defense to murder. Essay: majority rule plus MPC contrast.

**Example:** A gang tells D they will kill his daughter unless he drives the getaway car. Duress excuses the robbery; it would not excuse D for personally killing a guard.

### R-CRM-029 — Entrapment
**Rule (write this):** Under the majority subjective test, entrapment is a defense if government agents induced the defendant to commit a crime he was not predisposed to commit. Under the objective test used by the MPC and California, the question is whether the police conduct would induce a normally law-abiding person to commit the crime, regardless of the defendant's predisposition.

**Elements:** Subjective: 1. Inducement by a government agent (more than offering an opportunity). 2. Lack of predisposition before contact with the government. Objective: 1. Police conduct likely to induce a normally law-abiding person.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Private inducement is not entrapment. Offering an ordinary opportunity is not inducement. Outrageous-government-conduct due-process claims almost never succeed.

**Authority:** *Sorrells v. United States*, 287 U.S. 435 (1932); *Sherman v. United States*, 356 U.S. 369 (1958); *Jacobson v. United States*, 503 U.S. 540 (1992); *Mathews v. United States*, 485 U.S. 58 (1988); MPC § 2.13; *People v. Barraza* (1979) 23 Cal.3d 675.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: subjective test. Essay: state both and apply both; California uses the objective test if the call asks for California law.

**Example:** An informant pleads for weeks, invoking friendship and his own drug withdrawal, until D (no prior drug history) sells him heroin. Entrapment likely under both tests.

### R-CRM-030 — Consent
**Rule (write this):** The victim's consent is a defense only when lack of consent is an element of the crime or when the harm is minor or part of a recognized lawful activity, such as contact sports or medical treatment. Consent is never a defense to murder or serious bodily injury and is ineffective if given by one legally incapable or obtained by force or fraud in the factum.

**Elements:** 1. Crime where consent negates an element, or minor harm. 2. Consent voluntary, by a person with capacity. 3. Within the scope of what was consented to.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Forgiveness after the crime (condonation) and the victim's contributory negligence are no defenses.

**Authority:** MPC § 2.11; common law.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** A boxer's punch within the rules is not battery; a punch after the bell may be.

## B. Homicide (outline I)

### R-CRM-031 — Homicide framework and malice aforethought
**Rule (write this):** Murder is the unlawful killing of a human being with malice aforethought. Malice exists if the defendant intended to kill, intended to inflict serious bodily injury, acted with a depraved heart (extreme reckless indifference to human life), or killed during an inherently dangerous felony; an unlawful killing without malice is manslaughter.

**Elements:** 1. Unlawful killing (no justification). 2. Of a human being. 3. Caused by the defendant (actual and proximate cause). 4. With one of the four malice states.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Justification (self-defense) makes the killing lawful. Provocation or imperfect self-defense reduces murder to voluntary manslaughter.

**Authority:** Common law; Cal. Pen. Code §§ 187, 188; MPC §§ 210.1–210.4.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule. On essays, discuss each plausible malice state, then the degree, then manslaughter alternatives.

**Example:** D swings a baseball bat at V's head, killing him. Malice (intent to kill or to cause serious bodily injury) is present; the killing is murder unless justified or mitigated.

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: PRC law splits intentional homicide (故意杀人) from negligent homicide (过失致人死亡); "malice aforethought" is broader, covering some killings PRC law treats as intentional injury causing death (approx.).

### R-CRM-032 — Intent-to-kill (express-malice) murder
**Rule (write this):** A killing committed with the intent to kill — the purpose of causing death or knowledge that death is practically certain — is murder unless justified, excused or mitigated. The jury may infer intent to kill from the intentional use of a deadly weapon aimed at a vital part of the body.

**Elements:** 1. Purpose or knowledge as to death. 2. Death caused. 3. No justification or mitigation.

**Exceptions / defenses:** The deadly-weapon inference is permissive only; a mandatory presumption of intent violates due process (Sandstrom). Motive (mercy) does not negate intent.

**Authority:** Common law; MPC § 210.2(1)(a); Cal. Pen. Code § 188(a)(1); *Sandstrom v. Montana*, 442 U.S. 510 (1979).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** D fires a pistol at V's chest from five feet. The jury may infer intent to kill.

### R-CRM-033 — First-degree murder: premeditation and deliberation
**Rule (write this):** A murder is first-degree if it was willful, deliberate and premeditated — the defendant reflected on the decision to kill, even briefly, and made it with a cool mind — or if a statute so classifies it by its means (poison, lying in wait, torture) or because it occurred during an enumerated felony. All other murders are second-degree.

**Elements:** 1. Intent to kill. 2. Premeditation (thought beforehand). 3. Deliberation (cool reflection). Evidence: planning, motive, manner of killing.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Courts differ on how much time is needed ("no time too short" vs. appreciable time). Intoxication and mental-disorder evidence may negate premeditation. The MPC has no degrees of murder.

**Authority:** Cal. Pen. Code § 189(a) [S-357] [VERIFY V-253]; *People v. Anderson* (1968) 70 Cal.2d 15; MPC § 210.2.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: look for planning or lying in wait. Essay: apply the planning/motive/manner factors and argue both sides.

**Example:** D buys rope and a shovel, waits for V outside her apartment and strangles her. Planning and manner show premeditation and deliberation: first-degree murder.

### R-CRM-034 — Voluntary manslaughter: heat of passion
**Rule (write this):** An intentional killing is voluntary manslaughter if the defendant acted in a sudden heat of passion caused by adequate provocation — provocation that would cause a reasonable person to lose self-control — and killed before a reasonable person would have cooled off and before he actually cooled off.

**Elements:** 1. Adequate (objectively sufficient) provocation. 2. Actually provoked. 3. No reasonable cooling time. 4. Did not actually cool.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Words alone are not adequate provocation (majority). MPC: extreme mental or emotional disturbance with a reasonable explanation, judged from the actor's situation as he believed it to be — broader, no fixed cooling rule. California: provocation that would cause an ordinary person of average disposition to act rashly, from passion rather than judgment.

**Authority:** Common law; MPC § 210.3(1)(b); Cal. Pen. Code § 192(a); *People v. Beltran* (2013) 56 Cal.4th 935; *People v. Berry* (1976) 18 Cal.3d 509.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: common-law categories (serious battery, mutual combat, adultery discovered). Essay: common law, then the MPC EMED alternative.

**Example:** D returns home, finds his spouse in bed with V and immediately shoots V. Voluntary manslaughter. If D had waited three days and then killed V, cooling time would bar mitigation.

### R-CRM-035 — Intent to inflict serious bodily injury
**Rule (write this):** A killing is murder if the defendant intended to inflict serious bodily injury on the victim and death resulted, even though he did not intend to kill.

**Elements:** 1. Intent to cause serious (grievous) bodily injury — a substantial risk of death, permanent disfigurement, or loss of a bodily function. 2. Death caused.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Intent to cause only minor injury does not qualify (consider misdemeanor manslaughter instead). Attempted murder cannot rest on this mental state.

**Authority:** Common law; Cal. Pen. Code § 188(a)(2) (implied malice).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** D kicks V repeatedly in the ribs to "teach him a lesson"; V dies of a punctured lung. Second-degree murder.

### R-CRM-036 — Depraved-heart murder
**Rule (write this):** A killing is murder if the defendant acted with a depraved heart — conscious disregard of an extremely high and unjustifiable risk of death, showing extreme indifference to the value of human life.

**Elements:** 1. Conduct creating a very high risk of death. 2. Defendant aware of the risk (most courts). 3. No social justification. 4. Death caused.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A lesser risk or lack of awareness → involuntary manslaughter (R-CRM-037). Voluntary intoxication does not negate this mental state.

**Authority:** Common law; MPC § 210.2(1)(b); *People v. Knoller* (2007) 41 Cal.4th 139; *People v. Watson* (1981) 30 Cal.3d 290.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** As a prank, D fires a rifle into a passing passenger train, killing a rider. Depraved-heart murder.

### R-CRM-037 — Involuntary manslaughter: criminal negligence
**Rule (write this):** An unintentional killing is involuntary manslaughter if the defendant acted with criminal negligence — a gross deviation from the standard of care of a reasonable person that creates a substantial and unjustifiable risk of death or serious injury. Ordinary civil negligence is not enough.

**Elements:** 1. Substantial, unjustifiable risk. 2. Gross deviation from reasonable care. 3. Awareness required in some jurisdictions (recklessness); objective in others. 4. Death caused.

**Exceptions / defenses:** MPC splits reckless killings (manslaughter) from negligent ones (negligent homicide).

**Authority:** Common law; MPC §§ 210.3(1)(a), 210.4; Cal. Pen. Code § 192(b); *Commonwealth v. Welansky*, 316 Mass. 383 (1944).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule. On essays, contrast with depraved-heart murder.

**Example:** A day-care owner leaves a toddler in a hot car for an hour while running errands; the child dies. Involuntary manslaughter (possibly depraved-heart murder if she knew of the extreme risk).

### R-CRM-038 — MPC homicide scheme
**Rule (write this):** Under the MPC, criminal homicide is murder if committed purposely or knowingly, or recklessly under circumstances manifesting extreme indifference to human life; manslaughter if committed recklessly or under extreme mental or emotional disturbance for which there is a reasonable explanation or excuse; and negligent homicide if committed negligently.

**Elements:** 1. Identify the culpability for death. 2. Murder (§ 210.2); manslaughter (§ 210.3); negligent homicide (§ 210.4). 3. EMED reduces murder to manslaughter.

**Exceptions / defenses:** No felony-murder rule; extreme indifference is presumed if the actor is engaged in robbery, rape, arson, burglary, kidnapping or felonious escape (§ 210.2(1)(b)).

**Authority:** MPC §§ 210.1–210.4.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE uses it only when the question says the jurisdiction follows the MPC. Essays: one comparative sentence.

**Example:** D, a hunter, carelessly fires at a rustle in the bushes without looking, killing a hiker. If D did not perceive the risk: negligent homicide; if he did and ignored it: manslaughter.

### R-CRM-039 — Felony murder: basic rule
**Rule (write this):** A killing that occurs during the commission or attempted commission of an inherently dangerous felony is murder, even if accidental, because the intent to commit the felony supplies malice. Statutes typically make killings during burglary, arson, robbery, rape and kidnapping first-degree murder.

**Elements:** 1. Defendant committed or attempted a qualifying felony (enumerated, or inherently dangerous). 2. A death resulted. 3. During the felony (attempt through immediate flight). 4. Causation and the limits in R-CRM-040 and R-CRM-041.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Inherently dangerous judged in the abstract (California) or as committed (some states). A defense to the felony defeats felony murder.

**Authority:** Common law; Cal. Pen. Code § 189(a), (e) [S-357]; MPC § 210.2(1)(b) (presumption only).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: common-law felony murder. Essay: general rule, then (if the call allows) California's § 189(e) limits (R-CRM-043).

**Example:** During a burglary, D's flashlight falls and starts a fire that kills a sleeping resident. Felony murder.

### R-CRM-040 — Felony murder: limitations
**Rule (write this):** The predicate felony must be independent of the killing, so assault-type felonies merge and cannot support felony murder. The killing must occur during the felony, from attempt through immediate flight to a place of temporary safety, and must be a foreseeable result of it, and the defendant must be guilty of the underlying felony.

**Elements:** 1. Independent felony (no merger). 2. Temporal link (ends at temporary safety). 3. Causal link (foreseeable death; victim taken as found). 4. Guilt of the felony.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Killings after the felon reaches temporary safety are outside the rule. Felony murder applies even to an unforeseeable heart attack in many courts if the felony caused it.

**Authority:** Common law (majority rule; the merger and in-furtherance limits are general doctrine tested on the MBE); California illustrations: *People v. Ireland* (1969) 70 Cal.2d 522; *People v. Chun* (2009) 45 Cal.4th 1172; *People v. Stamp* (1969) 2 Cal.App.3d 203.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** D commits aggravated assault on V with a club, killing him. The assault merges; felony murder does not apply, but intent-to-injure murder may.

### R-CRM-041 — Felony murder: killings by non-felons; co-felon deaths
**Rule (write this):** Under the majority agency theory, felony murder applies only to killings committed by a felon or an accomplice in furtherance of the felony, not to killings by victims, police or bystanders. Under the minority proximate-cause theory, felons are liable for any death that is a foreseeable result of the felony, though many such courts exclude the justified killing of a co-felon.

**Elements:** 1. Who fired the fatal shot? 2. Agency: a felon → liability; a non-felon → none. 3. Proximate cause: foreseeable resistance → liability (subject to the co-felon exclusion).

**Exceptions / defenses:** The provocative-act doctrine may support malice murder where a felon's own malicious act provokes the lethal response.

**Authority:** *State v. Canola*, 73 N.J. 206 (1977); *People v. Washington* (1965) 62 Cal.2d 777; *People v. Gilbert* (1965) 63 Cal.2d 690 [VERIFY V-251].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: agency rule unless told otherwise. Essay: present both theories and conclude under each (Feb 2022 Q1: customer and co-felon deaths).

**Example:** A police officer shoots at fleeing robber A and kills robber B. Under the agency rule, A is not guilty of felony murder for B's death.

### R-CRM-042 — Misdemeanor manslaughter (unlawful-act manslaughter)
**Rule (write this):** An unintended killing during the commission of an unlawful act that is not a qualifying felony is involuntary manslaughter. Many jurisdictions limit the rule to acts that are malum in se or that are the proximate, foreseeable cause of death.

**Elements:** 1. Unlawful act (misdemeanor or non-dangerous felony). 2. Death caused. 3. Limits: malum in se or dangerous; proximate cause.

**Exceptions / defenses:** The MPC rejects the doctrine.

**Authority:** Common law; Cal. Pen. Code § 192(b).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** D shoves V in an argument (simple battery); V falls, strikes his head and dies. Involuntary manslaughter.

### R-CRM-043 — California felony murder after SB 1437
**Rule (write this):** In California, a participant in an enumerated felony in which a death occurs is liable for murder only if he was the actual killer, aided or abetted the actual killer in first-degree murder with intent to kill, or was a major participant in the felony who acted with reckless indifference to human life. Malice may not be imputed to a person based solely on his participation in a crime.

**Elements:** 1. Enumerated felony (§ 189(a)). 2. Death occurred. 3. Defendant is (a) actual killer, (b) intent-to-kill aider, or (c) major participant + reckless indifference (Banks/Clark factors: planning role, supplying weapons, presence at the scene, ability to prevent the killing, knowledge of a co-felon's propensity for violence).

**Exceptions / defenses:** The limits do not apply where the victim was a peace officer killed in the performance of duty and the defendant knew or reasonably should have known it (§ 189(f)) [VERIFY V-253]. Natural and probable consequences cannot support murder (§ 188(a)(3); Gentile). Whether second-degree felony murder survives is unsettled [VERIFY V-252]. Persons convicted under abolished theories may petition for resentencing (§ 1172.6).

**Authority:** Cal. Pen. Code §§ 188(a)(3), 189(e)–(f), 1172.6 [S-357] [S-360]; *People v. Strong* (2022) [S-358]; *People v. Gentile* (2020) [S-359]; *People v. Banks* (2015) 61 Cal.4th 788; *People v. Clark* (2016) 63 Cal.4th 522 [VERIFY V-251].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Never on the MBE. On essays, add it only if the call asks for California law; otherwise one sentence noting California's narrower rule.

**Example:** D is the unarmed getaway driver in a robbery during which his partner unexpectedly shoots a clerk. Under § 189(e), D is not guilty of murder unless he was a major participant who acted with reckless indifference to human life (likely not on these facts).

## C. Other crimes (outline II)

### R-CRM-044 — Larceny
**Rule (write this):** Larceny is the trespassory taking and carrying away of the tangible personal property of another with the intent to permanently deprive the owner of it. The intent must exist at the time of the taking, except that a wrongful taking without that intent becomes larceny when the intent is later formed (continuing trespass).

**Elements:** 1. Trespassory (without consent). 2. Taking (caption) from one with possession — an employee with mere custody who takes commits larceny. 3. Carrying away (slight movement). 4. Tangible personal property. 5. Of another. 6. Intent to permanently deprive (or to keep for an unreasonable time or create a substantial risk of loss).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Claim of right (good-faith belief the property is one's own, or in many states taking to satisfy a debt) negates intent. Intent to borrow and return is not larceny. Intent to repay equivalent money is no defense. A finder who intends to keep property and knows how to find the owner commits larceny.

**Authority:** Common law; Cal. Pen. Code § 484(a); MPC § 223.2.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Common-law elements on both; on essays, add one line that modern codes consolidate theft (R-CRM-047).

**Example:** D takes V's bicycle without permission, planning to ride it for an hour and return it; later that day he decides to keep it. Larceny under continuing trespass, because the original taking was wrongful.

### R-CRM-045 — Embezzlement
**Rule (write this):** Embezzlement is the fraudulent conversion of the property of another by a person in lawful possession of it. Conversion is a serious act of interference with the owner's rights, such as selling, spending or keeping the property.

**Elements:** 1. Lawful possession (not mere custody) — trustee, agent, bailee. 2. Property of another. 3. Conversion. 4. Intent to defraud.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Intent to return the exact property is a defense; intent to repay with equivalent money is not (unless authorized to use the funds). Claim of right negates intent.

**Authority:** Common law; Cal. Pen. Code § 503; MPC § 223.8.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule. The MBE tests custody (larceny) vs. possession (embezzlement).

**Example:** A lawyer holding a client's settlement in trust uses it to pay his own mortgage, intending to repay next month. Embezzlement.

### R-CRM-046 — False pretenses and larceny by trick
**Rule (write this):** False pretenses is obtaining title to the property of another by a knowing false representation of a past or present material fact, made with intent to defraud, that causes the victim to pass title. If the fraud obtains only possession, not title, the crime is larceny by trick.

**Elements:** 1. False representation of a past or present fact. 2. Knowledge of falsity. 3. Intent to defraud. 4. Victim relies. 5. Title passes (false pretenses) or only possession passes (larceny by trick).

**Exceptions / defenses:** A false promise of future conduct was not false pretenses at common law; the MPC includes it but bars inferring deception from mere non-performance. If the victim was not deceived, the crime is attempt.

**Authority:** Common law; MPC § 223.3; Cal. Pen. Code § 532.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** D pays for a TV with a check he knows will bounce. The store passes title: false pretenses. If D had "test-driven" a car and never returned it, he obtained possession only: larceny by trick.

### R-CRM-047 — Theft consolidation and grading
**Rule (write this):** Modern codes consolidate larceny, embezzlement, false pretenses and similar offenses into a single crime of theft, graded mainly by the value of the property. In California, theft of property worth more than $950 is generally grand theft, and most thefts of $950 or less are petty theft.

**Elements:** 1. Any recognized form of unlawful taking. 2. Value determines grade. 3. California: aggregation of multiple thefts permitted for the felony threshold, and repeat-theft felony charging (Proposition 36, 2024).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Consolidation does not change the underlying elements; the prosecution still proves one of the theft forms.

**Authority:** MPC § 223.1; Cal. Pen. Code §§ 484(a), 487, 490.2, 490.3, 666.1 [S-362] [S-363] [VERIFY V-257].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE tests the common-law crimes. Essay: name the common-law crime, then note consolidation in one sentence.

**Example:** An employee takes $400 a week from the till for three weeks. Under California's aggregation rule, the $1,200 total can be charged as grand theft.

### R-CRM-048 — Receiving stolen property
**Rule (write this):** Receiving stolen property is receiving possession or control of stolen property, knowing it is stolen, with intent to permanently deprive the owner of it. If the property is no longer stolen when received — because police recovered it and used it in a sting — the defendant is guilty of attempted receipt under the modern view.

**Elements:** 1. Receipt of possession or control (including arranging a sale or holding for another). 2. Property is stolen at the time of receipt. 3. Knowledge (or belief) that it is stolen. 4. Intent to permanently deprive.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Mere suspicion is not knowledge. Traditional courts treated the sting scenario as legal impossibility (no attempt); the MPC and most courts reject that defense.

**Authority:** Common law; Cal. Pen. Code § 496; MPC §§ 223.6, 5.01(1); *People v. Rojas* (1961) 55 Cal.2d 252 [VERIFY V-251].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: modern view (attempt) unless told common law. Essay: analyze receiving, then attempt and impossibility (Feb 2013 Q1).

**Example:** Police recover a stolen painting, then let a cooperating thief deliver it to D, who pays believing it stolen. D is not guilty of receiving (no longer stolen) but is guilty of attempted receipt.

### R-CRM-049 — Robbery (and extortion distinguished)
**Rule (write this):** Robbery is larceny of property from the victim's person or presence, accomplished by force or by intimidation — a threat of immediate harm. Extortion, by contrast, obtains property by a threat of future harm.

**Elements:** 1. All larceny elements. 2. From the person or presence (within reach or control). 3. Force beyond what is needed to take the property, or intimidation (threat of immediate bodily harm to the victim, family or someone present). 4. Force or fear used to obtain the property (common law); many statutes and California also cover force used to retain it or escape.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Pickpocketing without the victim's awareness is larceny, not robbery. Claim of right negates robbery in many jurisdictions. Robbery merges the included larceny and assault.

**Authority:** Common law; Cal. Pen. Code § 211; *People v. Estes* (1983) 147 Cal.App.3d 23; *People v. Gomez* (2008) 43 Cal.4th 249 [VERIFY V-251]; MPC §§ 222.1, 223.4.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: common-law timing (force before or during the taking). Essay: common law, then the modern/California retention rule.

**Example:** D pockets a watch in a store; when a guard grabs him at the door, D punches the guard and escapes. Larceny at common law (force came after the taking); robbery under the modern/California rule.

### R-CRM-050 — Burglary (common law)
**Rule (write this):** Common-law burglary is the breaking and entering of the dwelling of another at nighttime with the intent to commit a felony inside. The intent must exist at the time of entry.

**Elements:** 1. Breaking (actual: creating or enlarging an opening by even slight force; or constructive: fraud, threat). 2. Entering (any part of the body or a tool used to commit the felony). 3. Dwelling (place regularly slept in, including curtilage structures). 4. Of another (occupancy). 5. Nighttime. 6. Intent to commit a felony therein at entry.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Entry through an open door is not a breaking. Intent formed after entry is not burglary. Consent to enter defeats breaking (unless obtained by fraud).

**Authority:** Common law.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: common-law elements unless a statute is given. Essay: list them, then the modern relaxation (R-CRM-051).

**Example:** At 11 p.m., D pushes open a neighbor's unlocked window, climbs in and steals a laptop. Common-law burglary.

### R-CRM-051 — Burglary (modern statutes, MPC, California)
**Rule (write this):** Modern statutes define burglary as entering any building or structure (and often vehicles) at any time with intent to commit a felony or theft inside, usually without any breaking requirement. California punishes entry into listed structures with intent to commit larceny or any felony, makes burglary of an inhabited dwelling first degree, and treats entry into an open store during business hours to steal $950 or less as shoplifting.

**Elements:** 1. Entry. 2. Building or structure (broad). 3. Intent to commit a felony or theft (MPC: any crime) at entry.

**Exceptions / defenses:** MPC excludes premises open to the public and entries by those licensed or privileged. Burglary does not merge with the crime committed inside.

**Authority:** MPC § 221.1; Cal. Pen. Code §§ 459, 460, 459.5 [S-368].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Essay: state common law and modern rule; apply both.

**Example:** At noon, D walks through the open door of an office intending to steal a phone. Not common-law burglary (no breaking, not dwelling, daytime) but burglary under modern statutes.

### R-CRM-052 — Assault
**Rule (write this):** Assault is either an attempted battery or the intentional creation of a reasonable apprehension of imminent bodily harm in another. In California, assault is an unlawful attempt, coupled with a present ability, to commit a violent injury on another.

**Elements:** Attempted battery: 1. Intent to commit a battery. 2. Act beyond preparation. 3. (California) present ability. Apprehension: 1. Intent to cause apprehension. 2. Victim actually and reasonably apprehends imminent harm.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Words alone do not create apprehension assault. The victim need not be aware for attempted-battery assault. California has no apprehension-type criminal assault; its mental state is knowledge of facts that would lead a reasonable person to realize a battery would directly and probably result.

**Authority:** Common law; MPC § 211.1; Cal. Pen. Code § 240; *People v. Williams* (2001) 26 Cal.4th 779.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: both forms. Essay: both forms; note California's attempted-battery-only definition if asked.

**Example:** D swings at a sleeping V and misses. Attempted-battery assault (V was unaware, so not apprehension assault).

### R-CRM-053 — Battery and aggravated forms
**Rule (write this):** Battery is the unlawful application of force to another person resulting in bodily injury or an offensive touching. It is a general-intent crime satisfied by intentional, reckless or (in many states) criminally negligent conduct; aggravated battery and assault involve a deadly weapon, serious bodily injury, intent to commit a further felony, or a protected victim.

**Elements:** 1. Application of force (direct or indirect). 2. Injury or offensive touching. 3. Unlawful (no consent or justification). 4. General intent / recklessness.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Consent (sports, medical); self-defense; defense of others.

**Authority:** Common law; MPC § 211.1(2); Cal. Pen. Code §§ 242, 245.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** D throws a glass at V in a bar, cutting her face. Battery; aggravated if the glass is treated as a deadly weapon or the cut is serious bodily injury.

### R-CRM-054 — Rape
**Rule (write this):** Rape is sexual intercourse (penetration) with another person without consent, accomplished by force or threat or with a victim incapable of consent. Modern statutes are gender-neutral, abolish the marital exemption, and in many states require only lack of consent rather than force beyond the act itself.

**Elements:** 1. Sexual intercourse/penetration. 2. Without consent. 3. By force, threat, or with a victim unable to consent (unconscious, intoxicated to incapacity, mentally incapable). 4. General intent.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Fraud in the factum vitiates consent; fraud in the inducement generally does not. An honest and reasonable belief in consent is a defense in many jurisdictions.

**Authority:** Common law; Cal. Pen. Code § 261 [VERIFY V-258]; *People v. Mayberry* (1975) 15 Cal.3d 143; MPC § 213.1.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** A doctor tells a patient he is inserting a medical instrument but instead has intercourse with her. Fraud in the factum: rape.

### R-CRM-055 — Statutory rape
**Rule (write this):** Statutory rape is sexual intercourse with a person under the statutory age of consent. It is a strict-liability crime in most jurisdictions: the minor's consent and a reasonable mistake about age are no defense.

**Elements:** 1. Intercourse. 2. Victim under the age of consent. 3. Not the defendant's spouse (where statutes so provide).

**Exceptions / defenses:** MPC: reasonable belief the child was above the critical age is a defense if that age is above 10. California: a reasonable, good-faith mistake of age is a defense to unlawful sexual intercourse but not to lewd acts with a child under 14. The minor cannot be an accomplice or co-conspirator.

**Authority:** Common law; MPC § 213.6(1); Cal. Pen. Code § 261.5; *People v. Hernandez* (1964) 61 Cal.2d 529; *People v. Olsen* (1984) 36 Cal.3d 638.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: strict liability. Essay: majority strict liability; add California's mistake defense only if asked.

**Example:** D, 20, has intercourse with V, 15, who showed a fake ID saying 19. Guilty in most states; under California law D may raise a reasonable mistake of age.

### R-CRM-056 — Kidnapping and false imprisonment
**Rule (write this):** Kidnapping is the unlawful confinement of a person against his will, coupled with either moving the person or concealing him in a secret place. False imprisonment is the lesser crime of unlawfully confining a person without consent, with no movement or concealment required.

**Elements:** 1. Unlawful confinement. 2. Without consent (consent by fraud or force ineffective; a child's consent ineffective). 3. Movement (asportation) or secret concealment. 4. General intent.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Consent of a competent adult; lawful authority (arrest, parent). Movement merely incidental to another crime may not suffice (R-CRM-057).

**Authority:** Common law; MPC §§ 212.1, 212.3; Cal. Pen. Code §§ 207, 236; *People v. Martinez* (1999) 20 Cal.4th 225.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** D forces V into his car at gunpoint and drives 10 miles to an empty lot. Kidnapping.

### R-CRM-057 — Aggravated kidnapping and incidental movement
**Rule (write this):** Kidnapping is aggravated when committed for ransom, to facilitate a felony or flight, to inflict injury or terrorize, to interfere with a governmental function, or against a child. Movement that is merely incidental to another crime does not support kidnapping unless it is substantial and independently increases the risk of harm or decreases the chance of detection.

**Elements:** 1. Kidnapping. 2. Aggravating purpose or victim. 3. Movement or confinement beyond what is incidental to the other crime.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Brief movement within premises during a robbery is usually incidental.

**Authority:** MPC § 212.1; Cal. Pen. Code § 209; *People v. Daniels* (1969) 71 Cal.2d 1119.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule; essays pair it with attempt (Feb 2018 Q4).

**Example:** During a bank robbery, D herds tellers 10 feet into the vault room. Incidental; no kidnapping. Driving a teller across town as a hostage would be aggravated kidnapping.

### R-CRM-058 — Arson
**Rule (write this):** Common-law arson is the malicious burning of the dwelling of another. Burning requires some charring of the structure, and malice means intent to burn or reckless disregard of an obvious risk of burning; modern statutes extend arson to other structures, to one's own property burned to defraud an insurer, and to explosions.

**Elements:** 1. Burning (charring, not scorching or smoke damage). 2. Dwelling (modern: any structure). 3. Of another (occupancy). 4. Malice (intent or recklessness).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Arson is not a specific-intent crime; voluntary intoxication is no defense. Mere negligence is not malice. Arson and burglary do not merge.

**Authority:** Common law; MPC § 220.1; Cal. Pen. Code §§ 451, 452 [VERIFY V-260]; *People v. Atkins* (2001) 25 Cal.4th 76.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** D sets fire to a neighbor's curtains; the window frame is charred. Arson. If only the curtains burned (personal property), not common-law arson.

### R-CRM-059 — Possession offenses
**Rule (write this):** Possession is a sufficient act if the possessor knowingly procured or received the item or was aware of his control of it long enough to have ended possession. Possession may be actual or constructive (dominion and control over the item or the place where it is found) and may be joint, but mere proximity to contraband is not possession.

**Elements:** 1. Actual or constructive possession. 2. Knowledge of the item's presence. 3. Usually knowledge of its illicit nature. 4. Duration sufficient to terminate possession.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Statutory presumptions are valid only as rational permissive inferences (Ulster County). Courts read knowledge into serious possession crimes (Staples; Rehaif).

**Authority:** MPC § 2.01(4); *County Court of Ulster County v. Allen*, 442 U.S. 140 (1979); *Staples v. United States*, 511 U.S. 600 (1994); *Rehaif v. United States*, 588 U.S. 225 (2019).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** Police find cocaine in the locked glove box of a car D borrowed an hour earlier. Without evidence D knew of it, possession is doubtful.

## D. Inchoate crimes and parties (outline III)

### R-CRM-060 — Attempt: mental state
**Rule (write this):** Attempt requires the specific intent to commit the target crime, even if the target crime itself requires a lesser mental state. There is therefore no attempt to commit a crime of recklessness or negligence, no attempted felony murder, and attempted murder requires intent to kill.

**Elements:** 1. Intent to engage in the conduct. 2. Intent to bring about the result of the target crime. 3. For attendant circumstances, the MPC requires only the culpability needed for the completed crime.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Intent to inflict serious bodily injury or depraved-heart recklessness will not support attempted murder.

**Authority:** Common law; MPC § 5.01(1); Cal. Pen. Code § 21a.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** D fires into a crowded room to scare people, and no one is hit. Not attempted murder (no intent to kill); consider assault with a deadly weapon.

### R-CRM-061 — Attempt: the act
**Rule (write this):** Attempt requires an act beyond mere preparation. The common law asks whether the defendant came dangerously close to completing the crime, while the MPC and modern majority ask whether he took a substantial step strongly corroborative of his criminal purpose.

**Elements:** 1. Proximity test: conduct so near completion that, but for interruption, the crime would occur. 2. Substantial step: e.g., lying in wait, enticing the victim, reconnoitering, unlawful entry, possessing tools specially designed for the crime, soliciting an innocent agent.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Purchasing a weapon or merely planning is usually preparation under the proximity test but may be a substantial step. California: a direct but ineffectual act beyond preparation; slight acts suffice where the intent is clear.

**Authority:** *People v. Rizzo*, 246 N.Y. 334 (1927); MPC § 5.01(1)(c), (2); Cal. Pen. Code § 21a; *People v. Superior Court (Decker)* (2007) 41 Cal.4th 1 [VERIFY V-251].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: substantial step unless told common law. Essay: apply both tests.

**Example:** D buys duct tape and a ski mask, drives to V's school and waits by the gate where V exits each day. Substantial step (lying in wait, possession of materials); likely not dangerous proximity.

### R-CRM-062 — Attempt: impossibility
**Rule (write this):** Factual impossibility — where the crime could not be completed because of facts unknown to the defendant — is no defense to attempt. Most modern courts and the MPC also reject "legal" (hybrid) impossibility by judging the defendant's conduct on the circumstances as he believed them to be, but pure legal impossibility — intending to do something that is not a crime at all — remains a defense.

**Elements:** 1. What did the defendant intend? 2. Would that intended conduct, if completed as he believed, be a crime? Yes → attempt. 3. Is the "crime" he believed he was committing actually a crime? No → no attempt.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Traditional courts treated receiving goods no longer stolen as a legal-impossibility defense.

**Authority:** MPC § 5.01(1)(a); *People v. Jaffe*, 185 N.Y. 497 (1906).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: modern rule. Essay: explain the categories and apply the modern rule, noting the traditional view.

**Example:** D shoots a man asleep in bed who, unknown to D, died of a heart attack an hour earlier. Attempted murder: factual impossibility is no defense.

### R-CRM-063 — Attempt: abandonment
**Rule (write this):** At common law, abandonment is no defense once the defendant has gone beyond preparation. Under the MPC and in some states, a complete and voluntary renunciation of criminal purpose is an affirmative defense if the defendant abandons the attempt or prevents the crime.

**Elements:** MPC: 1. Complete renunciation (not postponement or a switch of victim). 2. Voluntary (not prompted by increased risk of detection or harder circumstances). 3. Abandons the effort or prevents completion.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Fleeing because police arrive is not voluntary.

**Authority:** Common law; MPC § 5.01(4).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same; the MBE often tests "voluntary" (fear of capture is not).

**Example:** D breaks a store window to steal, then leaves because a car approaches. No defense: the renunciation was prompted by fear of detection.

### R-CRM-064 — Solicitation
**Rule (write this):** Solicitation is asking, encouraging or commanding another person to commit a crime with the intent that the person commit it. The crime is complete when the request is made, whether or not the other person agrees.

**Elements:** 1. Request or encouragement. 2. Intent that the crime be committed. 3. (MPC) conduct designed to communicate is enough even if the message never arrives.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Impossibility and the other's refusal are no defense. MPC: renunciation that prevents the crime is a defense. Merges into conspiracy (if agreement) or the completed crime.

**Authority:** Common law; MPC § 5.02; Cal. Pen. Code § 653f.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** D offers an undercover officer $5,000 to kill D's business partner. Solicitation of murder is complete at the offer.

### R-CRM-065 — Conspiracy: agreement and overt act
**Rule (write this):** Conspiracy is an agreement between two or more persons to commit an unlawful act, with the intent to agree and the intent to achieve the objective, and in most jurisdictions an overt act in furtherance by any conspirator. The common law requires two genuinely agreeing parties (bilateral), while the MPC and most modern statutes require only that the defendant agree (unilateral).

**Elements:** 1. Agreement (express or inferred from concerted action). 2. Intent to agree. 3. Intent to achieve the object. 4. Overt act (any act, even lawful, by any member) — required by statute in most jurisdictions, not at common law.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Under the bilateral rule, there is no conspiracy with an undercover officer or a feigning partner, and acquittal of all other alleged conspirators in the same trial bars conviction of the remaining one.

**Authority:** Common law; MPC § 5.03(1), (5); 18 U.S.C. § 371; Cal. Pen. Code §§ 182, 184.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: watch for which approach the question specifies; default to the majority (overt act required). Essay: discuss both bilateral and unilateral views.

**Example:** D agrees with X, an undercover agent, to rob a bank, and D buys masks. Conspiracy under the unilateral view; none under the bilateral view.

### R-CRM-066 — Conspiracy: mental state and suppliers
**Rule (write this):** Conspiracy is a specific-intent crime requiring intent to agree and intent that the object crime be committed. A supplier of lawful goods or services who merely knows of the buyer's criminal purpose is not a conspirator unless he intends to further it, which may be inferred from a stake in the venture.

**Elements:** 1. Intent to agree. 2. Intent to achieve the object. 3. Supplier cases: inflated prices, goods with no legitimate use, a large proportion of business from the illegal use, or a serious crime.

**Exceptions / defenses:** No conspiracy to commit a crime defined by recklessness or negligence (in California, no conspiracy to commit implied-malice murder).

**Authority:** *United States v. Falcone*, 311 U.S. 205 (1940); *Direct Sales Co. v. United States*, 319 U.S. 703 (1943); *People v. Lauria* (1967) 251 Cal.App.2d 471; *People v. Swain* (1996) 12 Cal.4th 593 [VERIFY V-251].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** A pharmacy sells unusually large quantities of morphine by mail order to one doctor at normal prices. Knowledge alone may not suffice; the volume and lack of legitimate use support an inference of intent (Direct Sales).

### R-CRM-067 — Pinkerton liability
**Rule (write this):** Under the Pinkerton rule, each conspirator is liable for crimes committed by any co-conspirator in furtherance of the conspiracy if those crimes were reasonably foreseeable as a natural consequence of it, even without personal participation. The MPC rejects Pinkerton liability and holds a conspirator liable for other crimes only as an accomplice.

**Elements:** 1. A conspiracy existed. 2. The crime was committed by a co-conspirator. 3. In furtherance of the conspiracy. 4. Reasonably foreseeable. 5. Committed before the defendant withdrew.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Withdrawal (communicated to all co-conspirators or to police) cuts off liability for later crimes.

**Authority:** *Pinkerton v. United States*, 328 U.S. 640 (1946); MPC § 2.06.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: Pinkerton applies unless the question says MPC. Essay: apply Pinkerton for each co-conspirator crime, then note the MPC.

**Example:** A and B agree to rob a bank. During the robbery, B shoots a guard. A is liable for the shooting under Pinkerton because violence during an armed robbery is foreseeable.

### R-CRM-068 — Conspiracy: withdrawal, duration, Wharton's rule and merger
**Rule (write this):** Conspiracy does not merge with the completed crime, so a defendant can be convicted of both. Withdrawal is no defense to the conspiracy itself at common law once the agreement (and any required overt act) exists, but a timely, communicated withdrawal cuts off liability for co-conspirators' later crimes; Wharton's rule bars conspiracy where the crime necessarily requires exactly the agreeing parties.

**Elements:** 1. No merger (except under MPC § 1.07(1)(b)). 2. Withdrawal: affirmative act communicated to all co-conspirators or notice to police; MPC requires thwarting the conspiracy for a full defense. 3. Duration: ends on achievement or abandonment of the objective; concealment is not included unless agreed. 4. Wharton's rule; protected-class (Gebardi) rule. 5. Impossibility no defense.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Withdrawal before any overt act defeats the conspiracy in overt-act jurisdictions. Police frustration of the object does not end the conspiracy (Jimenez Recio).

**Authority:** *Callanan v. United States*, 364 U.S. 587 (1961); MPC §§ 1.07(1)(b), 5.03(6)–(7); *Iannelli v. United States*, 420 U.S. 770 (1975); *Gebardi v. United States*, 287 U.S. 112 (1932); *United States v. Jimenez Recio*, 537 U.S. 270 (2003); *Smith v. United States*, 568 U.S. 106 (2013).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** A, B and C agree to burn a warehouse; A buys gasoline, then calls only B to say he quits. A remains guilty of conspiracy and, because he did not notify C, is liable for the arson C commits.

### R-CRM-069 — Accomplice liability: elements
**Rule (write this):** A person is liable as an accomplice, and punished as a principal, if he aided, counseled, encouraged or induced the commission of a crime with the intent to aid and the intent that the crime be committed. Mere presence at the scene, even with approval, is not enough.

**Elements:** 1. Act: aid, encouragement (words suffice), supplying tools, lookout, getaway driver, prior promise of help. 2. Dual intent: intent to assist and intent that the principal commit the crime (purpose, majority). 3. The crime was committed by someone.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Knowledge alone usually insufficient (some courts accept it for serious crimes). For crimes of recklessness or negligence, the accomplice must have the culpability required for the result. The principal need not be convicted. Advance knowledge of a weapon is required for armed offenses (Rosemond).

**Authority:** Common law; MPC § 2.06; 18 U.S.C. § 2; Cal. Pen. Code § 31; *Standefer v. United States*, 447 U.S. 10 (1980); *Rosemond v. United States*, 572 U.S. 65 (2014).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** D drives P to a house knowing P plans to break in and waits outside with the engine running. D is an accomplice to the burglary.

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: Similar to PRC joint-crime liability for principal and accessory offenders (主犯/从犯), though US law punishes the accomplice as the principal rather than reducing his sentence by status (approx.).

### R-CRM-070 — Accomplice liability: scope
**Rule (write this):** An accomplice is liable for the crime he intended to aid and, under the common-law natural and probable consequences doctrine, for any other crime the principal commits that was a foreseeable result of the intended crime. The MPC limits accomplice liability to crimes the accomplice purposely aided.

**Elements:** 1. Target crime aided. 2. Additional crime by the principal. 3. Foreseeable consequence of the target crime (common law).

**Exceptions / defenses:** California does not allow the natural and probable consequences doctrine to support murder or attempted murder.

**Authority:** Common law; MPC § 2.06(3); *People v. Chiu* (2014) 59 Cal.4th 155; Cal. Pen. Code § 188(a)(3); *People v. Gentile* (2020) [S-359]; SB 775 [S-360].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: common-law natural and probable consequences. Essay: common law, then MPC; California limits only if asked.

**Example:** D lends P a crowbar to break into a store. Inside, P assaults a night guard. D is liable for the burglary and, if the assault was foreseeable, for the assault (common law), but not under the MPC.

### R-CRM-071 — Accomplice withdrawal and limits; accessory after the fact
**Rule (write this):** An accomplice escapes liability by withdrawing before the crime becomes unstoppable — repudiating encouragement, neutralizing any aid he gave, or warning police or otherwise trying to prevent the crime. An accessory after the fact, who knowingly helps a felon avoid arrest or punishment after the felony, commits a separate, lesser crime rather than the felony itself.

**Elements:** Withdrawal: 1. Timely. 2. Repudiation (encouragement) or neutralization (material aid) or notice to police. Accessory after the fact: 1. Completed felony. 2. Knowledge of it. 3. Aid to the felon. 4. Intent to hinder arrest, trial or punishment.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A protected-class victim (the minor in statutory rape) and a feigned accomplice are not accomplices. Misprision (failure to report) is generally not a crime; federal misprision requires active concealment.

**Authority:** MPC § 2.06(6); Cal. Pen. Code § 32; 18 U.S.C. §§ 3, 4.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** D gives P a gun for a robbery, then changes his mind and tells P "don't do it" but does not retrieve the gun. Not an effective withdrawal: the material aid was not neutralized.

## E. Fourth Amendment: arrest, search and seizure (outline V.A)

### R-CRM-072 — Government action and incorporation
**Rule (write this):** The Fourth Amendment, applied to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment, protects only against searches and seizures by government agents or private persons acting as their instruments. A purely private search is not covered, and police may repeat a private search without a warrant so long as they do not exceed its scope.

**Elements:** 1. Actor is a government official (police, school official, inspector) or a private person acting at government direction. 2. If private, did police exceed the private search?

**Exceptions / defenses:** None; this is a threshold requirement.

**Authority:** U.S. Const. amends. IV, XIV; *Mapp v. Ohio*, 367 U.S. 643 (1961); *United States v. Jacobsen*, 466 U.S. 109 (1984).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule; California courts apply federal law (Cal. Const. art. I, § 28(f)(2) [S-145]).

**Example:** A FedEx employee opens a damaged package and sees white powder, then calls police, who field-test it. No Fourth Amendment violation: the private search revealed the powder, and the field test exposed nothing more of privacy interest.

### R-CRM-073 — What is a search
**Rule (write this):** A Fourth Amendment search occurs when the government intrudes on an expectation of privacy that the person actually holds and that society recognizes as reasonable, or when it physically trespasses on a person, house, paper or effect to obtain information. Using technology not in general public use to learn details of a home's interior, and acquiring long-term cell-phone location records, are searches.

**Elements:** 1. Katz: subjective expectation + objectively reasonable. 2. Jones/Jardines: physical intrusion on a protected area to gather information. 3. Technology: Kyllo (home), Carpenter (historical CSLI), Chatrie (geofence location history).

**Exceptions / defenses:** See R-CRM-074 (no reasonable expectation of privacy).

**Authority:** *Katz v. United States*, 389 U.S. 347 (1967); *United States v. Jones*, 565 U.S. 400 (2012); *Florida v. Jardines*, 569 U.S. 1 (2013); *Kyllo v. United States*, 533 U.S. 27 (2001); *Carpenter v. United States*, 585 U.S. 296 (2018); *Chatrie v. United States* (2026) [S-353] [VERIFY V-034].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** Police aim a thermal imager at a garage attached to D's home to detect grow lamps. A search under Kyllo; a warrant is required.

### R-CRM-074 — No reasonable expectation of privacy
**Rule (write this):** There is no reasonable expectation of privacy in what a person knowingly exposes to the public, including open fields, areas visible from public airspace, garbage left for collection, information voluntarily conveyed to third parties such as bank records and dialed numbers, and physical characteristics such as voice and handwriting. The curtilage of a home, however, is protected like the home itself.

**Elements:** 1. Open fields vs. curtilage (Dunn factors: proximity, enclosure, use, steps to shield). 2. Public vantage point (street, airspace). 3. Third-party doctrine (except long-term location data). 4. Misplaced trust in informants. 5. Dog sniffs in public places or during lawful stops.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A dog sniff at a home's front door is a search (Jardines); squeezing a bag to explore its contents is a search (Bond).

**Authority:** *Oliver v. United States*, 466 U.S. 170 (1984); *United States v. Dunn*, 480 U.S. 294 (1987); *California v. Ciraolo*, 476 U.S. 207 (1986); *California v. Greenwood*, 486 U.S. 35 (1988); *Smith v. Maryland*, 442 U.S. 735 (1979); *United States v. Miller*, 425 U.S. 435 (1976); *Hoffa v. United States*, 385 U.S. 293 (1966); *Illinois v. Caballes*, 543 U.S. 405 (2005); *Bond v. United States*, 529 U.S. 334 (2000).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** Police fly a helicopter at 400 feet and see marijuana in D's fenced backyard. No search (Florida v. Riley): public airspace.

### R-CRM-075 — Fourth Amendment standing
**Rule (write this):** Fourth Amendment rights are personal: a defendant may challenge a search or seizure only if it violated his own reasonable expectation of privacy or property interest. Owners and lawful possessors, overnight guests, and drivers in sole possession of a rental car have standing; passengers and short-term business visitors generally do not, though a passenger may challenge the stop itself.

**Elements:** 1. Defendant's own interest in the place or item. 2. Legitimate presence and relationship to the place. 3. Not abandoned or disclaimed.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A passenger is seized during a traffic stop and may challenge it (Brendlin) and its fruits.

**Authority:** *Rakas v. Illinois*, 439 U.S. 128 (1978); *Minnesota v. Olson*, 495 U.S. 91 (1990); *Minnesota v. Carter*, 525 U.S. 83 (1998); *Byrd v. United States*, 584 U.S. 395 (2018); *Brendlin v. California*, 551 U.S. 249 (2007).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule. On essays, address standing first for each defendant and each place.

**Example:** Police illegally search A's apartment and find drugs belonging to B, who was visiting for two hours to package cocaine. B lacks standing (Carter).

### R-CRM-076 — Seizure of a person
**Rule (write this):** A person is seized when police, by physical force or a show of authority to which he submits, restrain his freedom of movement, so that a reasonable person would not feel free to leave or to decline the officers' requests. A show of authority without submission is not a seizure, but applying physical force with intent to restrain is a seizure even if the person escapes.

**Elements:** 1. Physical force with intent to restrain; or 2. Show of authority + submission. 3. Reasonable-person test (number of officers, weapons, tone, retention of ID or tickets, blocking exit).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Consensual encounters (questions, requests for ID, bus sweeps where passengers may decline) need no justification.

**Authority:** *United States v. Mendenhall*, 446 U.S. 544 (1980); *Florida v. Bostick*, 501 U.S. 429 (1991); *California v. Hodari D.*, 499 U.S. 621 (1991); *Torres v. Madrid* (2021) [VERIFY V-250].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** Officers yell "Stop!" at D, who keeps running and tosses a bag of drugs before being tackled. The drugs were abandoned before any seizure (Hodari D.) and are admissible.

### R-CRM-077 — Terry stop: reasonable suspicion
**Rule (write this):** Police may briefly detain a person for investigation if they have reasonable suspicion, supported by specific and articulable facts under the totality of the circumstances, that criminal activity is afoot. The stop must be limited in time and scope to its purpose; a longer or more intrusive detention becomes an arrest requiring probable cause.

**Elements:** 1. Specific, articulable facts (not a hunch). 2. Totality: flight, location, time, behavior, tips. 3. Tips: anonymous tip needs predictive detail or corroboration (J.L. vs. White); a contemporaneous 911 eyewitness report can suffice (Navarette). 4. Reasonable duration and scope.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Presence in a high-crime area or nervousness alone is not enough; unprovoked flight in a high-crime area is (Wardlow).

**Authority:** *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968); *United States v. Sokolow*, 490 U.S. 1 (1989); *Illinois v. Wardlow*, 528 U.S. 119 (2000); *Florida v. J.L.*, 529 U.S. 266 (2000); *Alabama v. White*, 496 U.S. 325 (1990); *Navarette v. California*, 572 U.S. 393 (2014); *Florida v. Royer*, 460 U.S. 491 (1983).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** An anonymous caller says a young man in a plaid shirt at a bus stop has a gun. Officers find him and frisk him. No reasonable suspicion (J.L.); the gun is suppressed.

### R-CRM-078 — Terry frisk and protective searches
**Rule (write this):** During a lawful stop, an officer who reasonably believes the suspect is armed and dangerous may pat down his outer clothing for weapons, and may seize contraband whose identity is immediately apparent by touch. Officers may also search a car's passenger compartment for weapons, and sweep a home during an arrest, on reasonable suspicion of danger.

**Elements:** 1. Lawful stop. 2. Reasonable belief the person is armed and dangerous. 3. Scope limited to a pat-down for weapons. 4. Plain feel: identity immediately apparent, no manipulation.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Squeezing or manipulating a lump to identify it exceeds the frisk (Dickerson).

**Authority:** *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968); *Minnesota v. Dickerson*, 508 U.S. 366 (1993); *Michigan v. Long*, 463 U.S. 1032 (1983); *Maryland v. Buie*, 494 U.S. 325 (1990).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** Frisking a stopped burglary suspect, an officer feels a small soft lump, squeezes it repeatedly and concludes it is crack. Suppressed: not immediately apparent.

### R-CRM-079 — Traffic stops
**Rule (write this):** A traffic stop is a seizure of the driver and all passengers and requires reasonable suspicion or probable cause of a violation; the officer's subjective motive is irrelevant. Officers may order occupants out of the car, but may not prolong the stop beyond the time needed for its mission, such as to conduct a dog sniff, without independent reasonable suspicion.

**Elements:** 1. Objective basis for the stop (pretext irrelevant). 2. Occupants may be ordered out; frisk if reasonably believed armed and dangerous. 3. Mission: ticket, license and registration checks, safety. 4. No unjustified prolongation.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A reasonable mistake of law can supply reasonable suspicion (Heien). Random license checks are invalid (Prouse).

**Authority:** *Whren v. United States*, 517 U.S. 806 (1996); *Pennsylvania v. Mimms*, 434 U.S. 106 (1977); *Maryland v. Wilson*, 519 U.S. 408 (1997); *Arizona v. Johnson*, 555 U.S. 323 (2009); *Rodriguez v. United States*, 575 U.S. 348 (2015); *Heien v. North Carolina*, 574 U.S. 54 (2014); *Delaware v. Prouse*, 440 U.S. 648 (1979).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** After writing a speeding warning, an officer holds the driver eight more minutes for a K-9 unit with no other suspicion. The extension violates Rodriguez; drugs found are suppressed.

### R-CRM-080 — Arrest
**Rule (write this):** An arrest requires probable cause — facts sufficient for a reasonable person to believe the suspect committed a crime. Police may arrest in a public place without a warrant, even for a minor offense committed in their presence, but absent exigency or consent they need an arrest warrant to enter the suspect's home and a search warrant to enter a third party's home.

**Elements:** 1. Probable cause. 2. Public place → no warrant. 3. Suspect's home → arrest warrant (Payton). 4. Third party's home → search warrant (Steagald). 5. Prompt judicial probable-cause determination (within 48 hours presumptively).

**Exceptions / defenses:** An illegal arrest does not bar prosecution; it supports suppression of its fruits. A Payton violation does not taint a later station-house statement if probable cause existed (Harris). An arrest made in good-faith reliance on a statute later held unconstitutional is valid, unless the statute was so grossly and flagrantly unconstitutional that any reasonable person would see its flaws (*DeFillippo*).

**Authority:** *United States v. Watson*, 423 U.S. 411 (1976); *Atwater v. City of Lago Vista*, 532 U.S. 318 (2001); *Payton v. New York*, 445 U.S. 573 (1980); *Steagald v. United States*, 451 U.S. 204 (1981); *County of Riverside v. McLaughlin*, 500 U.S. 44 (1991); *New York v. Harris*, 495 U.S. 14 (1990); *Maryland v. Pringle*, 540 U.S. 366 (2003). *Michigan v. DeFillippo*, 443 U.S. 31 (1979).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** With probable cause but no warrant, officers walk into D's living room through an open door and arrest him. Payton violation; evidence seen in the home is suppressible.

### R-CRM-081 — Excessive force
**Rule (write this):** Force used in an arrest or stop is a seizure judged by objective reasonableness under the totality of the circumstances, considering the severity of the crime, the immediacy of the threat, and whether the suspect is resisting or fleeing. Deadly force to stop a fleeing suspect is reasonable only if the officer has probable cause to believe the suspect poses a significant threat of death or serious physical injury.

**Elements:** 1. Seizure by force. 2. Graham factors. 3. Totality of the circumstances, not only the final moment. 4. Garner limit on deadly force.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Claims usually arise in civil suits (42 U.S.C. § 1983) and as the basis for justification defenses.

**Authority:** *Graham v. Connor*, 490 U.S. 386 (1989); *Tennessee v. Garner*, 471 U.S. 1 (1985); *Barnes v. Felix* (2025) [VERIFY V-035].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** An officer shoots an unarmed shoplifter running away. Unreasonable deadly force under Garner.

### R-CRM-082 — Search warrants
**Rule (write this):** A valid search warrant must be issued by a neutral and detached magistrate on probable cause — a fair probability, under the totality of the circumstances, that evidence of a crime will be found in the place described — supported by oath and particularly describing the place to be searched and the items to be seized. A defendant who shows that the affiant deliberately or recklessly included a false statement necessary to probable cause is entitled to suppression.

**Elements:** 1. Probable cause (Gates totality; informant's veracity and basis of knowledge; corroboration). 2. Neutral magistrate. 3. Particularity. 4. Oath. 5. Proper execution: knock and announce (unless danger, futility or destruction of evidence), detention of occupants, scope.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Franks hearing for false statements. Knock-and-announce violations do not trigger exclusion (Hudson). Good-faith reliance may save evidence from a defective warrant (R-CRM-092).

**Authority:** U.S. Const. amend. IV; *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213 (1983); *Franks v. Delaware*, 438 U.S. 154 (1978); *Coolidge v. New Hampshire*, 403 U.S. 443 (1971); *Groh v. Ramirez*, 540 U.S. 551 (2004); *Wilson v. Arkansas*, 514 U.S. 927 (1995); *Michigan v. Summers*, 452 U.S. 692 (1981); *Ybarra v. Illinois*, 444 U.S. 85 (1979).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** An informant with no track record says D sells drugs from his house; police watch and see 30 short visits in two hours. The corroboration supplies probable cause under Gates.

### R-CRM-083 — Search incident to arrest
**Rule (write this):** After a lawful custodial arrest, police may search the arrestee's person and the area within his immediate control without a warrant. They may search a vehicle's passenger compartment only if the arrestee is unsecured and within reaching distance of it or it is reasonable to believe evidence of the offense of arrest is in the vehicle, and they generally need a warrant to search the digital contents of a cell phone.

**Elements:** 1. Lawful custodial arrest (probable cause). 2. Contemporaneous search. 3. Person and wingspan (Chimel/Robinson). 4. Vehicle: Gant's two prongs. 5. Phone data: warrant (Riley).

**Exceptions / defenses:** No search incident to a citation (Knowles). Breath but not blood tests incident to DUI arrest (Birchfield). Exigency may justify a warrantless phone search in rare cases.

**Authority:** *Chimel v. California*, 395 U.S. 752 (1969); *United States v. Robinson*, 414 U.S. 218 (1973); *Arizona v. Gant*, 556 U.S. 332 (2009); *Riley v. California*, 573 U.S. 373 (2014); *Knowles v. Iowa*, 525 U.S. 113 (1998); *Birchfield v. North Dakota*, 579 U.S. 438 (2016).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** D is arrested for driving on a suspended license, handcuffed and placed in the patrol car; officers then search his car and find cocaine. Invalid under Gant: D was secured and no evidence of license suspension would be in the car.

### R-CRM-084 — Automobile exception
**Rule (write this):** Police with probable cause to believe a vehicle contains contraband or evidence of a crime may search it without a warrant, including any container in it that could hold the object of the search and passengers' belongings. The exception does not allow entry onto a home's curtilage to search a vehicle parked there.

**Elements:** 1. Readily mobile vehicle. 2. Probable cause as to the vehicle (or a specific container). 3. Scope: wherever the object could be. 4. May be done later at the station.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Probable cause limited to a container permits searching that container only (Acevedo). Curtilage (Collins).

**Authority:** *Carroll v. United States*, 267 U.S. 132 (1925); *California v. Acevedo*, 500 U.S. 565 (1991); *Wyoming v. Houghton*, 526 U.S. 295 (1999); *Chambers v. Maroney*, 399 U.S. 42 (1970); *Collins v. Virginia*, 584 U.S. 586 (2018).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** A reliable informant says D's car trunk holds heroin. Police stop the car and search the trunk and a closed backpack inside. Valid under the automobile exception.

### R-CRM-085 — Plain view
**Rule (write this):** Police may seize an item without a warrant if they are lawfully in the place from which they see it, have a lawful right of access to it, and its incriminating character is immediately apparent. Inadvertence is not required, but moving an item to inspect it is a separate search requiring probable cause.

**Elements:** 1. Lawful vantage point. 2. Lawful access. 3. Probable cause that the item is contraband or evidence, without further search.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Hicks (moving stereo equipment to read serial numbers was a search).

**Authority:** *Coolidge v. New Hampshire*, 403 U.S. 443 (1971); *Horton v. California*, 496 U.S. 128 (1990); *Arizona v. Hicks*, 480 U.S. 321 (1987).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** Officers executing a warrant for stolen rifles see a bag of methamphetamine on the kitchen table. They may seize it under plain view.

### R-CRM-086 — Consent searches
**Rule (write this):** A warrantless search is valid if a person with actual or apparent authority voluntarily consents, judged by the totality of the circumstances; knowledge of the right to refuse is a factor but not required. A physically present co-occupant's express objection overrides another occupant's consent as to him.

**Elements:** 1. Voluntary (no coercion; not after a false claim of a warrant). 2. Authority: actual (common authority) or apparent (reasonable belief). 3. Scope: what a reasonable person would understand. 4. Not revoked.

**Exceptions / defenses:** If the objector is lawfully removed, the remaining occupant may consent (Fernandez). Landlords and hotel clerks cannot consent for tenants or guests.

**Authority:** *Schneckloth v. Bustamonte*, 412 U.S. 218 (1973); *Bumper v. North Carolina*, 391 U.S. 543 (1968); *United States v. Matlock*, 415 U.S. 164 (1974); *Illinois v. Rodriguez*, 497 U.S. 177 (1990); *Georgia v. Randolph*, 547 U.S. 103 (2006); *Fernandez v. California*, 571 U.S. 292 (2014); *Florida v. Jimeno*, 500 U.S. 248 (1991).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** D's ex-girlfriend, who moved out a month ago but still has a key and says she lives there, lets police in. Valid if the officers reasonably believed she lived there (apparent authority).

### R-CRM-087 — Exigent circumstances and emergency aid
**Rule (write this):** Police may search or enter without a warrant when exigent circumstances make obtaining a warrant impracticable: hot pursuit of a fleeing felon, imminent destruction of evidence, or an emergency threatening life or safety. Under the emergency-aid exception, officers may enter a home when they have an objectively reasonable basis to believe someone inside needs immediate aid; probable cause is not required.

**Elements:** 1. Specific exigency. 2. Objectively reasonable belief (officer's motive irrelevant). 3. Police did not create the exigency by violating or threatening to violate the Fourth Amendment. 4. Scope limited to the exigency.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Pursuit of a misdemeanant is judged case by case (Lange). Alcohol dissipation is not a per se exigency (McNeely). No free-standing community-caretaking exception for homes (Caniglia).

**Authority:** *Warden v. Hayden*, 387 U.S. 294 (1967); *Kentucky v. King*, 563 U.S. 452 (2011); *Brigham City v. Stuart*, 547 U.S. 398 (2006); *Missouri v. McNeely*, 569 U.S. 141 (2013); *Case v. Montana* (2026) [S-351] [S-352] [VERIFY V-034]; *Lange v. California* and *Caniglia v. Strom* (2021) [VERIFY V-250].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** Responding to a 911 call that D threatened suicide, officers see through a window a gun on the table and D slumped in a chair, and enter. Valid emergency-aid entry (Case v. Montana).

### R-CRM-088 — Administrative, special-needs, inventory, border and checkpoint searches
**Rule (write this):** Searches serving special needs beyond ordinary law enforcement are judged by balancing the government's need against the privacy intrusion, without probable cause or a warrant. This covers standardized inventory searches, sobriety and border checkpoints (but not general crime-control checkpoints), border searches, reasonable school searches, searches of probationers and parolees, and inspections of closely regulated businesses.

**Elements:** 1. Primary purpose other than general crime control. 2. Limited discretion (standardized procedures). 3. Reasonable scope.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Drug-interdiction checkpoints are invalid (Edmond). School strip searches need more justification (Redding).

**Authority:** *Colorado v. Bertine*, 479 U.S. 367 (1987); *Florida v. Wells*, 495 U.S. 1 (1990); *Michigan Dept. of State Police v. Sitz*, 496 U.S. 444 (1990); *City of Indianapolis v. Edmond*, 531 U.S. 32 (2000); *New Jersey v. T.L.O.*, 469 U.S. 325 (1985); *Samson v. California*, 547 U.S. 843 (2006); *United States v. Knights*, 534 U.S. 112 (2001); *New York v. Burger*, 482 U.S. 691 (1987).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** A vice principal searches a student's purse on a teacher's report that she was smoking and finds marijuana. Valid under T.L.O.

### R-CRM-089 — Electronic and digital surveillance
**Rule (write this):** Electronic eavesdropping on private conversations is a search requiring a warrant (and, under federal law, a Title III order), unless one party to the conversation consents or the speaker has no reasonable expectation of privacy. Long-term digital location tracking and geofence location data are searches, and the contents of a cell phone generally require a warrant.

**Elements:** 1. Private conversation (Katz). 2. Consent of one party (informant wearing a wire) → no search. 3. Location tracking: Knotts (public movements) vs. Jones (GPS trespass) vs. Carpenter (CSLI) vs. Chatrie (geofence). 4. Phone contents: Riley.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Statements made loudly in public or audible without enhancement carry no expectation of privacy.

**Authority:** *Katz v. United States*, 389 U.S. 347 (1967); *United States v. White*, 401 U.S. 745 (1971); 18 U.S.C. §§ 2510–2522; *Carpenter v. United States*, 585 U.S. 296 (2018); *Chatrie v. United States* (2026) [S-353] [VERIFY V-034]; *Riley v. California*, 573 U.S. 373 (2014).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule. Essays (Jul 2015 Q3; Jul 2019 Q3) have tested overheard conversations.

**Example:** Police use a store-bought parabolic microphone from across a street to capture D's whispered payphone call in an enclosed booth. Arguably a search under Katz (enclosed booth; whisper), though the device's general public availability is a counter-argument under Kyllo.

### R-CRM-090 — Exclusionary rule: scope and limits (and California)
**Rule (write this):** Evidence obtained in violation of the Fourth Amendment is generally inadmissible in the prosecution's case-in-chief in state and federal court. Because the rule exists to deter police misconduct, it does not apply in grand-jury, civil or parole proceedings, does not bar impeachment of the defendant's own testimony, and applies only where deterrence benefits outweigh its costs; in California, Truth-in-Evidence means evidence is excluded only when federal law requires.

**Elements:** 1. Violation of the defendant's own rights (standing). 2. Evidence offered in the case-in-chief. 3. No exception (R-CRM-091, R-CRM-092).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Impeachment (Havens); grand jury (Calandra); parole revocation (Scott); federal habeas (Stone v. Powell).

**Authority:** *Weeks v. United States*, 232 U.S. 383 (1914); *Mapp v. Ohio*, 367 U.S. 643 (1961); *Herring v. United States*, 555 U.S. 135 (2009); *United States v. Havens*, 446 U.S. 620 (1980); *United States v. Calandra*, 414 U.S. 338 (1974); Cal. Const. art. I, § 28(f)(2) [S-145]; *In re Lance W.* (1985) 37 Cal.3d 873; Cal. Pen. Code § 1538.5.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same federal rule. On a California essay, one sentence: "Under Proposition 8, California excludes evidence only where the federal Constitution requires."

**Example:** Cocaine illegally seized from D's car is suppressed in the case-in-chief; if D testifies he never touched cocaine, the prosecution may use it to impeach him.

### R-CRM-091 — Fruit of the poisonous tree and its exceptions
**Rule (write this):** Evidence derived from an unconstitutional search, seizure or arrest is excluded as fruit of the poisonous tree unless the prosecution shows an independent source, inevitable discovery by lawful means, or attenuation that dissipates the taint. Attenuation is judged by the time elapsed, any intervening circumstances, and the purpose and flagrancy of the misconduct.

**Elements:** 1. Primary illegality. 2. Derivative evidence (physical items, statements, observations). 3. Independent source (Murray: later valid warrant not prompted by the illegal entry). 4. Inevitable discovery (Nix, by a preponderance). 5. Attenuation (Brown factors; Strieff: pre-existing warrant; Wong Sun: voluntary return to confess).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Miranda warnings alone do not purge an illegal arrest (Brown). Live witnesses are rarely suppressed (Ceccolini).

**Authority:** *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471 (1963); *Murray v. United States*, 487 U.S. 533 (1988); *Nix v. Williams*, 467 U.S. 431 (1984); *Brown v. Illinois*, 422 U.S. 590 (1975); *Utah v. Strieff*, 579 U.S. 232 (2016); *United States v. Ceccolini*, 435 U.S. 268 (1978).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** Officers illegally enter a warehouse, see bales of marijuana, leave, and get a warrant using only information they had before the entry. The marijuana is admissible under the independent-source doctrine (Murray), if the entry did not prompt the decision to seek the warrant.

### R-CRM-092 — Good-faith and related exceptions
**Rule (write this):** Evidence is not excluded when officers acted in objectively reasonable reliance on a warrant later held invalid, a statute later held unconstitutional, binding appellate precedent later overruled, or a database maintained by court clerks or containing an isolated negligent police error. Good faith does not save a bare-bones affidavit, a facially deficient warrant, a warrant obtained by deliberate or reckless falsehood, or one issued by a magistrate who abandoned the judicial role.

**Elements:** 1. Objective reasonableness of reliance. 2. Not one of the four Leon exceptions. 3. For record errors: isolated negligence, not systemic or reckless.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Knock-and-announce violations do not lead to exclusion (Hudson). Related: evidence found in a search incident to an arrest under a later-invalidated ordinance is admissible if the officer reasonably relied on it (*DeFillippo*; compare *Krull* for statutes authorizing searches).

**Authority:** *United States v. Leon*, 468 U.S. 897 (1984); *Massachusetts v. Sheppard*, 468 U.S. 981 (1984); *Michigan v. DeFillippo*, 443 U.S. 31 (1979); *Illinois v. Krull*, 480 U.S. 340 (1987); *Arizona v. Evans*, 514 U.S. 1 (1995); *Herring v. United States*, 555 U.S. 135 (2009); *Davis v. United States*, 564 U.S. 229 (2011); *Hudson v. Michigan*, 547 U.S. 586 (2006).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** A magistrate issues a warrant on an affidavit an appellate court later finds slightly short of probable cause. The evidence comes in under Leon because reliance was reasonable.

## F. Confessions and self-incrimination (outline V.B)

### R-CRM-093 — Due-process voluntariness
**Rule (write this):** A confession obtained by police coercion that overbears the suspect's will, judged by the totality of the circumstances, is involuntary and inadmissible for any purpose, including impeachment. Coercive police activity is a necessary predicate; the suspect's own mental illness alone does not make a confession involuntary.

**Elements:** 1. Police coercion (threats, violence, prolonged questioning, deprivation, certain promises). 2. Causal link to the statement. 3. Totality: age, education, mental state, duration, conditions.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Admission of a coerced confession is reviewed for harmless error (Fulminante).

**Authority:** U.S. Const. amend. XIV; *Colorado v. Connelly*, 479 U.S. 157 (1986); *Mincey v. Arizona*, 437 U.S. 385 (1978); *Arizona v. Fulminante*, 499 U.S. 279 (1991); *Jackson v. Denno*, 378 U.S. 368 (1964).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule. Analyze it before Miranda on essays.

**Example:** After 16 hours of continuous questioning without sleep, during which officers tell D his children will be taken away unless he confesses, D confesses. Involuntary.

### R-CRM-094 — Miranda: custody
**Rule (write this):** Miranda warnings are required only for custodial interrogation. A suspect is in custody when, under the objective circumstances, a reasonable person would not feel free to end the questioning and leave, and the setting presents the inherently coercive pressures of station-house questioning; routine traffic and Terry stops are not custody.

**Elements:** 1. Objective test (officer's undisclosed suspicions irrelevant). 2. Factors: location, duration, restraints, number of officers, statements that suspect is or is not free to leave, release afterward. 3. A child's age if known or apparent.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Voluntary station-house interviews where the suspect is told he is free to leave are not custody (Mathiason; Beheler).

**Authority:** *Miranda v. Arizona*, 384 U.S. 436 (1966); *Stansbury v. California*, 511 U.S. 318 (1994); *Berkemer v. McCarty*, 468 U.S. 420 (1984); *Howes v. Fields*, 565 U.S. 499 (2012); *J.D.B. v. North Carolina*, 564 U.S. 261 (2011); *Oregon v. Mathiason*, 429 U.S. 492 (1977).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** D is handcuffed in the back of a patrol car and asked "Where did you put the money?" He is in custody; without warnings his answer is inadmissible in the case-in-chief.

### R-CRM-095 — Miranda: interrogation
**Rule (write this):** Interrogation means express questioning or its functional equivalent — words or actions by police that they should know are reasonably likely to elicit an incriminating response. Volunteered statements, routine booking questions, and questioning by an undercover agent the suspect does not know is an officer are not Miranda interrogation.

**Elements:** 1. Express question; or 2. Functional equivalent (focus on the suspect's perceptions). 3. Not volunteered. 4. Not booking. 5. Suspect knows he is speaking to police.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Undercover questioning after formal charges may violate the Sixth Amendment (Massiah/Henry).

**Authority:** *Rhode Island v. Innis*, 446 U.S. 291 (1980); *Pennsylvania v. Muniz*, 496 U.S. 582 (1990) (routine-booking-question exception recognized by a plurality); *Illinois v. Perkins*, 496 U.S. 292 (1990).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** As D is booked, he blurts out, "I only took the money, I didn't hurt anyone." Volunteered; admissible without warnings.

### R-CRM-096 — Miranda: warnings and waiver
**Rule (write this):** Before custodial interrogation, police must warn the suspect of the right to remain silent, that statements can be used against him, the right to an attorney, and the right to appointed counsel if indigent. A waiver must be knowing, intelligent and voluntary, and may be implied when a suspect who understood the warnings makes an uncoerced statement.

**Elements:** 1. Warnings reasonably conveying the four rights (no exact words). 2. Waiver: express or implied (Butler; Thompkins). 3. Knowing and intelligent (understood the rights). 4. Voluntary (no coercion). 5. Prosecution proves by a preponderance.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Police need not tell the suspect a lawyer is trying to reach him (Burbine) or all subjects of questioning (Spring).

**Authority:** *Miranda v. Arizona*, 384 U.S. 436 (1966); *Dickerson v. United States*, 530 U.S. 428 (2000); *Duckworth v. Eagan*, 492 U.S. 195 (1989); *North Carolina v. Butler*, 441 U.S. 369 (1979); *Berghuis v. Thompkins*, 560 U.S. 370 (2010); *Moran v. Burbine*, 475 U.S. 412 (1986); *Colorado v. Spring*, 479 U.S. 564 (1987).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** Warned, D says nothing for two hours, then answers "Yes" when asked if he prays for forgiveness for the shooting. Implied waiver (Thompkins).

### R-CRM-097 — Miranda: invoking the right to silence
**Rule (write this):** A suspect who wants to cut off questioning must invoke the right to remain silent unambiguously; once he does, police must scrupulously honor the invocation. They may resume questioning only after a significant break, with fresh warnings, typically on a different crime.

**Elements:** 1. Unambiguous invocation (silence alone is not enough). 2. Questioning stops. 3. Resumption only under Mosley conditions (time, new warnings, different subject or other indicia of respect).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Post-warning silence cannot be used to impeach (Doyle).

**Authority:** *Michigan v. Mosley*, 423 U.S. 96 (1975); *Berghuis v. Thompkins*, 560 U.S. 370 (2010); *Doyle v. Ohio*, 426 U.S. 610 (1976).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** D says, "I don't want to talk." Officers keep asking for 10 minutes until he confesses. Inadmissible: the invocation was not scrupulously honored.

### R-CRM-098 — Miranda: invoking the right to counsel (Edwards)
**Rule (write this):** If a suspect in custody unambiguously requests a lawyer, all interrogation about any crime must stop until counsel is present, unless the suspect himself reinitiates communication with the police. The protection lapses once the suspect has been released from custody for 14 days.

**Elements:** 1. Unambiguous request (Davis: "Maybe I should talk to a lawyer" is not). 2. All questioning stops, on any offense (Roberson), even after consultation (Minnick). 3. Reinitiation by the suspect showing a desire for a generalized discussion (Bradshaw) + valid waiver. 4. 14-day break in custody ends Edwards (Shatzer).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Statements obtained in violation of Edwards may still be used to impeach if voluntary.

**Authority:** *Edwards v. Arizona*, 451 U.S. 477 (1981); *Davis v. United States*, 512 U.S. 452 (1994); *Arizona v. Roberson*, 486 U.S. 675 (1988); *Minnick v. Mississippi*, 498 U.S. 146 (1990); *Oregon v. Bradshaw*, 462 U.S. 1039 (1983); *Maryland v. Shatzer*, 559 U.S. 98 (2010).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** D asks for a lawyer during questioning about a burglary. The next day, a different detective warns him and asks about an unrelated murder; D confesses. Inadmissible under Roberson.

### R-CRM-099 — Public-safety exception
**Rule (write this):** Police may ask questions reasonably prompted by an objective, immediate concern for public safety before giving Miranda warnings, and the answers and resulting evidence are admissible. The exception covers questions necessary to secure the officers' or the public's safety, not questions designed to gather evidence.

**Elements:** 1. Objective, immediate threat (a discarded gun in a public place). 2. Questions limited to neutralizing it.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Once the danger is secured, further questioning requires warnings.

**Authority:** *New York v. Quarles*, 467 U.S. 649 (1984).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** Officers chase an armed robber into a supermarket, find him with an empty holster and ask, "Where's the gun?" His answer and the gun are admissible.

### R-CRM-100 — Miranda fruits, two-step interrogation and impeachment
**Rule (write this):** Physical evidence derived from a voluntary but unwarned statement is admissible, and a later warned statement is admissible if the first unwarned statement was voluntary — unless police deliberately used a question-first, warn-later strategy without curative measures. A voluntary statement obtained in violation of Miranda may be used to impeach the defendant's inconsistent trial testimony, but an involuntary statement may not.

**Elements:** 1. Physical fruits: admissible (Patane — plurality opinion plus a two-Justice concurrence in the judgment). 2. Second statement: Elstad (admissible) vs. Seibert (deliberate two-step → excluded absent curative steps such as a substantial break or a warning that the first statement is inadmissible). 3. Impeachment (Harris). 4. Silence after warnings cannot be used (Doyle); pre-arrest, non-custodial silence without express invocation can be (Salinas).

**Exceptions / defenses:** A Miranda violation does not support a § 1983 damages action [VERIFY V-250].

**Authority:** *United States v. Patane*, 542 U.S. 630 (2004); *Oregon v. Elstad*, 470 U.S. 298 (1985); *Missouri v. Seibert*, 542 U.S. 600 (2004); *Harris v. New York*, 401 U.S. 222 (1971); *Salinas v. Texas*, 570 U.S. 178 (2013); *People v. May* (1988) 44 Cal.3d 309 [S-364].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule; California follows Harris under Truth-in-Evidence (May).

**Example:** Police intentionally question D without warnings until he confesses, then give warnings and have him repeat it after a 15-minute break. The warned confession is excluded under Seibert.

### R-CRM-101 — Fifth Amendment privilege: scope
**Rule (write this):** The privilege against self-incrimination protects a person from being compelled by the government to provide testimonial communications that could incriminate him in a criminal case. It does not protect physical evidence such as blood, fingerprints, or voice and handwriting exemplars, and it can be displaced by a grant of use and derivative-use immunity.

**Elements:** 1. Compelled. 2. Testimonial (communicates a fact or belief). 3. Incriminating (real risk of prosecution). 4. Personal (no privilege for corporations or for a custodian's production of entity records).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Act of production may be testimonial (Hubbell). No comment on a defendant's failure to testify (Griffin); no-adverse-inference instruction on request (Carter).

**Authority:** U.S. Const. amend. V; *Malloy v. Hogan*, 378 U.S. 1 (1964); *Schmerber v. California*, 384 U.S. 757 (1966); *Fisher v. United States*, 425 U.S. 391 (1976); *United States v. Hubbell*, 530 U.S. 27 (2000); *Braswell v. United States*, 487 U.S. 99 (1988); *Kastigar v. United States*, 406 U.S. 441 (1972); *Griffin v. California*, 380 U.S. 609 (1965).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** The prosecutor tells the jury, "If D were innocent, he would have taken the stand to explain." Griffin violation.

## G. Counsel and identification (outline V.C, V.D)

### R-CRM-102 — Sixth Amendment counsel: attachment and deliberate elicitation
**Rule (write this):** The Sixth Amendment right to counsel attaches when adversary judicial proceedings begin — formal charge, preliminary hearing, indictment, information, arraignment or first appearance — and applies at every critical stage after that. Once it attaches, the government may not deliberately elicit statements about the charged offense from the defendant without counsel or a valid waiver; the right is offense-specific.

**Elements:** 1. Attachment (Kirby; Rothgery). 2. Critical stage or deliberate elicitation by police or an active informant (Massiah; Brewer; Henry). 3. Same offense as charged (Blockburger test, Cobb). 4. No valid waiver (Miranda warnings suffice; Montejo).

**Exceptions / defenses:** A passive "listening post" informant does not violate the right (Kuhlmann). Statements taken in violation may impeach (Ventris).

**Authority:** U.S. Const. amend. VI; *Massiah v. United States*, 377 U.S. 201 (1964); *Brewer v. Williams*, 430 U.S. 387 (1977); *United States v. Henry*, 447 U.S. 264 (1980); *Kirby v. Illinois*, 406 U.S. 682 (1972); *Rothgery v. Gillespie County*, 554 U.S. 191 (2008); *McNeil v. Wisconsin*, 501 U.S. 171 (1991); *Texas v. Cobb*, 532 U.S. 162 (2001); *Montejo v. Louisiana*, 556 U.S. 778 (2009); *Kansas v. Ventris*, 556 U.S. 586 (2009).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** After D is indicted for robbery, police place an informant in his cell who asks repeatedly about the robbery. D's statements are inadmissible in the case-in-chief (Henry); statements about an uncharged murder are not barred by the Sixth Amendment.

### R-CRM-103 — Right to appointed counsel
**Rule (write this):** An indigent defendant has a right to appointed counsel in every felony prosecution and in any misdemeanor case where a sentence of imprisonment, including a suspended sentence, is imposed. The right extends to the first appeal as of right but not to discretionary appeals or collateral proceedings.

**Elements:** 1. Indigence. 2. Felony, or actual imprisonment imposed. 3. Stage: trial and critical pretrial stages; first appeal of right.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A fine-only misdemeanor conviction without counsel is valid (Scott).

**Authority:** *Gideon v. Wainwright*, 372 U.S. 335 (1963); *Argersinger v. Hamlin*, 407 U.S. 25 (1972); *Scott v. Illinois*, 440 U.S. 367 (1979); *Alabama v. Shelton*, 535 U.S. 654 (2002); *Douglas v. California*, 372 U.S. 353 (1963); *Ross v. Moffitt*, 417 U.S. 600 (1974).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** Unrepresented, D is convicted of a misdemeanor punishable by up to a year and fined $500. Valid; if the judge had imposed 30 days in jail, the conviction would violate the Sixth Amendment.

### R-CRM-104 — Self-representation, counsel of choice and consultation limits
**Rule (write this):** A competent defendant may waive counsel and represent himself if his request is timely and unequivocal and his waiver is knowing and intelligent, though a court may require counsel for a defendant too mentally ill to conduct his own defense. A defendant who can retain counsel has the right to counsel of his choice, and wrongful denial is structural error.

**Elements:** 1. Faretta: timely, unequivocal request; knowing and intelligent waiver (warned of dangers); competence. 2. Edwards (Indiana) limit for severe mental illness. 3. Standby counsel permitted. 4. Counsel of choice (Gonzalez-Lopez), subject to conflict concerns (Wheat).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Courts may bar consultation about ongoing testimony during an overnight recess if other consultation is allowed (Villarreal), but not all overnight consultation (Geders). Counsel may not concede guilt over the client's objection (McCoy).

**Authority:** *Faretta v. California*, 422 U.S. 806 (1975); *Indiana v. Edwards*, 554 U.S. 164 (2008); *Godinez v. Moran*, 509 U.S. 389 (1993); *United States v. Gonzalez-Lopez*, 548 U.S. 140 (2006); *Wheat v. United States*, 486 U.S. 153 (1988); *Geders v. United States*, 425 U.S. 80 (1976); *Villarreal v. Texas* (2026) [S-355] [VERIFY V-034]; *McCoy v. Louisiana*, 584 U.S. 414 (2018).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule (Jul 2014 Q4 tested a self-representation request a week before trial).

**Example:** A week before trial, D, of average education, asks to fire his lawyer and represent himself. If the request is unequivocal and the judge confirms D understands the risks, it should be granted; lack of legal skill is irrelevant, though a court may deny a request made to delay trial.

### R-CRM-105 — Ineffective assistance and conflicts
**Rule (write this):** To prove ineffective assistance, a defendant must show that counsel's performance fell below an objective standard of reasonableness and that there is a reasonable probability that, but for the errors, the result would have been different. Prejudice is presumed only for complete denial of counsel, a total failure of adversarial testing, or an actual conflict of interest that adversely affected performance.

**Elements:** 1. Deficient performance (strong presumption of sound strategy). 2. Prejudice (reasonable probability). 3. Plea context: would have gone to trial (Hill); duty to advise on clear deportation consequences (Padilla) and to communicate plea offers (Frye); rejected-offer prejudice (Lafler).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Joint representation over timely objection → automatic reversal (Holloway); otherwise actual conflict + adverse effect (Cuyler). Refusing to assist client perjury is not ineffective (Nix v. Whiteside).

**Authority:** *Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668 (1984); *United States v. Cronic*, 466 U.S. 648 (1984); *Hill v. Lockhart*, 474 U.S. 52 (1985); *Padilla v. Kentucky*, 559 U.S. 356 (2010); *Missouri v. Frye*, 566 U.S. 134 (2012); *Lafler v. Cooper*, 566 U.S. 156 (2012); *Cuyler v. Sullivan*, 446 U.S. 335 (1980); *Holloway v. Arkansas*, 435 U.S. 475 (1978); *Nix v. Whiteside*, 475 U.S. 157 (1986); *Garza v. Idaho*, 586 U.S. 232 (2019).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule. Pairs with PR on crossovers.

**Example:** Defense counsel never tells D about a prosecution offer of two years; D is convicted at trial and gets ten. Deficient performance (Frye); prejudice if D would have accepted and the court would have approved the deal.

### R-CRM-106 — Identification: right to counsel
**Rule (write this):** A defendant has a Sixth Amendment right to counsel at a post-charge, in-person lineup or show-up, but not at a pre-charge lineup or at any photographic identification. If counsel was wrongly absent, the lineup identification is excluded, and an in-court identification is allowed only if the prosecution proves by clear and convincing evidence an independent source.

**Elements:** 1. Formal charges filed? 2. Live (corporeal) identification? 3. Counsel absent without waiver. 4. Remedy: exclude; independent-source test for in-court identification.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Photo arrays (Ash); pre-charge lineups (Kirby) — due process only.

**Authority:** *United States v. Wade*, 388 U.S. 218 (1967); *Gilbert v. California*, 388 U.S. 263 (1967); *Kirby v. Illinois*, 406 U.S. 682 (1972); *United States v. Ash*, 413 U.S. 300 (1973).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** After indictment, police hold a lineup without notifying D's lawyer. The lineup identification is excluded; the victim may identify D in court if her identification rests on her own clear view during the crime.

### R-CRM-107 — Identification: due process
**Rule (write this):** An identification procedure violates due process if police arranged a procedure that was unnecessarily suggestive and created a substantial likelihood of misidentification. Reliability is the linchpin: a suggestive identification is still admissible if reliable under the totality of the circumstances, considering the witness's opportunity to view, degree of attention, accuracy of the prior description, level of certainty, and the time between the crime and the identification.

**Elements:** 1. Police-arranged procedure. 2. Unnecessarily suggestive (single-photo, show-up without need, lineup where only the suspect fits). 3. Substantial likelihood of misidentification under the Biggers factors.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Suggestive circumstances not arranged by police are left to cross-examination and jury instructions (Perry). Necessity can justify a show-up (Stovall: dying witness).

**Authority:** *Stovall v. Denno*, 388 U.S. 293 (1967); *Neil v. Biggers*, 409 U.S. 188 (1972); *Manson v. Brathwaite*, 432 U.S. 98 (1977); *Foster v. California*, 394 U.S. 440 (1969); *Perry v. New Hampshire*, 565 U.S. 228 (2012).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule (Oct 2020 Q4 tested a lineup under the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments).

**Example:** A robbery victim who glimpsed the robber for three seconds is shown a single photo of D an hour later and says "I think that's him." Suggestive and unreliable; excluded.

## H. Fair trial, pleas, double jeopardy, punishment, burdens and appeal (outline V.E–V.I)

### R-CRM-108 — Pretrial: bail, probable-cause hearing, grand jury, preliminary hearing
**Rule (write this):** Bail may not be set higher than reasonably necessary to assure the defendant's appearance, but preventive detention of dangerous arrestees after an adversary hearing is constitutional. A person arrested without a warrant is entitled to a prompt judicial determination of probable cause, presumptively within 48 hours; the grand-jury requirement binds only federal prosecutions, and grand juries may consider evidence that would be inadmissible at trial.

**Elements:** 1. Bail (Stack; Salerno). 2. Gerstein/McLaughlin hearing. 3. Federal felonies → grand-jury indictment; states may use information after a preliminary hearing. 4. Preliminary hearing is a critical stage (counsel).

**Exceptions / defenses:** The prosecutor need not present exculpatory evidence to a grand jury (Williams); exclusionary rule does not apply there (Calandra).

**Authority:** U.S. Const. amends. V, VIII; *Stack v. Boyle*, 342 U.S. 1 (1951); *United States v. Salerno*, 481 U.S. 739 (1987); *Gerstein v. Pugh*, 420 U.S. 103 (1975); *County of Riverside v. McLaughlin*, 500 U.S. 44 (1991); *Hurtado v. California*, 110 U.S. 516 (1884); *Costello v. United States*, 350 U.S. 359 (1956); *Coleman v. Alabama*, 399 U.S. 1 (1970).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** D, arrested without a warrant on Friday night, is held until Wednesday without seeing a judge. The delay exceeds 48 hours; the state must show an emergency.

### R-CRM-109 — Speedy trial
**Rule (write this):** Whether the Sixth Amendment right to a speedy trial was violated is decided by balancing the length of the delay, the reason for the delay, the defendant's assertion of the right, and prejudice to the defendant. The right attaches at arrest or formal charge, and the remedy for a violation is dismissal with prejudice.

**Elements:** 1. Length (about a year triggers inquiry). 2. Reason (deliberate delay weighs heavily; negligence less; valid reasons none). 3. Assertion. 4. Prejudice (oppressive incarceration, anxiety, impaired defense).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Pre-charge delay is governed by statutes of limitations and due process; the period between conviction and sentencing is outside the Clause (Betterman).

**Authority:** U.S. Const. amend. VI; *Barker v. Wingo*, 407 U.S. 514 (1972); *Doggett v. United States*, 505 U.S. 647 (1992); *Betterman v. Montana*, 578 U.S. 437 (2016).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** The prosecution delays trial three years to gain tactical advantage while D, jailed, repeatedly demands trial and a key defense witness dies. Violation; dismissal with prejudice.

### R-CRM-110 — Jury trial
**Rule (write this):** A defendant has a right to a jury trial for any offense punishable by more than six months' imprisonment. The jury must have at least six members, must be unanimous to convict of a serious offense, must be drawn from a fair cross-section of the community, and may not be shaped by peremptory challenges based on race or sex.

**Elements:** 1. Serious offense (> 6 months authorized). 2. Size ≥ 6 (6-person jury must be unanimous). 3. Unanimity (Ramos). 4. Fair cross-section of the venire (Duren test). 5. Batson three-step procedure. 6. Impartiality (voir dire, publicity, death qualification).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Aggregated petty offenses do not trigger the right (Lewis). California requires 12 jurors and unanimity in felonies and adds Code Civ. Proc. § 231.7 for peremptory challenges.

**Authority:** U.S. Const. amend. VI; *Duncan v. Louisiana*, 391 U.S. 145 (1968); *Baldwin v. New York*, 399 U.S. 66 (1970); *Blanton v. City of North Las Vegas*, 489 U.S. 538 (1989); *Lewis v. United States*, 518 U.S. 322 (1996); *Ballew v. Georgia*, 435 U.S. 223 (1978); *Burch v. Louisiana*, 441 U.S. 130 (1979); *Ramos v. Louisiana* (2020) [VERIFY V-250]; *Duren v. Missouri*, 439 U.S. 357 (1979); *Batson v. Kentucky*, 476 U.S. 79 (1986); *J.E.B. v. Alabama ex rel. T.B.*, 511 U.S. 127 (1994); Cal. Const. art. I, § 16; Code Civ. Proc. § 231.7 [S-365].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same federal rule; California's 12-juror and § 231.7 rules only if asked.

**Example:** The prosecutor strikes all three Black venire members, explaining that they "seemed uninterested," while keeping white jurors who behaved the same way. Batson violation at step three.

### R-CRM-111 — Apprendi and sentencing facts
**Rule (write this):** Other than the fact of a prior conviction, any fact that increases the penalty for a crime beyond the prescribed statutory maximum, or increases a mandatory minimum, must be submitted to a jury and proved beyond a reasonable doubt. The relevant maximum is the highest sentence the judge may impose based solely on facts found by the jury or admitted by the defendant.

**Elements:** 1. A fact (not a prior conviction) that raises the ceiling or floor. 2. Found by the judge rather than the jury → violation. 3. Harmless-error review applies.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Advisory guidelines (Booker). In California, the upper term requires aggravating facts stipulated, found true beyond a reasonable doubt, or (prior convictions) shown by certified records, and the California Supreme Court requires a jury finding.

**Authority:** *Apprendi v. New Jersey*, 530 U.S. 466 (2000); *Blakely v. Washington*, 542 U.S. 296 (2004); *United States v. Booker*, 543 U.S. 220 (2005); *Alleyne v. United States*, 570 U.S. 99 (2013); *Cunningham v. California*, 549 U.S. 270 (2007); *Erlinger v. United States* (2024) [VERIFY V-035]; Cal. Pen. Code § 1170(b) [S-369]; *People v. Lynch* (2024) 16 Cal.5th 730 [S-133].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same federal rule; Lynch and § 1170(b) only on a California-sentencing call (Jul 2025 Q5 asked whether a sentence was proper).

**Example:** A jury convicts D of assault (maximum 5 years); the judge finds the crime was racially motivated and imposes 10 years under a hate-crime enhancement. Apprendi violation.

### R-CRM-112 — Confrontation Clause
**Rule (write this):** The Confrontation Clause bars admission of a testimonial statement by a witness who does not testify at trial unless the witness is unavailable and the defendant had a prior opportunity to cross-examine. A statement is testimonial when its primary purpose is to establish past facts for later prosecution, not to meet an ongoing emergency.

**Elements:** 1. Hearsay statement offered for its truth (basis testimony of an expert counts, Smith v. Arizona). 2. Testimonial (police interrogation after the emergency, affidavits, forensic reports). 3. Declarant absent. 4. Unavailable + prior opportunity to cross-examine, or forfeiture by wrongdoing.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Non-testimonial statements (911 calls about an ongoing emergency; statements to private persons). Forfeiture requires intent to prevent testimony (Giles). Bruton bars facially incriminating codefendant confessions at joint trials.

**Authority:** U.S. Const. amend. VI; *Crawford v. Washington*, 541 U.S. 36 (2004); *Davis v. Washington*, 547 U.S. 813 (2006); *Michigan v. Bryant*, 562 U.S. 344 (2011); *Melendez-Diaz v. Massachusetts*, 557 U.S. 305 (2009); *Bullcoming v. New Mexico*, 564 U.S. 647 (2011); *Ohio v. Clark*, 576 U.S. 237 (2015); *Giles v. California*, 554 U.S. 353 (2008); *Bruton v. United States*, 391 U.S. 123 (1968); *Smith v. Arizona* (2024) [VERIFY V-035].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule; it appears in Evidence/Criminal crossovers (Jul 2026 Q5).

**Example:** A domestic-violence victim tells an officer, an hour after the assault and with the suspect gone, what happened; she does not testify. Testimonial; inadmissible against the defendant.

### R-CRM-113 — Brady disclosure and false evidence
**Rule (write this):** Due process requires the prosecution to disclose evidence favorable to the accused, whether exculpatory or impeaching, that is material — meaning there is a reasonable probability that disclosure would have changed the result — regardless of the prosecutor's good or bad faith. The knowing use of false testimony, or failure to correct it, requires a new trial if there is any reasonable likelihood it affected the verdict.

**Elements:** 1. Favorable evidence. 2. Suppressed by the prosecution (including evidence known only to police). 3. Material (cumulative assessment). 4. False testimony: knowing use or failure to correct + reasonable likelihood of effect.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Failure to preserve merely potentially useful evidence violates due process only in bad faith (Youngblood).

**Authority:** *Brady v. Maryland*, 373 U.S. 83 (1963); *Giglio v. United States*, 405 U.S. 150 (1972); *United States v. Bagley*, 473 U.S. 667 (1985); *Kyles v. Whitley*, 514 U.S. 419 (1995); *Napue v. Illinois*, 360 U.S. 264 (1959); *Arizona v. Youngblood*, 488 U.S. 51 (1988); *Glossip v. Oklahoma* (2025) [VERIFY V-250].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule; also a PR crossover point (prosecutor's disclosure duties).

**Example:** The prosecution does not disclose that its key eyewitness was promised dismissal of his own charges. Giglio violation if the testimony was material.

### R-CRM-114 — Fair trial: competency, impartial judge, appearance, public trial
**Rule (write this):** Due process forbids trying a defendant who lacks a sufficient present ability to consult with counsel with a reasonable degree of rational understanding and a rational and factual understanding of the proceedings. It also requires an impartial judge, forbids compelling a defendant to appear in prison clothes or visible shackles without case-specific justification, and the Sixth Amendment guarantees a public trial absent an overriding interest.

**Elements:** 1. Competency (Dusky); court must inquire when there is bona fide doubt (Pate). 2. Judge with no financial interest or probability of actual bias. 3. Appearance (Estelle; Deck). 4. Public trial (Waller four-part test).

**Exceptions / defenses:** A state may place the burden of proving incompetence on the defendant by a preponderance (Medina) but not by clear and convincing evidence (Cooper).

**Authority:** *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402 (1960); *Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. 375 (1966); *Medina v. California*, 505 U.S. 437 (1992); *Cooper v. Oklahoma*, 517 U.S. 348 (1996); *Tumey v. Ohio*, 273 U.S. 510 (1927); *Caperton v. A.T. Massey Coal Co.*, 556 U.S. 868 (2009); *Estelle v. Williams*, 425 U.S. 501 (1976); *Deck v. Missouri*, 544 U.S. 622 (2005); *Waller v. Georgia*, 467 U.S. 39 (1984).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** A town mayor who keeps a share of the fines he imposes presides over D's traffic trial. Due process violation (Tumey).

### R-CRM-115 — Guilty pleas and plea bargaining
**Rule (write this):** A guilty plea is valid only if it is knowing, intelligent and voluntary, as shown on the record: the court must address the defendant personally and determine that he understands the nature of the charge, the maximum penalty and the rights he waives, and must find a factual basis. Plea bargains are enforceable against the prosecution, and a valid plea waives most non-jurisdictional defects before it.

**Elements:** 1. Understanding of the charge (elements). 2. Of the consequences (maximum, mandatory minimum). 3. Of rights waived (jury, confrontation, privilege). 4. Voluntary (no improper threats or promises). 5. Factual basis. 6. Competence.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Withdrawal or specific performance if the prosecution breaks the bargain (Santobello). An Alford plea is valid with strong evidence of guilt. Threatening greater charges in bargaining is permissible (Bordenkircher). A plea does not waive a claim that the statute is unconstitutional (Class). Appeal waivers are generally enforceable (Hunter).

**Authority:** *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238 (1969); *Henderson v. Morgan*, 426 U.S. 637 (1976); Fed. R. Crim. P. 11; *North Carolina v. Alford*, 400 U.S. 25 (1970); *Santobello v. New York*, 404 U.S. 257 (1971); *Bordenkircher v. Hayes*, 434 U.S. 357 (1978); *Brady v. United States*, 397 U.S. 742 (1970); *Tollett v. Henderson*, 411 U.S. 258 (1973); *Class v. United States*, 583 U.S. 174 (2018); *Hunter v. United States* (2026) [S-356] [VERIFY V-255].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule (Jul 2025 Q5 asked whether the judge properly accepted a plea).

**Example:** The judge accepts D's guilty plea to second-degree murder without explaining that intent to kill or serious bodily injury is an element, and nothing in the record shows D knew it. The plea is invalid (Henderson v. Morgan).

### R-CRM-116 — Double jeopardy: attachment, same offense, multiple punishment
**Rule (write this):** The Double Jeopardy Clause protects against a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal or conviction and against multiple punishments for the same offense. Jeopardy attaches when the jury is sworn, when the first witness is sworn in a bench trial, or when a guilty plea is accepted, and two offenses are the same unless each requires proof of an element the other does not.

**Elements:** 1. Attachment. 2. Same offense (Blockburger); a lesser-included offense is the same offense as the greater (Brown v. Ohio). 3. Multiple punishments in one trial allowed only if the legislature clearly authorized them.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A greater offense not complete at the time of the first prosecution (victim dies later) may be prosecuted. Civil sanctions are not punishment unless punitive in purpose or effect (Hudson).

**Authority:** U.S. Const. amend. V; *Benton v. Maryland*, 395 U.S. 784 (1969); *Crist v. Bretz*, 437 U.S. 28 (1978); *Blockburger v. United States*, 284 U.S. 299 (1932); *Brown v. Ohio*, 432 U.S. 161 (1977); *United States v. Dixon*, 509 U.S. 688 (1993); *Missouri v. Hunter*, 459 U.S. 359 (1983); *Barrett v. United States* (2026) [S-350] [VERIFY V-255].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** D is convicted of joyriding (taking a car without consent), then charged with auto theft (joyriding + intent to permanently deprive) for the same taking. Barred: joyriding is a lesser-included offense (Brown v. Ohio).

### R-CRM-117 — Double jeopardy: retrial, separate sovereigns, collateral estoppel
**Rule (write this):** An acquittal bars retrial, and conviction of a lesser offense is an implied acquittal of the greater. Retrial is permitted after a hung jury, after a mistrial declared for manifest necessity or at the defendant's request (unless the prosecution goaded the request), and after a successful defense appeal, except one based on insufficient evidence; separate sovereigns may each prosecute, and an issue of ultimate fact decided by an acquittal cannot be relitigated.

**Elements:** 1. Prior outcome (acquittal, conviction, mistrial, reversal). 2. Exception that permits retrial. 3. Same sovereign? 4. Collateral estoppel (Ashe).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Reversal on the weight (not sufficiency) of the evidence permits retrial (Tibbs). A city and its state are one sovereign.

**Authority:** *Green v. United States*, 355 U.S. 184 (1957); *Illinois v. Somerville*, 410 U.S. 458 (1973); *Oregon v. Kennedy*, 456 U.S. 667 (1982); *Burks v. United States*, 437 U.S. 1 (1978); *Tibbs v. Florida*, 457 U.S. 31 (1982); *Gamble v. United States*, 587 U.S. 678 (2019); *Waller v. Florida*, 397 U.S. 387 (1970); *Ashe v. Swenson*, 397 U.S. 436 (1970).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** D is acquitted by a state jury of robbing a bank; federal prosecutors then charge him with federal bank robbery. Permitted under dual sovereignty.

### R-CRM-118 — Cruel and unusual punishment
**Rule (write this):** The Eighth Amendment forbids punishments grossly disproportionate to the offense, punishment for mere status, and excessive fines. The death penalty is barred for non-homicide crimes against individuals, for offenders under 18 or intellectually disabled, and for felony-murder accomplices who did not kill, attempt or intend to kill unless they were major participants who acted with reckless indifference to human life.

**Elements:** 1. Non-capital: gross disproportionality (rarely met). 2. Capital: individualized sentencing; categorical bars (Coker, Kennedy, Enmund/Tison, Atkins, Roper). 3. Juveniles: no LWOP for non-homicide (Graham); no mandatory LWOP (Miller). 4. Status vs. conduct (Robinson; Powell; Grants Pass). 5. Excessive fines (Bajakajian; Timbs).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Long recidivist sentences are upheld (Ewing; Harmelin).

**Authority:** U.S. Const. amend. VIII; *Harmelin v. Michigan*, 501 U.S. 957 (1991); *Ewing v. California*, 538 U.S. 11 (2003); *Coker v. Georgia*, 433 U.S. 584 (1977); *Kennedy v. Louisiana*, 554 U.S. 407 (2008); *Enmund v. Florida*, 458 U.S. 782 (1982); *Tison v. Arizona*, 481 U.S. 137 (1987); *Atkins v. Virginia*, 536 U.S. 304 (2002); *Roper v. Simmons*, 543 U.S. 551 (2005); *Graham v. Florida*, 560 U.S. 48 (2010); *Miller v. Alabama*, 567 U.S. 460 (2012); *Robinson v. California*, 370 U.S. 660 (1962); *Powell v. Texas*, 392 U.S. 514 (1968); *City of Grants Pass v. Johnson* (2024) [S-366]; *Timbs v. Indiana*, 586 U.S. 146 (2019).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** A state sentences a 16-year-old to mandatory life without parole for a murder. Unconstitutional under Miller; the court must consider youth before imposing LWOP.

### R-CRM-119 — Burdens of proof, presumptions and affirmative defenses
**Rule (write this):** Due process requires the prosecution to prove every element of the charged crime beyond a reasonable doubt. A state may require the defendant to prove an affirmative defense that does not negate an element, but may not use a mandatory presumption that shifts to the defendant the burden of persuasion on an element.

**Elements:** 1. Identify the elements (Winship). 2. Affirmative defenses (insanity, EMED, self-defense, duress) may be placed on the defendant (Patterson; Martin). 3. Mandatory presumptions on elements → invalid (Sandstrom). 4. Permissive inferences → valid if rational on the facts (Ulster County).

**Exceptions / defenses:** If the absence of provocation is part of the definition of murder, the state cannot make the defendant prove provocation (Mullaney).

**Authority:** *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358 (1970); *Mullaney v. Wilbur*, 421 U.S. 684 (1975); *Patterson v. New York*, 432 U.S. 197 (1977); *Martin v. Ohio*, 480 U.S. 228 (1987); *Sandstrom v. Montana*, 442 U.S. 510 (1979); *County Court of Ulster County v. Allen*, 442 U.S. 140 (1979); *Jackson v. Virginia*, 443 U.S. 307 (1979).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** The judge instructs, "The law presumes that a person intends the ordinary consequences of his voluntary acts." In a murder trial where intent is contested, the instruction violates Sandstrom.

### R-CRM-120 — Appeal and error
**Rule (write this):** There is no federal constitutional right to appeal, but a state that grants appeals must provide indigents with counsel for the first appeal as of right and with transcripts. Most constitutional errors are harmless if the prosecution proves beyond a reasonable doubt that they did not contribute to the verdict, while structural errors — such as complete denial of counsel, a biased judge, or a defective reasonable-doubt instruction — require automatic reversal.

**Elements:** 1. Preserved error → harmless-error review (Chapman). 2. Structural error → automatic reversal. 3. Unpreserved error → plain-error test (error, plain, affects substantial rights, seriously affects fairness). 4. Sufficiency → any rational trier of fact (Jackson v. Virginia). 5. Federal habeas → AEDPA deference; Stone v. Powell; Brecht harmlessness.

**Exceptions / defenses:** New procedural rules do not apply retroactively on federal collateral review (Teague; Edwards v. Vannoy).

**Authority:** *McKane v. Durston*, 153 U.S. 684 (1894); *Griffin v. Illinois*, 351 U.S. 12 (1956); *Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18 (1967); *Arizona v. Fulminante*, 499 U.S. 279 (1991); *Neder v. United States*, 527 U.S. 1 (1999); *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725 (1993); Fed. R. Crim. P. 52(b); 28 U.S.C. § 2254(d); *Brecht v. Abrahamson*, 507 U.S. 619 (1993); *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288 (1989); *Edwards v. Vannoy* (2021) [VERIFY V-250].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** A trial court erroneously admits a coerced confession, but three eyewitnesses and video show D committing the crime. The conviction may be affirmed if the error was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt (Fulminante).
