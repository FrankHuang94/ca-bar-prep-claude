# Criminal Law and Procedure — worked examples

Original fact patterns. Each gives the rule (with its ID in [rule-statements.md](rule-statements.md)), the application and a conclusion. Cover the analysis and try each one first.

## Homicide

### WE-01 — The parking-lot argument
**Facts.** During a shouting match over a parking space, V calls D a coward and spits on his shoes. D goes to his car, takes a tire iron from the trunk, walks back and strikes V on the head. V dies.

**Rule.** Murder requires malice; an intentional killing in a sudden heat of passion from adequate provocation is voluntary manslaughter (R-CRM-031, R-CRM-034).

**Application.** Striking the head with a tire iron supports intent to kill or at least intent to inflict serious bodily injury (R-CRM-032, R-CRM-035). Insults and spitting on shoes are words and a trivial touching; most courts hold words alone inadequate, and a reasonable person would not lose self-control over this. Walking to the car and returning is evidence of some reflection, which also weakens passion and supports premeditation arguments (R-CRM-033).

**Conclusion.** Murder, likely second degree (the reflection was brief); voluntary manslaughter is unlikely because the provocation was not adequate.

### WE-02 — The getaway driver and the clerk
**Facts.** A and B plan to rob a convenience store. B, the driver, waits outside. Inside, the clerk pulls a gun and fires at A, missing him and killing a customer.

**Rule.** Felony murder applies to killings during an inherently dangerous felony; under the majority agency theory, felons are not liable for killings by non-felons (R-CRM-039, R-CRM-041).

**Application.** Robbery is an enumerated, inherently dangerous felony, and the death occurred during it. But the clerk, not a felon, fired the fatal shot. Under the agency rule, neither A nor B is guilty of felony murder; under the proximate-cause rule, both are, because armed resistance to an armed robbery is foreseeable. Both are guilty of robbery (B as an accomplice, R-CRM-069) and conspiracy (R-CRM-065).

**Conclusion.** MBE default: no felony murder. Essay: argue both theories and conclude each.

### WE-03 — The fight and the hospital
**Facts.** D stabs V in the abdomen in a bar fight. At the hospital, a resident misreads V's chart and gives him the wrong antibiotic, and V dies of an allergic reaction. Without the error, V would have recovered.

**Rule.** Ordinary medical negligence is a foreseeable, dependent intervening cause that does not break the chain of proximate causation (R-CRM-013).

**Application.** D's stabbing is a but-for cause: V would not have been in the hospital (R-CRM-012). Negligent treatment of an injury is a normal consequence of inflicting it; only gross, independent maltreatment that is the sole cause would supersede.

**Conclusion.** D is the proximate cause; D is liable for V's death (murder or manslaughter depending on mental state).

### WE-04 — The drag race
**Facts.** D and X race their cars at 100 mph on a city street at night. X loses control and kills a pedestrian.

**Rule.** Depraved-heart murder requires conscious disregard of an extremely high risk to life; an accomplice to reckless conduct is liable if he has the culpability required for the result (R-CRM-036, R-CRM-069).

**Application.** Racing at 100 mph on a city street creates an obvious, very high risk of death, and both drivers were aware of it. D intentionally participated in and encouraged the joint conduct that caused the death; under MPC § 2.06(4), he shares liability for the result if he had the required recklessness.

**Conclusion.** X is likely guilty of depraved-heart murder (or at least involuntary manslaughter); D is liable as an accomplice to the same homicide.

## Other crimes

### WE-05 — The borrowed laptop
**Facts.** E's employer lets her take a company laptop home for work. After she is fired, she sells it online.

**Rule.** Embezzlement is fraudulent conversion by one in lawful possession; larceny requires a trespassory taking (R-CRM-044, R-CRM-045).

**Application.** E received the laptop with permission to take it home — lawful possession, not mere custody, because she had authority over it away from the employer's premises. Selling it is conversion with intent to defraud. There was no trespassory taking at the start.

**Conclusion.** Embezzlement, not larceny.

### WE-06 — The rental car
**Facts.** D rents a car using his own name, intending never to return it, and drives it to another state where he sells it.

**Rule.** Fraud that obtains possession only is larceny by trick; fraud that obtains title is false pretenses (R-CRM-046).

**Application.** The rental company gave possession, not title. D's intent to keep the car existed at the time he obtained it.

**Conclusion.** Larceny by trick.

### WE-07 — The purse on the bus
**Facts.** D lifts a wallet from V's coat pocket on a crowded bus; V does not notice. Moments later V sees D and grabs his arm; D shoves V away and runs.

**Rule.** Robbery requires force or intimidation to take property from the person or presence; at common law the force must accompany the taking, while modern statutes include force used to retain or escape (R-CRM-049).

**Application.** The taking itself was a stealthy pickpocketing (larceny). The force came afterward, to escape.

**Conclusion.** Common law (MBE default): larceny plus battery, not robbery. Under the modern and California view, robbery.

### WE-08 — Shelter from the storm
**Facts.** Caught in a flash flood, D pries open the back door of a closed mountain store at night to escape rising water. Inside, warm and safe, he notices a display of watches and pockets one.

**Rule.** Burglary requires intent to commit a felony at the time of entry; necessity justifies conduct to avoid a greater imminent harm (R-CRM-050, R-CRM-027).

**Application.** D's purpose at entry was survival, not theft, so the intent element fails; the entry itself is justified by necessity (flood waters, no alternative). The watch theft was formed after entry.

**Conclusion.** No burglary; larceny of the watch.

### WE-09 — The fence and the sting
**Facts.** Police arrest a thief with a stolen sculpture and persuade him to deliver it, as planned, to D, a dealer, who pays half its value and says "I'll keep this one quiet."

**Rule.** Receiving requires that the property be stolen when received; under the modern rule, legal impossibility is no defense to attempt (R-CRM-048, R-CRM-062).

**Application.** Once police recovered the sculpture and used it with the owner's consent, it lost its stolen character, so the completed crime fails. D believed it stolen (low price, secrecy) and took a substantial step by paying.

**Conclusion.** Not guilty of receiving; guilty of attempted receipt under the modern rule (the traditional legal-impossibility view would acquit).

### WE-10 — The garage fire
**Facts.** On a freezing night, D breaks a garage window behind an occupied house, lights a small fire in a metal bucket to keep warm, and falls asleep. A spark ignites rags, and the garage wall chars before neighbors put it out.

**Rule.** Arson is the malicious burning of the dwelling of another; malice includes recklessness but not mere negligence; necessity may justify entry (R-CRM-058, R-CRM-027, R-CRM-050).

**Application.** The garage is within the curtilage, so it counts as part of the dwelling; the wall charred. Malice: lighting a fire indoors near rags and sleeping beside it is arguably reckless (a known risk consciously disregarded), but D used a metal bucket, suggesting she tried to contain it — arguably only negligence. Burglary fails for lack of intent to commit a felony on entry; trespass may be excused by necessity if the cold posed an imminent danger with no alternative.

**Conclusion.** Arson turns on recklessness vs. negligence (argue both; modern reckless-burning statutes fit more easily). No burglary.

## Inchoate crimes and parties

### WE-11 — The hit man who was a cop
**Facts.** W offers an undercover officer $5,000 to kill her husband and gives him a photo and the husband's schedule. The officer arrests her.

**Rule.** Solicitation is complete on the request; conspiracy needs an agreement — bilateral at common law, unilateral under the MPC (R-CRM-064, R-CRM-065).

**Application.** W asked another to commit murder intending that it be committed: solicitation. The officer never truly agreed, so there is no conspiracy under the bilateral rule; under the unilateral rule, W agreed and handing over the photo and schedule is an overt act. Attempt? Giving materials to a purported hit man is likely a substantial step under the MPC (soliciting an agent) but not dangerous proximity.

**Conclusion.** Solicitation in all jurisdictions; conspiracy only in unilateral jurisdictions; attempted murder possible under the MPC.

### WE-12 — The partner who quit
**Facts.** A, B and C agree to rob an armored truck. A buys masks. Two days later A tells B, "I'm out," but says nothing to C. On the day, B and C rob the truck and C shoots a guard.

**Rule.** Conspiracy is complete on agreement plus overt act; withdrawal avoids later Pinkerton liability only if communicated to all co-conspirators (R-CRM-065, R-CRM-067, R-CRM-068).

**Application.** The agreement and mask purchase completed the conspiracy, so A remains guilty of conspiracy. A's notice reached only B, not C, so it was ineffective to cut off Pinkerton liability. The robbery was the object; shooting a guard during an armed truck robbery is foreseeable and in furtherance.

**Conclusion.** A is liable for conspiracy, robbery and the shooting under Pinkerton (not under the MPC unless A was an accomplice).

### WE-13 — The lookout
**Facts.** L knows that his friend P plans to break into a house. L walks with P to the house and stands on the sidewalk, saying nothing, while P breaks in. They had not discussed L's role.

**Rule.** Accomplice liability requires aid or encouragement with the purpose that the crime be committed; mere presence is not enough (R-CRM-069).

**Application.** L's presence, with knowledge, is not aid unless it was an agreed signal of support or L acted as lookout. No prior agreement; no act. The prosecution could argue presence encouraged P, but without more the inference is weak.

**Conclusion.** Likely not an accomplice.

## General principles and defenses

### WE-14 — Drunk at the party
**Facts.** Very drunk, D picks up a coat at a party believing it is his. It belongs to X.

**Rule.** Voluntary intoxication and honest mistake of fact can negate a specific intent (R-CRM-018, R-CRM-010).

**Application.** Larceny requires intent to permanently deprive another of his property. D believed the coat his, so he lacked that intent; the belief need not be reasonable for a specific-intent crime.

**Conclusion.** Not guilty of larceny.

### WE-15 — The seizure at the wheel
**Facts.** D, who has had several seizures and was told by her doctor not to drive, drives to work, has a seizure and crashes into a crosswalk, killing a child.

**Rule.** A voluntary act is required; liability can rest on a prior voluntary act made with the required culpability (R-CRM-002, R-CRM-037).

**Application.** The seizure itself is involuntary, but deciding to drive despite medical warnings was a voluntary act that consciously or grossly disregarded a substantial risk.

**Conclusion.** Involuntary manslaughter (criminal negligence or recklessness); depraved-heart murder is unlikely absent extreme awareness.

### WE-16 — The persistent informant
**Facts.** A police informant asks D, a college student with no record, eight times over two months to sell him "a little" cocaine for his "dying sister's pain." D finally finds some and sells it.

**Rule.** Entrapment: government inducement and lack of predisposition (subjective, majority), or police conduct likely to induce a normally law-abiding person (objective, MPC and California) (R-CRM-029).

**Application.** The informant acted for police (government agent). Repeated pleas and a sympathy appeal go beyond offering an opportunity. D had no record and no contacts, so predisposition is weak. An ordinary law-abiding person might yield to persistent emotional appeals.

**Conclusion.** Entrapment likely succeeds under both tests.

### WE-17 — The retreat that was possible
**Facts.** In a public park, V, much larger, approaches D with a knife. D could easily step into a nearby café and lock the door, but instead shoots V.

**Rule.** Deadly force is permitted against an imminent threat of death or serious injury; the majority imposes no duty to retreat, while the minority and MPC require retreat if completely safe, except in the home (R-CRM-020, R-CRM-021).

**Application.** A knife attack threatens death or serious injury, so deadly force was proportional. Retreat was available in complete safety.

**Conclusion.** Justified in a no-retreat (majority) jurisdiction; not justified in a retreat jurisdiction (possible imperfect self-defense → voluntary manslaughter).

## Fourth Amendment

### WE-18 — The handcuffed driver
**Facts.** Police stop D for an expired registration and arrest him on an outstanding warrant for unpaid fines. He is handcuffed in the patrol car when officers search his car and find a gun under the seat.

**Rule.** A car may be searched incident to arrest only if the arrestee is unsecured and within reach or it is reasonable to believe evidence of the arrest offense is inside (R-CRM-083).

**Application.** D was secured. Evidence of unpaid fines would not be in the car. The automobile exception requires probable cause, which is absent (R-CRM-084). An inventory search is possible only if the car was lawfully impounded and searched under standard procedures (R-CRM-088).

**Conclusion.** The search is invalid under Gant; the gun is suppressed unless the prosecution proves inevitable discovery through a standardized inventory (R-CRM-091).

### WE-19 — The overnight guest and the visitor
**Facts.** Police unlawfully enter H's apartment and find drugs in a bag belonging to G, who has stayed there for a week, and in a bag belonging to C, who came an hour earlier to buy drugs.

**Rule.** Standing requires the defendant's own reasonable expectation of privacy (R-CRM-075).

**Application.** G, an overnight guest, has a legitimate expectation of privacy in the host's home (Olson). C, present briefly for a commercial transaction, does not (Carter) — though C may still have a privacy interest in his own closed bag (an "effect") if the officers opened it.

**Conclusion.** G can move to suppress; C cannot challenge the entry, but can challenge the search of his own bag.

### WE-20 — The 911 call
**Facts.** A neighbor calls 911 reporting screaming and a crash inside D's house. Officers arrive, see through a window a woman with a bloody face, knock without answer, and enter. They see a bag of cocaine on the counter.

**Rule.** Police may enter without a warrant when they have an objectively reasonable basis to believe an occupant needs emergency aid; plain view allows seizure of evidence seen while lawfully present (R-CRM-087, R-CRM-085).

**Application.** The 911 report, the visible injury and the failure to answer give an objectively reasonable basis to believe someone needs immediate aid (Brigham City; Case v. Montana — probable cause not required). Once inside lawfully, the cocaine's incriminating character was immediately apparent.

**Conclusion.** Entry and seizure valid; the cocaine is admissible.

### WE-21 — The tip and the jacket
**Facts.** An anonymous caller reports that "a man in a red jacket at Fifth and Main is selling drugs." Officers see D in a red jacket at that corner, standing alone, and frisk him, finding a pocketknife and a bag of heroin they identify by squeezing.

**Rule.** A Terry stop needs reasonable suspicion; a bare anonymous tip identifying a person in a public place is not enough; a frisk needs reasonable belief of danger and may not exceed a pat-down (R-CRM-077, R-CRM-078).

**Application.** The tip gave only readily observable details and no predictive information; police corroborated nothing suspicious (J.L.). Even if the stop were valid, nothing suggested D was armed and dangerous, and squeezing to identify the heroin exceeded plain feel (Dickerson).

**Conclusion.** Stop and frisk invalid; the heroin is suppressed.

## Statements and identification

### WE-22 — "I think I want a lawyer"
**Facts.** In custody and after valid warnings, D waives and answers questions about a burglary. After an hour he says, "I think I want a lawyer." The detective says, "Do you? Let's just finish this," and D confesses.

**Rule.** Invocation of counsel must be unambiguous; once invoked, interrogation must stop (R-CRM-098).

**Application.** "I think I want a lawyer" is closer to an unambiguous request than Davis's "Maybe I should talk to a lawyer," but courts split on "I think." The detective's response did not clarify; it pressed on. The prosecution will cite Davis; the defense will argue a reasonable officer would understand a request.

**Conclusion.** Likely an invocation (the statement expresses a present desire), so the confession is inadmissible in the case-in-chief; usable to impeach if voluntary (R-CRM-100).

### WE-23 — The cellmate after indictment
**Facts.** After D is indicted for arson, police place informant I in D's cell with instructions to "find out what you can." I repeatedly asks D about the fire, and D admits setting it. D also mentions an unrelated burglary.

**Rule.** After the Sixth Amendment attaches, police may not deliberately elicit statements about the charged offense; Miranda does not apply to undercover questioning (R-CRM-102, R-CRM-095).

**Application.** I acted as a government agent and actively questioned D (Henry, not Kuhlmann). The arson statements violate the Sixth Amendment. The burglary is uncharged; the Sixth Amendment is offense-specific, and Miranda does not apply because D did not know I was an agent (Perkins).

**Conclusion.** Suppress the arson statements (case-in-chief); the burglary statement is admissible.

### WE-24 — The show-up
**Facts.** Twenty minutes after a mugging, police drive the victim past D, who is handcuffed next to a squad car two blocks away. The victim, who saw the mugger for 30 seconds under a streetlight and described his distinctive green jacket and beard, says "That's him." D wears a green jacket and a beard.

**Rule.** Identification violates due process if police used an unnecessarily suggestive procedure creating a substantial likelihood of misidentification; reliability under the Biggers factors is decisive (R-CRM-107).

**Application.** A one-person show-up with the suspect in handcuffs is suggestive, though prompt show-ups are often justified to release the innocent quickly. Reliability: a 30-second view under a streetlight, an accurate and specific prior description, a confident identification, and only 20 minutes elapsed.

**Conclusion.** Suggestive but reliable; admissible. No Sixth Amendment issue (no charges filed, R-CRM-106).

### WE-25 — Two trials for one taking
**Facts.** D is convicted in municipal court of joyriding for taking V's car. The county prosecutor then charges D in state court with auto theft for the same taking.

**Rule.** A lesser-included offense and the greater offense are the same offense under Blockburger; a city and its state are one sovereign (R-CRM-116, R-CRM-117).

**Application.** Auto theft = joyriding + intent to permanently deprive, so joyriding has no element that auto theft lacks. The municipal court is an arm of the state (Waller v. Florida).

**Conclusion.** The second prosecution is barred by double jeopardy.
