# Criminal Law and Procedure — primer

Read this before the [outline](outline.md). It gives you the map, the vocabulary and the handful of ideas that decide most questions.

## 1. Two subjects in one

**Criminal law** (substantive) asks: *did this person commit a crime, and is there a defense?* It is mostly **state** law, built on English common law and, in many states, reorganized along the lines of the **Model Penal Code (MPC)**, a 1962 model statute drafted by the American Law Institute. The MPC is not law anywhere by itself, but it shapes most modern codes and is the standard comparison on the bar.

**Criminal procedure** (constitutional) asks: *did the government follow the Constitution in investigating, charging and trying this person?* It is **federal constitutional law**, applied to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment. The key provisions are the **Fourth Amendment** (searches and seizures), **Fifth** (self-incrimination, double jeopardy, due process), **Sixth** (counsel, jury, confrontation, speedy trial) and **Eighth** (cruel and unusual punishment, excessive bail and fines).

On the MBE the two halves get roughly equal weight [S-040]. On California essays, procedure — especially the Fourth Amendment and Miranda — appears more often than substance, and the two are often combined in one question ("What crimes can D be charged with, and can he suppress the gun?").

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: In PRC terms, think of substantive criminal law as the Criminal Law (刑法) and procedure as the Criminal Procedure Law (刑事诉讼法) — except that in the US the core procedural rules are constitutional rights enforced by excluding evidence (approx.).

## 2. Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Actus reus** | The physical element: a voluntary act, or an omission where there is a legal duty to act |
| **Mens rea** | The mental element: intent, knowledge, recklessness, negligence (or none, for strict liability) |
| **Specific intent / general intent** | Common-law labels; specific-intent crimes require an extra purpose (larceny: intent to permanently deprive). They matter for intoxication and mistake defenses |
| **Malice aforethought** | The mental state for murder: intent to kill, intent to cause serious injury, extreme recklessness, or felony murder |
| **Inchoate crime** | An incomplete crime: attempt, solicitation, conspiracy |
| **Accomplice** | One who intentionally aids or encourages the crime; liable as the principal |
| **Justification / excuse** | Defenses: justification says the act was right (self-defense, necessity); excuse says the actor is not blameworthy (insanity, duress) |
| **Probable cause** | A fair probability (search) or reasonable belief (arrest) based on facts — required for warrants and arrests |
| **Reasonable suspicion** | Less than probable cause; specific, articulable facts — enough for a brief stop (Terry) |
| **Exclusionary rule** | Evidence obtained unconstitutionally is excluded at trial; "fruit of the poisonous tree" extends it to derivative evidence |
| **Custodial interrogation** | The trigger for Miranda warnings |
| **Attachment** | The point (formal charges) when the Sixth Amendment right to counsel begins |
| **Jeopardy** | The point (jury sworn) after which a second trial for the same offense is barred |

## 3. How the pieces connect

**Every crime = act + mental state + (for result crimes) causation + no defense.** Homicide, theft and the rest are just specific combinations of these building blocks. Learn the building blocks (outline IV) first, and the specific crimes become checklists.

**Inchoate crimes and accomplice liability extend liability** beyond the person who completes the crime: to someone who tried and failed (attempt), asked another to do it (solicitation), agreed to do it (conspiracy), or helped (accomplice).

**Defenses come in two families.** Some **negate an element** (mistake of fact, intoxication — they show the defendant lacked the required mental state). Others are **affirmative defenses** that admit the elements but justify or excuse the act (self-defense, necessity, duress, insanity, entrapment).

**Procedure follows the life of a case:** investigation (search, seizure, interrogation, identification) → charging and pretrial (bail, grand jury, preliminary hearing, counsel) → trial (jury, confrontation, burden of proof) → plea or verdict → sentence (Eighth Amendment, Apprendi) → appeal (harmless error) → and the bar on re-prosecution (double jeopardy).

**The remedy drives the procedure questions.** On an essay, the defendant almost always wants evidence **suppressed**. So each item of evidence is analyzed for a constitutional violation and then for whether exclusion is the remedy (standing, fruits, good faith, attenuation).

## 4. The ideas that matter most

1. **Mental state is the hinge of substantive criminal law.** Most MBE criminal-law questions turn on whether the defendant had the required mental state: was larceny's intent present at the taking? Was the killing intentional, reckless or negligent? Does intoxication matter (only for specific intent)? Read every fact for what the defendant knew and intended, and **when**.

2. **Murder has four malice states, and manslaughter is the safety net.** Intent to kill, intent to cause serious bodily injury, depraved-heart recklessness and felony murder each make a killing murder. Provocation and imperfect self-defense knock an intentional killing down to voluntary manslaughter; criminal negligence and unlawful-act killings are involuntary manslaughter. See [flowcharts.md](flowcharts.md) chart 1.

3. **Attempt and conspiracy are specific-intent crimes, and they behave differently.** Attempt merges into the completed crime; conspiracy does not. Conspirators are liable for each other's foreseeable crimes (Pinkerton); attempt has no such rule. The MPC differs on several points (unilateral conspiracy, substantial step, renunciation), and the MBE often tells you which rule applies.

4. **The Fourth Amendment analysis has a fixed order.** Government action → standing → was there a "search" or "seizure"? → was it justified (warrant with probable cause, or an exception: incident to arrest, automobile, plain view, consent, exigency, stop and frisk, special needs)? → is exclusion the remedy (fruit of the poisonous tree; independent source, inevitable discovery, attenuation; good faith)? Follow it item by item on every essay.

5. **Miranda applies only to custodial interrogation.** No custody (a traffic stop, a voluntary interview) or no interrogation (a volunteered statement) → no warnings needed. If the suspect invokes counsel, everything stops (Edwards); if he invokes silence, police must scrupulously honor it (Mosley). Unwarned but voluntary statements can still impeach, and their physical fruits are admissible.

6. **Two separate rights to counsel.** Miranda's counsel right (Fifth Amendment) protects against custodial interrogation and must be invoked; the Sixth Amendment right attaches automatically when charges are filed and is offense-specific. Confusing them is the most common error in this area.

7. **California essays use general principles.** For crimes, state the common-law rule and note the MPC; for procedure, the federal Constitution controls — and California's Truth-in-Evidence clause means California courts exclude only what federal law requires [S-145]. California Penal Code rules (felony murder after SB 1437, the objective entrapment test) matter only if the call asks. See [ca-distinctions.md](ca-distinctions.md).

8. **Due process sets the floor for trials.** Proof beyond a reasonable doubt on every element (Winship); disclosure of favorable evidence (Brady); confrontation of testimonial hearsay (Crawford); a jury for serious offenses, unanimous; and any fact raising the maximum sentence goes to the jury (Apprendi).

## 5. How to study it (with a civil-law background)

- **Think in elements.** US criminal law, like PRC "constitution of crime" theory (犯罪构成), breaks crimes into elements, but the common-law element lists are narrower and more technical (breaking, nighttime, dwelling for burglary). Learn each list exactly.
- **Learn the majority/minority/MPC splits as tables.** The MBE rewards knowing which rule applies; the essay rewards showing you know both.
- **Case names are handles.** For procedure, the case name usually *is* the rule ("Miranda," "Terry," "Gant," "Edwards"). Learn the name with a one-line holding — [key-authorities.md](key-authorities.md) lists them.
- **Practise the item-by-item essay format** early. Graders reward the structure: one heading per item of evidence or per defendant, a rule, facts on both sides, and a conclusion for each.
