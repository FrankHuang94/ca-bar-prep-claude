# Constitutional Law — worked examples

Original fact patterns. Each answer gives the rule (with its ID), the application and a conclusion, in the short IRAC form you should use on essays. Cover the answer and try each one first.

---

### 1. The graduated student (mootness)
**Facts.** Lin, a high-school junior, was suspended for wearing a pin the district says is a "gang symbol." She sued for an injunction, a declaration that the rule is invalid, and $1 in nominal damages. Before trial she graduated; the district moves to dismiss as moot.

**Rule (R-CON-016).** A case is moot if no live controversy remains, subject to exceptions including voluntary cessation, capable-of-repetition, and surviving damages claims.

**Application.** Lin can no longer be disciplined under the rule, so her injunction and declaratory claims are moot as to her. Capable-of-repetition fails because she will not return to the school. Her nominal-damages claim redresses a completed injury (*Uzuegbunam*), and if the suspension remains on her record, expungement is effective relief.

**Conclusion.** Dismiss the injunctive and declaratory claims; the damages (and expungement) claims survive.

### 2. The would-be intervenor (standing)
**Facts.** A state sues to invalidate conditions in a federal school-fitness grant law. A national advocacy group that supports the law, but receives no grant money, moves to intervene to defend it after the federal government signals a weak defence.

**Rule (R-CON-014).** An intervenor seeking relief different from that of an existing party, or an appellant proceeding alone, must show its own Article III standing; ideological interest is not enough.

**Application.** The group's only stake is its policy preference; no member loses money or suffers a concrete harm if the law falls. If it seeks only to support the United States' position, it may participate as amicus curiae.

**Conclusion.** No standing to intervene to seek its own relief; it may file an amicus brief.

### 3. Pre-enforcement challenge (ripeness)
**Facts.** A city ordinance effective next week bans "open flames in places of worship." A congregation that lights candles at every service sues today; the fire chief has announced inspections.

**Rule (R-CON-015).** A pre-enforcement challenge is ripe if the plaintiff intends conduct arguably protected by the Constitution, the conduct is arguably proscribed, and enforcement is a credible threat.

**Application.** Candle lighting is religious exercise, is squarely banned, and the chief's announcement makes enforcement credible. The issues are legal and the congregation faces a choice between its practice and penalties.

**Conclusion.** Ripe.

### 4. Suing the state (Eleventh Amendment)
**Facts.** Employees of a state university sue the university in federal court for back overtime under the federal Fair Labor Standards Act, and ask for an injunction requiring future compliance.

**Rule (R-CON-005, R-CON-006).** A state and its arms are immune from private damages suits in federal court; Congress cannot abrogate under the Commerce Clause; prospective relief is available against state officers under *Ex parte Young*.

**Application.** The university is an arm of the state; back pay is retrospective relief from the treasury; the FLSA rests on the commerce power (*Seminole Tribe*). An injunction against the university's president to comply in the future fits *Ex parte Young*.

**Conclusion.** Back-pay claim barred; amend to sue the president for prospective relief.

### 5. The federal gun-near-school law redux (Commerce Clause)
**Facts.** Congress makes it a crime to possess a knife within 500 feet of a public park, citing the cost of violent crime to tourism. There is no jurisdictional element.

**Rule (R-CON-022).** Congress may regulate activities that substantially affect interstate commerce; noneconomic activity cannot be aggregated, and attenuated links do not suffice.

**Application.** Possessing a knife is not economic activity. The link to tourism runs through a chain of inferences like the one rejected in *Lopez*; no jurisdictional element ties a given knife to interstate commerce.

**Conclusion.** Beyond the commerce power; invalid.

### 6. Coercive grant condition (spending power)
**Facts.** Congress tells states they will lose all federal education funding (about 12% of state budgets) unless they require one hour of daily physical education in every school.

**Rule (R-CON-024).** Spending conditions must be for the general welfare, unambiguous, related to the federal interest, not barred by another provision, and not coercive.

**Application.** The condition is clear, related to federal education funding, and serves general welfare. But it threatens **existing** funds on a scale comparable to *NFIB*'s Medicaid threat, leaving states no real choice.

**Conclusion.** Likely unconstitutionally coercive; a condition attached only to new PE money would be valid.

### 7. Background checks by sheriffs (commandeering)
**Facts.** A federal statute requires county sheriffs to verify the immigration status of anyone they arrest and report to a federal database within 48 hours.

**Rule (R-CON-046).** Congress may not conscript state or local executive officers to administer a federal program.

**Application.** The statute orders local officers to perform federal investigative work, like the Brady Act checks in *Printz*. It does not regulate private actors or attach the duty to funding.

**Conclusion.** Invalid commandeering, unless restructured as a funding condition or voluntary program.

### 8. The fired commissioner (removal)
**Facts.** A federal Trade Practices Commission of five members enforces consumer-protection statutes, issues binding rules, and sues violators. Its statute allows removal only for "neglect of duty." The President removes a commissioner over policy.

**Rule (R-CON-036).** The President may remove principal officers who wield executive power at will; *Humphrey's Executor* is overruled (*Trump v. Slaughter* (2026) [VERIFY V-034]).

**Application.** The commission's rulemaking, enforcement and litigation powers are substantial executive power, as with the FTC in *Slaughter*. The for-cause limit therefore impermissibly restricts the President's control.

**Conclusion.** Removal valid; the statutory restriction is unconstitutional.

### 9. The waste-import ban (dormant Commerce Clause)
**Facts.** State Y bans private landfills from accepting waste generated out of state, citing limited landfill space.

**Rule (R-CON-047).** A law that discriminates on its face against interstate commerce is virtually per se invalid unless necessary to a legitimate local purpose with no nondiscriminatory alternative.

**Application.** The ban treats identical waste differently by origin. Conserving space is legitimate, but in-state waste uses space equally; a volume cap for all waste is a nondiscriminatory alternative.

**Conclusion.** Invalid (*City of Philadelphia v. New Jersey*).

### 10. The state-owned railroad (market participant and P&I)
**Facts.** State X owns a short-line railroad and gives scarce freight slots first to State X manufacturers. A State Y melon-growing corporation and an individual State Y farmer sue.

**Rule (R-CON-049, R-CON-053).** A state acting as a market participant may favor residents without violating the DCC; Article IV P&I protects only individual citizens' fundamental rights, with no market-participant exception.

**Application.** Selling its own rail service is participation, not regulation, so the DCC claim likely fails for both plaintiffs (unless State X attaches downstream conditions). The corporation cannot invoke P&I. The individual farmer can: shipping goods to earn a living is a fundamental commercial right, and State X must show nonresidents are a peculiar source of the scarcity problem.

**Conclusion.** DCC claims fail; the farmer's P&I claim likely succeeds; the corporation loses.

### 11. Stricter state labeling (preemption)
**Facts.** A federal statute sets minimum nutrition-label requirements and says states may not impose "requirements not identical to" federal labeling rules. A state requires an additional warning on sugary drinks' labels.

**Rule (R-CON-051).** Express preemption displaces state law within the clause's scope; no presumption against preemption applies to the text of an express clause.

**Application.** The state warning is a labeling requirement not identical to federal rules — within the express clause's terms.

**Conclusion.** Preempted. (A point-of-sale sign, not a label, would raise a closer question of scope.)

### 12. Private club and state liquor licence (state action)
**Facts.** A private social club holding a state liquor licence refuses to serve a guest because of his race. He sues the club under the Equal Protection Clause.

**Rule (R-CON-054, R-CON-055).** Licensing and regulation do not make private conduct state action absent compulsion, joint action, entanglement or public function.

**Application.** The state licensed the club but did not require or encourage the discrimination (*Moose Lodge*). Running a club is not an exclusive public function.

**Conclusion.** No state action; the equal-protection claim fails (a federal or state civil-rights statute might apply).

### 13. Teacher fired without a hearing (procedural due process)
**Facts.** Ana, a public-school teacher with five years of service under a statute providing dismissal "only for cause" after three years, is fired by letter for posting criticism of the principal online.

**Rule (R-CON-057, R-CON-058).** A for-cause job is property; before termination the employee needs notice, an explanation of the evidence and a chance to respond, followed by a full post-termination hearing (*Loudermill*).

**Application.** The statute creates an entitlement, so Ana has property. A letter with no opportunity to respond fails the pre-termination minimum. (Her speech claim is separate: example 21.)

**Conclusion.** Procedural due process violated.

### 14. Grandparent visitation (substantive due process)
**Facts.** A state statute lets any person petition for visitation with a child whenever a judge finds it in the child's best interest, over a fit parent's objection.

**Rule (R-CON-061).** Parents' right to the care, custody and control of their children is fundamental; courts must give special weight to a fit parent's decisions.

**Application.** The statute lets a judge override a fit mother's decision with no deference, as in *Troxel* (plurality).

**Conclusion.** Unconstitutional as applied.

### 15. Citizen-only state jobs (alienage)
**Facts.** State Z requires (a) state police officers and (b) state-licensed engineers to be U.S. citizens. A lawful permanent resident challenges both.

**Rule (R-CON-070).** State alienage classifications get strict scrutiny, except for positions in the political function, which get rational basis.

**Application.** Police officers exercise core sovereign power — rational basis, easily met (*Foley*). Licensed engineers perform no governmental function, so strict scrutiny applies, and no compelling need requires citizenship.

**Conclusion.** (a) valid; (b) invalid.

### 16. Women-only nursing scholarship (sex)
**Facts.** A state university offers a scholarship only to women entering nursing, stating that men "rarely choose nursing."

**Rule (R-CON-071).** Sex classifications require an exceedingly persuasive justification: an important objective and a substantial relationship, free of stereotypes.

**Application.** The stated reason is itself a stereotype; women are not underrepresented in nursing, so the program does not compensate for disadvantage (*Hogan*).

**Conclusion.** Invalid.

### 17. Group home permit (rational basis with bite)
**Facts.** A city requires a special-use permit for a home for adults with intellectual disabilities but not for fraternities or boarding houses, citing neighbors' fears.

**Rule (R-CON-074).** Disability classifications get rational basis, but a bare desire to harm or irrational fear of a group is not a legitimate interest.

**Application.** Other similar uses are not regulated, and the only reasons are neighbors' negative attitudes (*Cleburne*).

**Conclusion.** Invalid even under rational basis.

### 18. Beach easement condition (exactions)
**Facts.** A county will grant a homeowner's permit to enlarge her beach house only if she dedicates a public walkway across her lot. The county cites traffic congestion caused by larger houses.

**Rule (R-CON-080).** A permit condition requiring dedication must have an essential nexus to the government's interest and be roughly proportional to the development's impact.

**Application.** A walkway on the beach does nothing to relieve road congestion; there is no nexus, and the county made no individualized proportionality finding.

**Conclusion.** The condition is an uncompensated taking (*Nollan*; *Dolan*).

### 19. Coastal no-build rule (Lucas / Penn Central)
**Facts.** After a buyer pays $1 million for two beachfront lots zoned residential, the state bars all building to stop erosion. The lots can still be used for picnics.

**Rule (R-CON-079).** Total deprivation of economically beneficial use is a per se taking unless background nuisance or property law already prohibited the use; otherwise apply Penn Central.

**Application.** Picnicking is not economically beneficial use, so the regulation is a total wipeout. Building homes was lawful under background principles when purchased.

**Conclusion.** Per se taking; compensation owed.

### 20. City flag program (government speech vs. forum)
**Facts.** A city lets community groups fly flags on a city-hall pole on request, approving about 300 requests without review. It refuses a group's religious-heritage flag to avoid "endorsing religion."

**Rule (R-CON-096, R-CON-092).** Government speech is exempt from forum rules; but where the government does not control the messages, the program is a forum and viewpoint discrimination is barred.

**Application.** The city exercised no meaningful control and the public would not view each flag as the city's message (*Shurtleff*). Excluding a flag because it is religious is viewpoint discrimination, and allowing it would not violate the Establishment Clause.

**Conclusion.** The refusal violates the Free Speech Clause.

### 21. Teacher's public criticism (public employee speech)
**Facts.** Ana (example 13) posted on her personal blog that the principal is diverting library funds to athletics.

**Rule (R-CON-103).** A public employee's speech is protected if made as a citizen on a matter of public concern and the employee's interest outweighs the employer's interest in efficient operations; the employer may show it would have fired her anyway.

**Application.** Blogging about school spending is not part of her teaching duties (not *Garcetti*) and concerns use of public funds. There is no evidence of disruption of teaching; any workplace friction is modest (*Pickering*).

**Conclusion.** Protected; firing her for it violates the First Amendment unless the district proves it would have fired her anyway.

### 22. Gang-symbol rule (students, vagueness)
**Facts.** A school bans "any gang-related symbol, sign or tattoo," undefined, and suspends a student for a small star tattoo; no gang uses a star at the school.

**Rule (R-CON-102, R-CON-107).** Schools may restrict student expression if they reasonably forecast substantial disruption (*Tinker*); a rule that fails to give fair notice or invites arbitrary enforcement is void for vagueness.

**Application.** The star is arguably expressive (Spence), but the school shows no facts predicting disruption. "Gang-related" without definition leaves students to guess and gives officials unbridled discretion (*Morales*).

**Conclusion.** The suspension likely violates the First Amendment, and the rule is likely void for vagueness.

### 23. Candle ban after a church fire (free exercise)
**Facts.** After one church burns, a city bans lit candles "in churches, temples and mosques," while restaurants and theatres may use candles.

**Rule (R-CON-087).** A law that targets religious practice or treats comparable secular activity more favorably is not neutral or generally applicable and must survive strict scrutiny.

**Application.** The text names houses of worship only; restaurants pose the same fire risk yet are exempt (*Tandon*). Fire safety is compelling, but a ban limited to religious buildings is not narrowly tailored — fire-code rules applying to all indoor flames are a less restrictive means.

**Conclusion.** Fails strict scrutiny; a preliminary injunction is likely.

### 24. Ten Commandments in the jail (establishment)
**Facts.** A county sheriff posts the Ten Commandments in every jail cell and requires inmates to attend a weekly reading of them.

**Rule (R-CON-090).** The Establishment Clause is read by historical practice and understanding; government may not coerce religious participation.

**Application.** Compelled attendance at a religious reading is coercion of the kind the Clause historically forbade. The postings are a closer question. Postings that a captive audience cannot avoid were struck in the classroom context (*Stone v. Graham*), and the coercive jail setting strengthens the inmates' claim; but *Stone* was decided under *Lemon*, and its force after *Kennedy* is contested (the en banc Fifth Circuit has treated it as no longer good law) [VERIFY V-203].

**Conclusion.** The required attendance is invalid. The postings are probably invalid in this coercive setting, but argue both sides.

### 25. Compelled corps service (Thirteenth Amendment)
**Facts.** A state law requires every 16-year-old who leaves school to serve two years in a "Forest Corps," living in camps and working 40 hours a week, enforced by juvenile detention for refusal. Parents object.

**Rule (R-CON-113, R-CON-061).** Involuntary servitude is compelled labor through physical or legal coercion, except criminal punishment and traditional civic duties; parents have a fundamental right to direct their children's upbringing.

**Application.** The threat of detention is legal coercion; no crime has been adjudicated. The state will analogize to the draft and road-work duties (*Butler v. Perry*), but those apply broadly and are historically recognized, while this targets only dropouts and removes them from home. Removing children from parents for two years substantially burdens a fundamental right, triggering strict scrutiny, and less drastic alternatives (re-enrollment programs) exist.

**Conclusion.** Strong Thirteenth Amendment and substantive due process challenges; the law likely fails.

### 26. Nonresident lawyers (Article IV P&I)
**Facts.** State Q admits to its bar only lawyers who reside in State Q, citing the need for lawyers available for court hearings.

**Rule (R-CON-053).** Discrimination against nonresidents in pursuing a livelihood requires a substantial reason and a substantial relationship, considering less restrictive means.

**Application.** Practising law is a fundamental livelihood right (*Piper*). Availability can be ensured by requiring local associate counsel or attendance rules; nonresidents are not shown to be a peculiar source of the problem.

**Conclusion.** Invalid.
