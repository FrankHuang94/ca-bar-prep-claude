# Constitutional Law — primer

## 1. What the subject is about

The U.S. Constitution does three jobs:
1. It **creates** a national government with three branches and gives each specific powers.
2. It **divides** power between that national government and the 50 states (federalism).
3. It **protects individuals** against government, mainly through the Bill of Rights and the Fourteenth Amendment.

Constitutional Law on the bar asks one recurring question: **Is this government action valid?** You answer it in a fixed order:

```
Can a court hear it?  →  Did the government have power to act?  →  Does the action violate a limit (a right or a structural rule)?
   (justiciability)          (federal power / state power)              (scrutiny, specific tests)
```

The **courts** apply this method. Since *Marbury v. Madison* (1803), federal judges can strike down statutes that conflict with the Constitution (R-CON-001). Coming from PRC law, this is the first big difference: in the US, constitutional disputes are ordinary lawsuits decided by ordinary courts.

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: In the PRC the NPC Standing Committee interprets the Constitution and courts do not invalidate laws; in the US any federal court can hold a statute unconstitutional in a concrete case (approx.).

## 2. The map

| Part (NCBE) | Question it answers | Key tools |

## 4. How the pieces connect

**Threshold first.** A federal court decides only real "cases or controversies" (Article III). The plaintiff must have **standing**, the dispute must be **ripe** and not **moot**, and it must not be a **political question**. If a state is the defendant, the **Eleventh Amendment** blocks damages suits, so plaintiffs sue a state officer for an injunction (*Ex parte Young*). On essays, this always comes first, for every plaintiff.

**Federal statute? Find the power.** Congress has no general police power. Ask which listed power supports the law, usually the Commerce Clause (three categories, *Lopez*), the taxing power, the spending power (with the *Dole* conditions), or § 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment (*Boerne* congruence and proportionality). Then check external limits: the Tenth Amendment ban on **commandeering** states (*New York*, *Printz*), and individual rights.

**Executive action? Use Youngstown.** Did Congress authorize it, stay silent, or forbid it? The President's own powers (commander in chief, foreign affairs, pardon, appointment and removal) fill the gaps. Removal changed in 2026: the President may now remove FTC-type commissioners at will (*Trump v. Slaughter* [S-300]).

**State law affecting business across state lines? Three checks.**
1. **Preemption:** does a federal law displace it?
2. **Dormant Commerce Clause:** does it discriminate against out-of-state commerce (virtually per se invalid) or burden it excessively (*Pike* balancing)? Is the state a **market participant**?
3. **Article IV Privileges and Immunities:** does it discriminate against out-of-state **individuals** in earning a living?

**Individual rights? Use the scrutiny ladder.**
- First confirm **state action**.
- Then name the right and the classification, and choose the tier:
  - **Strict scrutiny:** race, national origin, state alienage, fundamental rights, content-based speech, laws targeting religion. Government must prove a compelling interest and narrow tailoring.
  - **Intermediate scrutiny:** sex, nonmarital birth, content-neutral speech regulation. Government must prove an important interest and a substantial relationship.
  - **Rational basis:** everything else. The challenger must show no rational relationship to any legitimate interest.
- Some rights use their own tests instead of tiers:
  - Second Amendment: text and history (*Bruen*).
  - Establishment Clause: history, tradition and coercion (*Kennedy*).
  - Takings: per se rules, then *Penn Central*.
  - Procedural due process: *Mathews* balancing.

## 5. The 8 ideas that matter most

1. **Justiciability comes first, for every plaintiff and every remedy.** Injury, causation and redressability; then ripeness and mootness and their exceptions (capable of repetition, voluntary cessation, damages claims).
2. **Know the tiers cold.** Who bears the burden, what the ends and means are, and which classes and rights trigger each tier. Most essay points come from applying the tier with facts.
3. **Discrimination is proved by purpose, not impact.** A facially neutral law gets rational basis unless the challenger proves discriminatory purpose (*Washington v. Davis*; *Arlington Heights*).
4. **Speech: content-based vs. content-neutral, then the forum.** Content-based → strict scrutiny; content-neutral in a public forum → the time, place and manner test; limited or nonpublic forum → reasonable and viewpoint-neutral. Special settings (schools, public employees, commercial speech) have their own tests.
5. **Religion has two clauses with different tests.** Free exercise: *Smith* rational basis unless the law is not neutral or not generally applicable, then strict scrutiny. Establishment: history, tradition and coercion; *Lemon* is gone.
6. **Congress needs a power; states need no power but face limits.** Federal laws fail for lack of an enumerated power or because they commandeer. State laws fail for preemption, the dormant Commerce Clause, P&I, or individual rights.
7. **The State Action requirement filters private defendants.** Only public functions, entanglement, or judicial enforcement turn private conduct into state action. The Thirteenth Amendment is the exception.
8. **Recent cases matter but do not replace the black letter.** Learn the classic tests first; then layer on the changes: *Dobbs* (2022), *Bruen* (2022), *Kennedy* (2022), *SFFA* (2023), *Loper Bright* (2024), *Slaughter* (2026). MBE items may be older; if a question clearly turns on a changed point, apply current law.

## 6. How the exam tests it

- **MBE (25 scored questions):** about half on individual rights and half on powers, federalism and justiciability [S-040]. Typical stems: "the strongest argument," "the court should hold that," "which standard applies." See [mbe-rules.md](mbe-rules.md).
- **Essays (about 4 in 10 exams):** fact patterns about a city or state rule challenged in federal court, often by several plaintiffs. Calls are usually open: "What constitutional challenges can X raise, and how should the court rule?" Use [issue-checklist.md](issue-checklist.md) and [essay-templates.md](essay-templates.md).
- **California law** almost never matters in Con Law; see [ca-distinctions.md](ca-distinctions.md).
