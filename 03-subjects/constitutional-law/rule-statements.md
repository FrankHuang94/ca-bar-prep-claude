# Constitutional Law — rule statements

**How to use.** Each block gives a 1–3 sentence rule you can write verbatim, then elements, exceptions, authority and a short example. Constitutional Law on the California essays is **federal** constitutional law, so the MBE rule and the essay rule are almost always the same. Where a California provision could matter, the `MBE vs. CA essay` line says so; the full list is in [ca-distinctions.md](ca-distinctions.md).

**Citation conventions.** Leading cases carry U.S. Reports citations only where certain. Decisions from October Terms 2022–2024 carry `[VERIFY V-035]`; October Term 2025 decisions confirmed from the opinion by search (supremecourt.gov or Cornell LII) carry only their `S-###` source; *Ellingburg v. United States*, which has no logged source yet, still carries `[VERIFY V-034]`. Other recent decisions are tagged `[VERIFY V-200]`. Plurality opinions, and judgments with no majority opinion, are labelled where cited; on an essay, cite them for the result, not as a majority holding.

## I. Judicial review and justiciability

### R-CON-001 — Judicial review and judicial supremacy
**Rule (write this):** Federal courts have the power to review the constitutionality of acts of Congress, the executive and the states, and the Supreme Court's interpretation of the Constitution binds all other courts and officials.

**Elements:** 1. A federal law or act in conflict with the Constitution is void (Marbury). 2. The Supreme Court may review state-court judgments on federal questions (Martin v. Hunter's Lessee). 3. State officials are bound by the Court's constitutional rulings (Cooper v. Aaron).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Justiciability limits (standing, ripeness, mootness, political questions) and the adequate-and-independent-state-ground rule restrict when review occurs.

**Authority:** U.S. Const. art. III, § 2; art. VI, cl. 2; *Marbury v. Madison*, 5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 137 (1803); *Martin v. Hunter's Lessee*, 14 U.S. (1 Wheat.) 304 (1816); *Cooper v. Aaron*, 358 U.S. 1 (1958).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A governor announces that a state will not follow a Supreme Court desegregation ruling because the state was not a party. Under Cooper v. Aaron the ruling binds state officials; the governor's position fails.

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: The PRC Constitution assigns interpretation to the NPC Standing Committee, and courts do not invalidate statutes; US courts do (approx.).

### R-CON-002 — Federal court structure and Supreme Court jurisdiction
**Rule (write this):** The Supreme Court has original jurisdiction over cases affecting ambassadors and cases in which a state is a party, and appellate jurisdiction over all other Article III cases "with such Exceptions, and under such Regulations as the Congress shall make." Congress cannot enlarge the Court's original jurisdiction.

**Elements:** 1. Original jurisdiction is fixed by Article III (Marbury). 2. Appellate review today is almost entirely by discretionary certiorari (28 U.S.C. §§ 1254, 1257). 3. Review of a state court requires a final judgment of the highest state court in which a decision could be had (§ 1257). 4. Lower federal courts exist only as Congress creates them.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Direct appeals survive from three-judge district courts (28 U.S.C. § 1253). Lower federal courts cannot review state-court judgments (Rooker–Feldman).

**Authority:** U.S. Const. art. III, §§ 1–2; 28 U.S.C. §§ 1251, 1253, 1254, 1257; *Marbury v. Madison*, 5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 137 (1803); *Rooker v. Fidelity Trust Co.*, 263 U.S. 413 (1923).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A statute lets a private citizen file an original action in the Supreme Court against a federal agency. The Act unconstitutionally expands original jurisdiction beyond Article III.

### R-CON-003 — Congressional power over federal jurisdiction
**Rule (write this):** Congress has broad power to create, abolish and limit the jurisdiction of the lower federal courts and to make exceptions to the Supreme Court's appellate jurisdiction.

**Elements:** 1. Inferior courts: Congress "may from time to time ordain and establish" them and define their jurisdiction (Sheldon v. Sill). 2. Supreme Court appellate jurisdiction: subject to the Exceptions Clause (Ex parte McCardle upheld withdrawal of a habeas appeal route while another route remained).

**Exceptions / defenses:** See R-CON-004 for the outer limits.

**Authority:** U.S. Const. art. III, § 1; art. III, § 2, cl. 2; *Sheldon v. Sill*, 49 U.S. (8 How.) 441 (1850); *Ex parte McCardle*, 74 U.S. (7 Wall.) 506 (1869).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** Congress removes federal district-court jurisdiction over a class of contract disputes, leaving state courts open. The statute is valid.

### R-CON-004 — Limits on jurisdiction-stripping and rule-of-decision statutes
**Rule (write this):** Congress may not use its jurisdiction power to dictate the outcome of a pending case without changing the underlying law, reopen final judgments of Article III courts, or violate independent constitutional guarantees such as due process, equal protection or the Suspension Clause.

**Elements:** 1. No rule of decision in a pending case without amending the law (United States v. Klein; narrowed by Bank Markazi). 2. No reopening of final judgments (Plaut). 3. Withdrawal of habeas requires an adequate substitute (Boumediene). 4. A statute barring all review of constitutional claims raises a serious constitutional question (Webster v. Doe).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Congress may amend the substantive law and apply it to pending cases.

**Authority:** *United States v. Klein*, 80 U.S. (13 Wall.) 128 (1872); *Plaut v. Spendthrift Farm, Inc.*, 514 U.S. 211 (1995); *Bank Markazi v. Peterson*, 578 U.S. 212 (2016); *Boumediene v. Bush*, 553 U.S. 723 (2008); *Webster v. Doe*, 486 U.S. 592 (1988).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** After a final federal judgment dismisses a securities claim as time-barred, Congress orders courts to reinstate such dismissed cases. Under Plaut the statute violates separation of powers.

### R-CON-005 — Eleventh Amendment and state sovereign immunity — scope
**Rule (write this):** A nonconsenting state cannot be sued for damages or other retrospective relief by a private party in federal court, whether the plaintiff is a citizen of another state or of the defendant state itself, and the same immunity applies in the state's own courts and in federal agency adjudications.

**Elements:** 1. Defendant is the state or an "arm of the state" (state agencies, state officials sued in official capacity for money from the treasury). 2. Plaintiff is a private party or a foreign nation. 3. Relief is retrospective (damages, restitution from the treasury).

**Exceptions / defenses:** See R-CON-006. Immunity does not protect cities, counties or school districts. The United States and other states may sue a state.

**Authority:** U.S. Const. amend. XI; *Hans v. Louisiana*, 134 U.S. 1 (1890); *Alden v. Maine*, 527 U.S. 706 (1999); *Federal Maritime Comm'n v. South Carolina State Ports Auth.*, 535 U.S. 743 (2002); *Edelman v. Jordan*, 415 U.S. 651 (1974).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A state employee sues the state university in federal court for back pay under a federal statute enacted under the Commerce Clause. The university is an arm of the state and the suit is barred.

### R-CON-006 — Sovereign immunity — exceptions
**Rule (write this):** State sovereign immunity does not bar (1) suits the state clearly consents to, (2) suits under statutes validly abrogating immunity under § 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment, (3) suits for prospective injunctive or declaratory relief against state officers for ongoing violations of federal law, (4) damages suits against officers in their personal capacity, or (5) suits against local governments.

**Elements:** 1. Waiver must be unequivocally expressed. 2. Abrogation requires unmistakably clear statutory language plus a valid § 5 basis (Fitzpatrick); Article I powers cannot abrogate (Seminole Tribe); but the states consented to suit "in the plan of the Convention" under the Bankruptcy Clause (Katz), the war powers (Torres v. Texas Dep't of Public Safety (2022) [VERIFY V-200]) and federally authorized eminent domain (PennEast). 3. Ex parte Young: name the officer, seek prospective relief, allege federal-law violation.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Ex parte Young does not permit federal injunctions against state officers based on state law (Pennhurst II). It does not reach state-court judges and clerks (Whole Woman's Health v. Jackson).

**Authority:** *Ex parte Young*, 209 U.S. 123 (1908); *Fitzpatrick v. Bitzer*, 427 U.S. 445 (1976); *Seminole Tribe of Fla. v. Florida*, 517 U.S. 44 (1996); *Central Va. Community College v. Katz*, 546 U.S. 356 (2006); *Pennhurst State School & Hospital v. Halderman*, 465 U.S. 89 (1984); *Hafer v. Melo*, 502 U.S. 21 (1991); *Whole Woman's Health v. Jackson*, 595 U.S. 30 (2021).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A church sues the state's health director (not the state) in federal court to enjoin enforcement of a state rule alleged to violate the Free Exercise Clause. Ex parte Young permits the suit.

### R-CON-007 — Case or controversy; no advisory opinions
**Rule (write this):** Article III limits federal courts to actual cases or controversies between adverse parties; federal courts may not issue advisory opinions, though they may grant declaratory judgments in a real, concrete dispute.

**Elements:** 1. Adverse parties. 2. A concrete dispute capable of resolution by a binding judgment. 3. Justiciability doctrines (standing, ripeness, mootness, political question) satisfied.

**Exceptions / defenses:** State courts are not bound by Article III and may issue advisory opinions if their state law allows.

**Authority:** U.S. Const. art. III, § 2; *Muskrat v. United States*, 219 U.S. 346 (1911); *Aetna Life Ins. Co. v. Haworth*, 300 U.S. 227 (1937); *MedImmune, Inc. v. Genentech, Inc.*, 549 U.S. 118 (2007); 28 U.S.C. § 2201.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule for federal court. CA note: California courts are not Article III courts (see ca-distinctions).

**Example:** The President asks the Supreme Court whether a proposed treaty would be constitutional. No case or controversy exists; the Court must decline.

### R-CON-008 — Standing: the three elements
**Rule (write this):** To have standing, a plaintiff must show (1) an injury in fact that is concrete, particularized and actual or imminent, (2) that is fairly traceable to the defendant's challenged conduct, and (3) that is likely to be redressed by a favorable decision. Standing is assessed claim by claim and for each form of relief sought.

**Elements:** 1. Injury in fact (R-CON-009). 2. Causation. 3. Redressability (R-CON-010).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Prudential limits: no third-party standing (R-CON-011), no generalized grievances (R-CON-013).

**Authority:** *Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*, 504 U.S. 555 (1992); *TransUnion LLC v. Ramirez*, 594 U.S. 413 (2021); *Murthy v. Missouri* (2024) [VERIFY V-035].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule; on essays, discuss standing first for every plaintiff.

**Example:** A produce grower excluded by a state purchasing preference loses sales (injury), caused by the preference (causation), and an injunction would let it compete (redressability). It has standing.

### R-CON-009 — Injury in fact
**Rule (write this):** An injury in fact is an invasion of a legally protected interest that is concrete (real, with a close relationship to a harm traditionally recognized by courts) and particularized (affecting the plaintiff in a personal and individual way), and actual or imminent rather than conjectural. A plaintiff seeking prospective relief must show a real and immediate threat of future injury, and a statute's grant of a cause of action does not by itself create a concrete injury.

**Elements:** 1. Economic, physical, aesthetic, environmental or constitutional (e.g., chilled speech) harm. 2. Future harm must be "certainly impending" or a "substantial risk" (Clapper; SBA List). 3. Bare procedural violations without concrete harm do not suffice (Spokeo; TransUnion).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Self-inflicted costs incurred to avoid speculative harm do not create standing (Clapper). Organizations cannot spend their way into standing by diverting resources to oppose a policy (FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine [VERIFY V-035]). A candidate has a personal stake in the rules for counting votes in his own election (Bost v. Illinois State Bd. of Elections (2026) [S-314]).

**Authority:** *Lujan*, 504 U.S. 555; *Clapper v. Amnesty Int'l USA*, 568 U.S. 398 (2013); *Spokeo, Inc. v. Robins*, 578 U.S. 330 (2016); *TransUnion*, 594 U.S. 413; *Summers v. Earth Island Inst.*, 555 U.S. 488 (2009).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** Lawyers fear their calls with foreign clients may be intercepted under a surveillance statute and buy encrypted phones. Under Clapper the feared interception is speculative and the cost is self-inflicted; no standing.

### R-CON-010 — Causation and redressability
**Rule (write this):** The injury must be fairly traceable to the defendant's conduct rather than to the independent action of a third party not before the court, and it must be likely, not merely speculative, that the requested relief will remedy it. Nominal damages can redress a completed past injury.

**Elements:** 1. Causal chain not broken by independent choices of third parties (Allen v. Wright; Murthy). 2. Relief against the named defendant would actually help.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Partial redress suffices (Massachusetts v. EPA). Nominal damages satisfy redressability for a completed violation (Uzuegbunam).

**Authority:** *Allen v. Wright*, 468 U.S. 737 (1984); *Massachusetts v. EPA*, 549 U.S. 497 (2007); *Uzuegbunam v. Preczewski*, 592 U.S. 279 (2021); *Murthy v. Missouri* (2024) [VERIFY V-035].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** Users whose posts were removed by platforms sue federal officials. Absent proof that the platforms' future moderation will be traceable to the officials rather than the platforms' own policies, an injunction against the officials is unlikely to redress the harm (Murthy).

### R-CON-011 — Third-party standing
**Rule (write this):** A plaintiff generally must assert its own rights, but may assert a third party's rights when the plaintiff suffers its own injury, has a close relationship with the third party, and the third party faces a genuine obstacle to suing.

**Elements:** 1. Plaintiff's own injury in fact. 2. Close relationship (doctor–patient, vendor–customer, lawyer–client in some settings, litigant–juror). 3. Hindrance to the third party's own suit.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Overbreadth challenges in free-speech cases are a recognized exception (R-CON-108). Kowalski rejected standing for lawyers asserting rights of hypothetical future clients.

**Authority:** *Craig v. Boren*, 429 U.S. 190 (1976); *Singleton v. Wulff*, 428 U.S. 106 (1976) (third-party-standing analysis, Part II-B, plurality); *Powers v. Ohio*, 499 U.S. 400 (1991); *Kowalski v. Tesmer*, 543 U.S. 125 (2004).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A beer vendor challenges a law barring sales of low-alcohol beer to men under 21 but to women only under 18. The vendor loses sales and may assert the young male customers' equal-protection rights (Craig).

### R-CON-012 — Associational (organizational) standing
**Rule (write this):** An organization has standing to sue on behalf of its members when (1) its members would otherwise have standing in their own right, (2) the interests it seeks to protect are germane to its purpose, and (3) neither the claim asserted nor the relief requested requires the participation of individual members.

**Elements:** Hunt's three prongs; the organization should identify at least one injured member (Summers).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Individual damages claims usually require member participation, defeating prong 3. An organization may also sue for its own injury, but diverting resources to oppose a policy is not enough (FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine [VERIFY V-035]).

**Authority:** *Hunt v. Washington State Apple Advertising Comm'n*, 432 U.S. 333 (1977); *Summers v. Earth Island Inst.*, 555 U.S. 488 (2009).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A growers' association seeks an injunction against a state labeling law that harms its members' sales. All three Hunt prongs are met.

### R-CON-013 — Taxpayer and citizen standing; generalized grievances
**Rule (write this):** A plaintiff cannot sue merely as a citizen or federal taxpayer to challenge how the government spends money or administers the law, because that is a generalized grievance shared by all. The sole exception is a federal taxpayer's Establishment Clause challenge to a specific congressional appropriation under the taxing and spending power.

**Elements (Flast exception):** 1. Taxpayer status. 2. Challenge to a congressional exercise of the taxing and spending power. 3. Claim that the spending violates the Establishment Clause.

**Exceptions / defenses:** No standing to challenge executive discretionary spending (Hein, plurality opinion; two more Justices concurred in the judgment), tax credits (Winn) or property transfers under the Property Clause (Valley Forge). State taxpayers lack standing in federal court to challenge state tax or spending decisions (DaimlerChrysler). Municipal taxpayers have traditionally been allowed to challenge municipal spending (dictum in Frothingham v. Mellon).

**Authority:** *Flast v. Cohen*, 392 U.S. 83 (1968); *Valley Forge Christian College v. Americans United for Separation of Church & State, Inc.*, 454 U.S. 464 (1982); *Hein v. Freedom From Religion Found.*, 551 U.S. 587 (2007) (plurality opinion); *Arizona Christian School Tuition Org. v. Winn*, 563 U.S. 125 (2011); *DaimlerChrysler Corp. v. Cuno*, 547 U.S. 332 (2006); *Lance v. Coffman*, 549 U.S. 437 (2007); *Massachusetts v. Mellon*, 262 U.S. 447 (1923).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule for federal court. CA note: in state court, a resident or corporation that pays (or is liable to pay) a tax funding a local agency may sue to restrain that agency's illegal expenditure or waste (Code Civ. Proc. § 526a) [S-1462] [S-1463]; see ca-distinctions.

**Example:** A federal taxpayer sues to stop a congressional grant program funding religious schools' religious instruction. Flast standing exists. The same taxpayer challenging a White House faith office funded from general appropriations lacks standing (Hein).

### R-CON-014 — Standing of states, legislators and intervenors
**Rule (write this):** A state may sue to protect its own sovereign, quasi-sovereign and proprietary interests, but not as parens patriae against the federal government; individual legislators lack standing to assert institutional injuries; and a person who intervenes or appeals to seek relief different from or beyond the original party's must independently satisfy Article III.

**Elements:** 1. State injury to its own treasury, property or sovereign interests (Massachusetts v. EPA "special solicitude"; Biden v. Nebraska [VERIFY V-200]). 2. Legislators: personal injury required (Raines); a whole legislature may sue for institutional injury (Arizona State Legislature). 3. Intervenors/appellants: own standing (Town of Chester; Hollingsworth; Diamond v. Charles).

**Exceptions / defenses:** States lack standing to challenge federal immigration-enforcement priorities based on indirect costs (United States v. Texas (2023) [VERIFY V-200]). Official initiative proponents lack standing to defend a law the state declines to defend (Hollingsworth — California's Proposition 8).

**Authority:** *Massachusetts v. Mellon*, 262 U.S. 447 (1923); *Massachusetts v. EPA*, 549 U.S. 497 (2007); *Raines v. Byrd*, 521 U.S. 811 (1997); *Arizona State Legislature v. Arizona Independent Redistricting Comm'n*, 576 U.S. 787 (2015); *Town of Chester v. Laroe Estates, Inc.*, 581 U.S. 433 (2017); *Hollingsworth v. Perry*, 570 U.S. 693 (2013); *Diamond v. Charles*, 476 U.S. 54 (1986).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A state challenges conditions on its own federal education grants; it has standing to protect its treasury. A national advocacy group seeking to intervene to defend the law must show its own concrete injury; ideological interest alone is not enough.

### R-CON-015 — Ripeness
**Rule (write this):** A claim is ripe when it presents a concrete dispute rather than an abstract or hypothetical one; courts weigh (1) the fitness of the issues for judicial decision and (2) the hardship to the parties of withholding review. A plaintiff may bring a pre-enforcement challenge when it intends to engage in conduct arguably protected by the Constitution, the conduct is arguably proscribed, and there is a credible threat of enforcement.

**Elements:** Abbott Labs factors; SBA List pre-enforcement test. A person need not violate the law and risk prosecution first (Steffel).

**Exceptions / defenses:** No ripeness if there is no realistic threat of enforcement (Poe v. Ullman, plurality opinion) or the facts are too contingent (United Public Workers v. Mitchell). A state investigative subpoena demanding donor information inflicts a present First Amendment injury even before a court orders compliance (First Choice Women's Resource Centers v. Davenport (2026) [S-313]).

**Authority:** *Abbott Labs. v. Gardner*, 387 U.S. 136 (1967); *Steffel v. Thompson*, 415 U.S. 452 (1974); *Susan B. Anthony List v. Driehaus*, 573 U.S. 149 (2014); *Poe v. Ullman*, 367 U.S. 497 (1961) (plurality opinion); *Babbitt v. United Farm Workers*, 442 U.S. 289 (1979).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A church that burns candles in services sues the day a city ordinance banning candles in churches takes effect, and the fire marshal announces inspections. The church intends to continue, the conduct is proscribed and enforcement is credible; the claim is ripe.

### R-CON-016 — Mootness and its exceptions
**Rule (write this):** A case is moot when events after filing eliminate the live controversy, so a court can no longer grant effective relief; mootness is often described as standing set in a time frame. Exceptions: (1) wrongs capable of repetition yet evading review, (2) voluntary cessation by the defendant, (3) surviving collateral consequences or damages claims, and (4) class actions where the named plaintiff's claim expires.

**Elements:** 1. Capable of repetition: challenged action too short to be fully litigated and a reasonable expectation the same complaining party will face it again (Weinstein). 2. Voluntary cessation: defendant must make it absolutely clear the conduct could not reasonably be expected to recur (Laidlaw). 3. A live claim for damages, including nominal damages, keeps the case alive (Uzuegbunam). 4. Certified class: claims of other members remain live (Sosna; Geraghty).

**Exceptions / defenses:** A student's graduation moots his own claims for injunctive and declaratory relief against school rules (DeFunis), but not a damages claim or a request to expunge discipline records.

**Authority:** *DeFunis v. Odegaard*, 416 U.S. 312 (1974); *Southern Pacific Terminal Co. v. ICC*, 219 U.S. 498 (1911); *Weinstein v. Bradford*, 423 U.S. 147 (1975); *Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Envtl. Servs.*, 528 U.S. 167 (2000); *Already, LLC v. Nike, Inc.*, 568 U.S. 85 (2013); *Sosna v. Iowa*, 419 U.S. 393 (1975); *U.S. Parole Comm'n v. Geraghty*, 445 U.S. 388 (1980); *Uzuegbunam v. Preczewski*, 592 U.S. 279 (2021).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A suspended student sues over a school symbol ban, graduates, and the district moves to dismiss as moot. Injunctive relief is moot as to him, but his damages claim (even nominal) and any request to clear his record keep the case alive; if the district merely paused the rule during litigation, voluntary cessation also defeats mootness.

### R-CON-017 — Political question doctrine
**Rule (write this):** Federal courts will not decide political questions — issues the Constitution commits to another branch or that lack judicially discoverable and manageable standards for resolving them.

**Elements (Baker factors, first two most important):** 1. Textually demonstrable constitutional commitment to a coordinate branch. 2. Lack of judicially manageable standards. 3–6. Need for a nonjudicial policy determination; lack of respect for coordinate branches; need for adherence to a political decision already made; potential embarrassment from multifarious pronouncements.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Not political questions: legislative apportionment and one-person-one-vote (Baker; Reynolds), exclusion of an elected member (Powell), statutory passport-recognition claims (Zivotofsky v. Clinton). Political questions: Guarantee Clause claims (Luther), Senate impeachment-trial procedures (Nixon v. United States), partisan gerrymandering (Rucho), and (plurality) treaty termination (Goldwater).

**Authority:** *Baker v. Carr*, 369 U.S. 186 (1962); *Nixon v. United States*, 506 U.S. 224 (1993); *Rucho v. Common Cause*, 588 U.S. 684 (2019); *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486 (1969); *Zivotofsky v. Clinton*, 566 U.S. 189 (2012); *Luther v. Borden*, 48 U.S. (7 How.) 1 (1849).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A federal judge removed after a Senate committee heard the evidence claims the full Senate must "try" him. The Senate has "sole Power to try" impeachments; the claim is nonjusticiable.

### R-CON-018 — Adequate and independent state ground
**Rule (write this):** The Supreme Court will not review a state-court judgment that rests on a state-law ground that is both adequate to support the judgment and independent of federal law. If the state decision fairly appears to rest primarily on federal law or is interwoven with it, and the state court has not clearly stated that it rests on state grounds, the Court presumes there is no independent state ground and may review.

**Elements:** 1. Adequate: the state ground alone supports the result. 2. Independent: it does not depend on federal law. 3. Plain-statement rule (Michigan v. Long).

**Exceptions / defenses:** A state procedural rule that is not firmly established and regularly followed is not adequate.

**Authority:** *Michigan v. Long*, 463 U.S. 1032 (1983); *Fox Film Corp. v. Muller*, 296 U.S. 207 (1935); 28 U.S.C. § 1257.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. CA note: California courts may rest decisions on the California Constitution, whose rights "are not dependent on" federal rights (Cal. Const. art. I, § 24).

**Example:** A state supreme court suppresses evidence "under both the Fourth Amendment and Article I of our state constitution, each independently." The Supreme Court cannot review; the state ground is adequate and independent.

### R-CON-019 — Abstention
**Rule (write this):** Federal courts may abstain (1) under Pullman, when an unclear state-law question could avoid a federal constitutional ruling, and (2) under Younger, when a federal plaintiff asks the court to enjoin a pending state criminal prosecution or certain quasi-criminal or civil enforcement proceedings that provide an adequate forum for the federal claim.

**Elements:** Younger: 1. Ongoing state judicial proceeding of the covered type. 2. Important state interest. 3. Adequate opportunity to raise federal claims.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Younger does not apply when no state proceeding is pending (Steffel permits declaratory relief) or in cases of bad faith, harassment or a flagrantly unconstitutional statute. The Anti-Injunction Act separately limits federal injunctions against state-court proceedings.

**Authority:** *Railroad Comm'n of Tex. v. Pullman Co.*, 312 U.S. 496 (1941); *Younger v. Harris*, 401 U.S. 37 (1971); *Steffel v. Thompson*, 415 U.S. 452 (1974); 28 U.S.C. § 2283.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A bookseller already being prosecuted in state court for selling obscene material asks a federal court to enjoin the prosecution. The federal court abstains under Younger.

## II. Separation of powers

### R-CON-020 — Enumerated powers and the Necessary and Proper Clause
**Rule (write this):** Congress may act only under a power enumerated in the Constitution, but the Necessary and Proper Clause lets it use any means rationally related to executing an enumerated power. There is no general federal police power.

**Elements:** 1. Identify the enumerated power. 2. The means must be "plainly adapted" (rationally related) to that end and not prohibited (McCulloch; Comstock). 3. The Clause is not an independent source of power.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Congress has police-type power over the District of Columbia, federal enclaves and territories, federal property, and the military. The Clause cannot justify a law that is not "proper" because it undermines state sovereignty or the structure of the Constitution (NFIB: Roberts, C.J., writing for himself on this point; the four joint dissenters reached the same view, so there is no majority opinion).

**Authority:** U.S. Const. art. I, § 8, cl. 18; *McCulloch v. Maryland*, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 316 (1819); *United States v. Comstock*, 560 U.S. 126 (2010); *NFIB v. Sebelius*, 567 U.S. 519 (2012).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** Congress creates a national bank to help collect taxes and borrow money. It is a rational means to execute the taxing and borrowing powers and is valid (McCulloch).

### R-CON-021 — Commerce power: the three categories
**Rule (write this):** Under the Commerce Clause, Congress may regulate (1) the channels of interstate commerce, (2) the instrumentalities of interstate commerce and persons or things in interstate commerce, even if the threat comes only from intrastate activity, and (3) activities that substantially affect interstate commerce.

**Elements:** Category 1: highways, waterways, airways, the internet; Congress may bar goods or people from moving interstate. Category 2: vehicles, aircraft, trains, goods in transit. Category 3: see R-CON-022.

**Exceptions / defenses:** External constitutional limits (Tenth Amendment anti-commandeering, Bill of Rights) still apply.

**Authority:** U.S. Const. art. I, § 8, cl. 3; *Gibbons v. Ogden*, 22 U.S. (9 Wheat.) 1 (1824); *United States v. Lopez*, 514 U.S. 549 (1995); *Heart of Atlanta Motel, Inc. v. United States*, 379 U.S. 241 (1964).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** Congress bans motels serving interstate travelers from refusing guests based on race. Valid under the commerce power (Heart of Atlanta).

### R-CON-022 — Commerce power: substantial effects, aggregation and limits
**Rule (write this):** Congress may regulate intrastate economic activity that, in the aggregate, substantially affects interstate commerce, and a court asks only whether Congress had a rational basis for so concluding. Congress may not regulate noneconomic intrastate activity based on attenuated effects, and it may not compel individuals to engage in commerce.

**Elements:** 1. Economic or commercial activity? If yes, aggregate (Wickard; Raich). 2. If noneconomic, the link must not be attenuated; a jurisdictional element and findings help but are not decisive (Lopez; Morrison). 3. Regulating inactivity (forcing a purchase) is beyond the Commerce Clause (NFIB: Roberts, C.J., for himself; the four joint dissenters agreed, so five Justices took this view without a majority opinion).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Part of a comprehensive regulatory scheme of economic activity: Congress may reach purely local noncommercial activity if necessary to make the scheme effective (Raich).

**Authority:** *Wickard v. Filburn*, 317 U.S. 111 (1942); *United States v. Lopez*, 514 U.S. 549 (1995); *United States v. Morrison*, 529 U.S. 598 (2000); *Gonzales v. Raich*, 545 U.S. 1 (2005); *NFIB v. Sebelius*, 567 U.S. 519 (2012) (Commerce Clause: Roberts, C.J., alone; joint dissent agreeing).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A federal statute criminalizes possessing a firearm within 1,000 feet of a school, with no jurisdictional element. Possession is noneconomic and its effect on commerce attenuated; the law exceeds the commerce power (Lopez).

### R-CON-023 — Taxing power
**Rule (write this):** Congress may lay and collect taxes to provide for the general welfare; a measure is upheld as a tax if it is reasonably related to raising revenue, judged functionally rather than by its label. A tax is valid even if it also has a regulatory purpose.

**Elements:** 1. Produces some revenue. 2. Not so punitive that it is really a penalty (amount, scienter, enforcement by ordinary tax agency — NFIB). 3. Indirect taxes uniform throughout the United States; direct taxes apportioned (income tax excepted by the Sixteenth Amendment). 4. No tax on exports from any state.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Revenue bills must originate in the House (art. I, § 7, cl. 1).

**Authority:** U.S. Const. art. I, § 8, cl. 1; art. I, § 9, cls. 4–5; amend. XVI; *NFIB v. Sebelius*, 567 U.S. 519 (2012) (taxing-power holding: opinion of the Court); *Bailey v. Drexel Furniture Co.*, 259 U.S. 20 (1922).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A federal law requires people without health insurance to pay a modest annual amount to the IRS, with no criminal sanction. It operates as a tax and is valid (NFIB).

### R-CON-024 — Spending power and conditional grants
**Rule (write this):** Congress may spend for the general welfare and may attach conditions to federal funds given to states if the conditions (1) serve the general welfare, (2) are stated unambiguously, (3) relate to the federal interest in the program, (4) do not require the state to violate the Constitution, and (5) are not so coercive that pressure turns into compulsion.

**Elements:** Dole's four requirements plus NFIB's coercion limit. Clear notice is judged from the state's perspective at the time it accepts the funds (Pennhurst I; Arlington Central).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Withholding a small percentage of existing funds is mild encouragement (Dole: 5% of highway funds). Threatening all existing Medicaid funding to force acceptance of a new program is coercive (NFIB: Roberts, C.J., for three Justices; the four joint dissenters also found coercion, so seven Justices agreed without a majority opinion). Spending is not limited to enumerated subjects (Butler).

**Authority:** U.S. Const. art. I, § 8, cl. 1; *South Dakota v. Dole*, 483 U.S. 203 (1987); *NFIB v. Sebelius*, 567 U.S. 519 (2012) (Medicaid coercion: Roberts, C.J., for three Justices); *United States v. Butler*, 297 U.S. 1 (1936); *Pennhurst State School & Hospital v. Halderman*, 451 U.S. 1 (1981).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** Congress offers states new physical-education grants if they require daily PE, but also threatens to cut all existing federal school funding for states that refuse. The new-grant condition is valid; the threat to existing funds risks coercion under NFIB.

### R-CON-025 — War and defense powers
**Rule (write this):** Congress has power to declare war, raise and support armies, provide and maintain a navy, regulate the armed forces and the militia, and to enact measures reasonably related to war and its aftermath, including economic regulation. Military courts may try service members, but civilians may not be tried by military tribunals when civilian courts are open.

**Elements:** 1. Textual source (art. I, § 8, cls. 11–16). 2. Rational relation to national defense; heavy deference.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Civilian dependents of service members may not be tried by court-martial abroad in peacetime (Reid v. Covert (plurality; the concurring Justices limited the result to capital cases); extended to noncapital cases by Kinsella v. Singleton, 361 U.S. 234 (1960)). The Bill of Rights still applies.

**Authority:** U.S. Const. art. I, § 8, cls. 11–16; *Woods v. Cloyd W. Miller Co.*, 333 U.S. 138 (1948); *Ex parte Milligan*, 71 U.S. (4 Wall.) 2 (1866); *Reid v. Covert*, 354 U.S. 1 (1957) (plurality); *Solorio v. United States*, 483 U.S. 435 (1987); *Selective Draft Law Cases*, 245 U.S. 366 (1918).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** Two years after a war ends, Congress extends rent controls because of a wartime housing shortage. Valid under the war power (Woods).

### R-CON-026 — Foreign affairs, treaty-implementing, immigration and naturalization powers of Congress
**Rule (write this):** Congress may regulate foreign commerce, define offenses against the law of nations, establish a uniform rule of naturalization, and exercise plenary power over immigration; it may also enact laws necessary and proper to implement a valid treaty. Neither a treaty nor a statute may violate the Constitution's individual-rights guarantees.

**Elements:** 1. Implementing legislation valid under the Necessary and Proper Clause even if no other Article I power supports it (Missouri v. Holland). 2. Courts read implementing statutes narrowly to avoid reaching purely local conduct (Bond). 3. Citizenship cannot be taken away without the citizen's voluntary relinquishment (Afroyim).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Federal alienage classifications get only rational-basis review because of the immigration power (Mathews v. Diaz).

**Authority:** U.S. Const. art. I, § 8, cls. 3, 4, 10, 18; *Missouri v. Holland*, 252 U.S. 416 (1920); *Bond v. United States*, 572 U.S. 844 (2014); *Reid v. Covert*, 354 U.S. 1 (1957) (plurality); *Afroyim v. Rusk*, 387 U.S. 253 (1967); *Arizona v. United States*, 567 U.S. 387 (2012).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A treaty protects migratory birds and Congress implements it with hunting limits that would otherwise exceed its powers. The statute is valid as treaty implementation (Missouri v. Holland).

### R-CON-027 — Section 5 enforcement power: congruence and proportionality
**Rule (write this):** Under § 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment Congress may enact appropriate legislation to remedy or prevent violations of rights the Court has recognized, but it may not redefine the substance of those rights, and the legislation must show congruence and proportionality between the injury to be prevented and the means adopted. Section 5 reaches only state action, not private conduct.

**Elements:** 1. Identify the constitutional right as the Court defines it. 2. Evidence (legislative record) of a pattern of state violations. 3. Remedy congruent and proportional.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Valid: FMLA family-leave provision (Hibbs, sex discrimination), ADA Title II as to access to courts (Lane). Invalid: RFRA as applied to states (Boerne), ADEA damages against states (Kimel), ADA Title I damages against states (Garrett), VAWA civil remedy against private actors (Morrison).

**Authority:** U.S. Const. amend. XIV, § 5; *City of Boerne v. Flores*, 521 U.S. 507 (1997); *United States v. Morrison*, 529 U.S. 598 (2000); *Kimel v. Florida Bd. of Regents*, 528 U.S. 62 (2000); *Board of Trustees of Univ. of Ala. v. Garrett*, 531 U.S. 356 (2001); *Nevada Dep't of Human Resources v. Hibbs*, 538 U.S. 721 (2003); *Tennessee v. Lane*, 541 U.S. 509 (2004); *Civil Rights Cases*, 109 U.S. 3 (1883).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** Congress, relying on § 5, lets state employees sue states for damages for age discrimination. Age gets only rational-basis review and the record showed little unconstitutional state age discrimination; the abrogation fails (Kimel).

### R-CON-028 — Thirteenth Amendment enforcement power
**Rule (write this):** Section 2 of the Thirteenth Amendment authorizes Congress to determine rationally what the badges and incidents of slavery are and to legislate against them, and because the Amendment has no state-action requirement, Congress may reach purely private conduct such as private racial discrimination.

**Elements:** 1. Rational determination that the conduct is a badge or incident of slavery. 2. Statute may bind private parties.

**Exceptions / defenses:** The courts, not Congress, define what § 1 itself forbids (involuntary servitude, R-CON-113).

**Authority:** U.S. Const. amend. XIII, § 2; *Jones v. Alfred H. Mayer Co.*, 392 U.S. 409 (1968); 42 U.S.C. § 1982.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A private developer refuses to sell a home to a Black buyer. Section 1982, enacted under the Thirteenth Amendment, reaches the private refusal (Jones).

### R-CON-029 — Fifteenth Amendment enforcement power and voting legislation
**Rule (write this):** Section 2 of the Fifteenth Amendment lets Congress enforce the ban on race-based denial or abridgment of the vote by appropriate legislation, including prophylactic measures, but measures that burden some states more than others must be justified by current conditions.

**Elements:** 1. Rational, remedial voting legislation (South Carolina v. Katzenbach). 2. Disparate treatment of states requires current needs (Shelby County: the preclearance coverage formula is unconstitutional).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Vote-dilution claims under VRA § 2 are statutory; Louisiana v. Callais (2026) now requires a strong inference of intentional discrimination [S-309].

**Authority:** U.S. Const. amend. XV, § 2; *South Carolina v. Katzenbach*, 383 U.S. 301 (1966); *Shelby County v. Holder*, 570 U.S. 529 (2013); 52 U.S.C. § 10301.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** Congress suspends literacy tests nationwide because of their history of racially discriminatory use. Valid enforcement legislation.

### R-CON-030 — Other congressional powers: property, investigation, bankruptcy, D.C.
**Rule (write this):** Congress has plenary power to dispose of and regulate federal property, may investigate any matter on which it may legislate, may enact uniform bankruptcy laws, and has general legislative power over the District of Columbia and federal enclaves.

**Elements:** 1. Property Clause: plenary over federal lands, including wildlife on them (Kleppe). 2. Investigation: valid legislative purpose; pertinent questions; witnesses keep constitutional rights such as the Fifth Amendment privilege (McGrain; Watkins). 3. Subpoenas for a President's personal records require a balancing test (Mazars).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Congress may not expose for exposure's sake (Watkins).

**Authority:** U.S. Const. art. IV, § 3, cl. 2; art. I, § 8, cls. 4, 17; *Kleppe v. New Mexico*, 426 U.S. 529 (1976); *McGrain v. Daugherty*, 273 U.S. 135 (1927); *Watkins v. United States*, 354 U.S. 178 (1957); *Trump v. Mazars USA, LLP*, 591 U.S. 848 (2020).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** Congress protects wild burros on federal lands from capture under state law. Valid under the Property Clause, and the federal law preempts the state law (Kleppe).

### R-CON-031 — Presidential power and the Youngstown framework
**Rule (write this):** The President's power must come from the Constitution or an act of Congress. When the President acts with express or implied congressional authorization, power is at its maximum; when Congress is silent, the President acts in a "zone of twilight"; and when the President acts against Congress's express or implied will, power is at its lowest ebb and is valid only if the Constitution gives the President exclusive power over the matter.

**Elements:** Jackson's three categories; identify which category, then whether an exclusive Article II power applies.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Exclusive powers prevail even in category three, e.g., recognition of foreign sovereigns (Zivotofsky v. Kerry), the pardon power and removal of principal executive officers.

**Authority:** U.S. Const. art. II; *Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer*, 343 U.S. 579 (1952) (Jackson, J., concurring); *Dames & Moore v. Regan*, 453 U.S. 654 (1981); *Zivotofsky v. Kerry*, 576 U.S. 1 (2015).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** During a labor dispute in wartime, the President seizes steel mills after Congress had rejected seizure authority. Category three; no exclusive power supports domestic seizure; invalid (Youngstown).

### R-CON-032 — Chief executive and Take Care: enforcement, spending and statutory authority
**Rule (write this):** The President must take care that the laws be faithfully executed, which includes enforcement discretion and supervision of executive officers, but the President may not make law, refuse to spend funds that a statute requires to be spent, or claim sweeping power from a statute unless Congress has clearly authorized it.

**Elements:** 1. Executive orders valid only if grounded in the Constitution or a statute. 2. Impoundment of mandated appropriations not allowed (Train, decided on statutory grounds). 3. Major questions: for decisions of vast economic and political significance, an agency or the President needs clear congressional authorization (West Virginia v. EPA; Biden v. Nebraska [VERIFY V-200]). 4. Courts decide statutory meaning independently; no Chevron deference (Loper Bright [VERIFY V-035]).

**Exceptions / defenses:** IEEPA's power to "regulate ... importation" does not authorize tariffs (Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump (2026) [S-302] [S-303]).

**Authority:** U.S. Const. art. II, §§ 1, 3; *Train v. City of New York*, 420 U.S. 35 (1975); *West Virginia v. EPA*, 597 U.S. 697 (2022); *Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo* (2024) [VERIFY V-035].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** The President, citing an emergency statute that lets him "regulate" imports, imposes broad tariffs. The statute does not clearly confer the taxing-type power to impose tariffs; under Learning Resources the tariffs are unauthorized.

### R-CON-033 — Commander in chief and war powers
**Rule (write this):** The President is commander in chief of the armed forces and may deploy troops and repel sudden attacks without a declaration of war, but only Congress may declare war, and wartime detention of citizens must satisfy due process.

**Elements:** 1. Defensive use of force without declaration (Prize Cases). 2. War Powers Resolution: report to Congress within 48 hours; withdraw within 60 days (plus 30) absent authorization. 3. A citizen detained as an enemy combatant gets notice and a meaningful opportunity to contest before a neutral decisionmaker (Hamdi, plurality opinion; five Justices agreed the detention itself was authorized). 4. Habeas extends to Guantánamo detainees (Boumediene); military commissions must comply with statutes (Hamdan).

**Exceptions / defenses:** The commander-in-chief power does not authorize domestic seizures of private property (Youngstown).

**Authority:** U.S. Const. art. II, § 2, cl. 1; art. I, § 8, cl. 11; *The Prize Cases*, 67 U.S. (2 Black) 635 (1863); *Hamdi v. Rumsfeld*, 542 U.S. 507 (2004); *Hamdan v. Rumsfeld*, 548 U.S. 557 (2006); *Boumediene v. Bush*, 553 U.S. 723 (2008); 50 U.S.C. §§ 1541–1548.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A U.S. citizen captured abroad is held indefinitely without any hearing. Due process requires notice and a chance to rebut the factual basis before a neutral decisionmaker (Hamdi).

### R-CON-034 — Treaties, executive agreements and foreign affairs
**Rule (write this):** The President makes treaties with the advice and consent of two-thirds of the Senators present; a treaty has the same rank as a federal statute (the later in time controls) and cannot override the Constitution. The President may make executive agreements without Senate consent, and they preempt inconsistent state law, but a non-self-executing treaty is not domestic law until Congress implements it.

**Elements:** 1. Self-executing vs. non-self-executing (Medellín). 2. Executive agreements and foreign-policy preemption of state law (Belmont; Pink; Garamendi). 3. President has exclusive recognition power (Zivotofsky v. Kerry).

**Exceptions / defenses:** A treaty or executive agreement cannot violate individual rights (Reid v. Covert, plurality opinion). The President cannot convert a non-self-executing treaty into binding domestic law unilaterally (Medellín).

**Authority:** U.S. Const. art. II, § 2, cl. 2; art. VI, cl. 2; *Medellín v. Texas*, 552 U.S. 491 (2008); *United States v. Belmont*, 301 U.S. 324 (1937); *United States v. Pink*, 315 U.S. 203 (1942); *American Ins. Ass'n v. Garamendi*, 539 U.S. 396 (2003); *United States v. Curtiss-Wright Export Corp.*, 299 U.S. 304 (1936).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A California statute requires insurers to disclose Holocaust-era policies, conflicting with the policy of executive agreements resolving those claims. The state law is preempted (Garamendi).

### R-CON-035 — Appointments Clause
**Rule (write this):** Principal officers must be appointed by the President with the advice and consent of the Senate; Congress may vest appointment of inferior officers in the President alone, the courts of law or the heads of departments. Congress may not appoint officers who exercise executive power.

**Elements:** 1. Officer: continuing position exercising significant authority under federal law (Buckley; Lucia). 2. Inferior: directed and supervised by a Senate-confirmed principal officer (Edmond); final, unreviewable decisions are inconsistent with inferior status (Arthrex). 3. Recess appointments: the President may fill any vacancy that exists during a recess of the Senate, whether between sessions or within a session, but a recess of more than 3 and fewer than 10 days is presumptively too short (the presumption yields only to a very unusual circumstance, such as a national catastrophe, and not to political opposition), and a break of 3 days or fewer is too short in any event; the Senate is in session, including during pro forma sessions, when it says it is, provided that under its own rules it retains the capacity to transact Senate business (Noel Canning).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Mere employees need not be appointed under the Clause. Interbranch appointment of inferior officers is allowed unless incongruous (Morrison).

**Authority:** U.S. Const. art. II, § 2, cls. 2–3; *Buckley v. Valeo*, 424 U.S. 1 (1976); *Edmond v. United States*, 520 U.S. 651 (1997); *Lucia v. SEC*, 585 U.S. 237 (2018); *United States v. Arthrex, Inc.*, 594 U.S. 1 (2021); *NLRB v. Noel Canning*, 573 U.S. 513 (2014); *Morrison v. Olson*, 487 U.S. 654 (1988).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A statute lets the Speaker of the House and the Senate President pro tempore appoint members of a commission that brings civil enforcement suits. The members are officers exercising executive power; the appointment method is unconstitutional (Buckley).

### R-CON-036 — Removal power
**Rule (write this):** The President may remove principal executive officers at will, and Congress may not reserve a role for itself in removal other than impeachment. After Trump v. Slaughter (2026), statutory for-cause protection for officers of an agency that wields substantial executive power, such as the FTC, is unconstitutional; Congress may still give limited for-cause protection to inferior officers with narrow duties.

**Elements:** 1. Principal officer exercising executive power → at-will removal (Myers; Seila Law; Slaughter [S-300] [S-301]). 2. Inferior officer → Congress may limit removal by a principal officer to good cause if this does not impede the President's duty (Morrison). 3. No two layers of for-cause protection (Free Enterprise Fund). 4. No congressional removal except by impeachment (Bowsher; Myers).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Federal Reserve governors: denying the President's stay application (5–4) in an interim-docket opinion, the Court treated their for-cause protection as consistent with Article II because of the Federal Reserve's distinct history, held that a for-cause removal is judicially reviewable, and found that the President had not given the governor the procedural protections (notice and an opportunity to be heard) the statute entitles her to; it left the meaning of "cause" largely undefined (Trump v. Cook (June 29, 2026)) [S-1450] [S-1451] [S-315]. *Humphrey's Executor* (1935) is overruled [S-300].

**Authority:** U.S. Const. art. II, §§ 1, 3; *Myers v. United States*, 272 U.S. 52 (1926); *Humphrey's Executor v. United States*, 295 U.S. 602 (1935) (overruled); *Morrison v. Olson*, 487 U.S. 654 (1988); *Free Enterprise Fund v. PCAOB*, 561 U.S. 477 (2010); *Seila Law LLC v. CFPB*, 591 U.S. 197 (2020); *Collins v. Yellen*, 594 U.S. 220 (2021); *Bowsher v. Synar*, 478 U.S. 714 (1986).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. MBE items written before 2026 may assume Humphrey's; if a question clearly turns on it, apply current law (Slaughter).

**Example:** A statute says FTC commissioners may be removed only for "inefficiency, neglect of duty, or malfeasance." The President fires one over policy. Under Slaughter the restriction is invalid and the removal stands.

### R-CON-037 — Pardon power
**Rule (write this):** The President may grant reprieves and pardons for federal offenses, before or after conviction, absolutely or on conditions, except in cases of impeachment; Congress cannot limit the effect of a pardon.

**Elements:** 1. Federal offense only. 2. Not impeachment. 3. Conditions allowed if not otherwise unconstitutional (Schick).

**Exceptions / defenses:** No power over state crimes or civil liability to private parties.

**Authority:** U.S. Const. art. II, § 2, cl. 1; *Ex parte Garland*, 71 U.S. (4 Wall.) 333 (1867); *Schick v. Reed*, 419 U.S. 256 (1974).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** The President pardons a governor convicted of state bribery. The pardon has no effect on the state conviction.

### R-CON-038 — Congressional limits on the executive
**Rule (write this):** Congress controls the executive through legislation, appropriations, Senate confirmation, oversight and impeachment, but it may act with binding legal effect only through bicameral passage and presentment, so a one-house or two-house legislative veto is unconstitutional, and Congress may not itself execute the laws or retain removal power over executive officers.

**Elements:** 1. Legislative veto invalid (Chadha). 2. No congressional execution or removal (Bowsher). 3. Impeachment: House by majority; Senate trial; conviction by two-thirds of members present; grounds are treason, bribery or other high crimes and misdemeanors.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Congress may repeal or amend a delegation by ordinary legislation and may condition appropriations.

**Authority:** U.S. Const. art. I, §§ 2, 3, 7; art. II, § 4; *INS v. Chadha*, 462 U.S. 919 (1983); *Bowsher v. Synar*, 478 U.S. 714 (1986).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A statute lets either House, by resolution, overturn an agency's deportation-suspension decisions. The one-House veto violates bicameralism and presentment (Chadha).

### R-CON-039 — Presentment and veto
**Rule (write this):** Every bill must pass both Houses in identical form and be presented to the President, who may sign it, veto it (subject to override by two-thirds of each House), or let it become law without signature after ten days (Sundays excepted) unless Congress's adjournment prevents return, in which case it is pocket-vetoed. The President has no line-item veto.

**Elements:** Bicameralism; presentment; veto and override; pocket veto.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A statute letting the President cancel individual spending items after signing is an unconstitutional line-item veto (Clinton v. City of New York).

**Authority:** U.S. Const. art. I, § 7, cls. 2–3; *Clinton v. City of New York*, 524 U.S. 417 (1998); *INS v. Chadha*, 462 U.S. 919 (1983).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** Congress passes a budget act and lets the President "cancel" particular tax benefits within five days of signing. The cancellation power is unconstitutional.

### R-CON-040 — Nondelegation doctrine
**Rule (write this):** Congress may delegate rulemaking and enforcement authority to the executive branch or independent agencies if it supplies an intelligible principle to guide the delegee's discretion; Congress may not delegate to itself or its agents the power to execute the laws.

**Elements:** 1. Intelligible principle (J.W. Hampton; Whitman; Gundy, plurality opinion). 2. Delegations to private parties are more suspect (Carter Coal). 3. Clear-statement canons (major questions) operate alongside.

**Exceptions / defenses:** The Court has invalidated delegations to the executive only twice, both in 1935 (Panama Refining; Schechter); Carter Coal (1936) struck a delegation to private parties. It reaffirmed the intelligible-principle test in 2025 (FCC v. Consumers' Research [VERIFY V-200]).

**Authority:** U.S. Const. art. I, § 1; *J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394 (1928); *Whitman v. American Trucking Ass'ns*, 531 U.S. 457 (2001); *Mistretta v. United States*, 488 U.S. 361 (1989); *Gundy v. United States*, 588 U.S. 128 (2019) (plurality opinion); *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495 (1935); *Carter v. Carter Coal Co.*, 298 U.S. 238 (1936).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** Congress directs an agency to set air-quality standards "requisite to protect the public health" with an adequate margin of safety. That is an intelligible principle; the delegation is valid (Whitman).

### R-CON-041 — Presidential immunity and executive privilege
**Rule (write this):** A President is absolutely immune from civil damages for official acts within the outer perimeter of official responsibility but not for unofficial acts; is absolutely immune from criminal prosecution for acts within his conclusive and preclusive constitutional authority, presumptively immune for other official acts, and not immune for unofficial acts; and holds a qualified executive privilege for confidential communications that yields to a demonstrated, specific need for evidence in a criminal case.

**Elements:** Civil: Nixon v. Fitzgerald; Clinton v. Jones (suit over pre-presidency conduct may proceed). Criminal: Trump v. United States [VERIFY V-035]. Privilege: United States v. Nixon. State criminal subpoena for personal records: no absolute immunity and no heightened-need standard (Trump v. Vance).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Privilege is stronger for military, diplomatic or national-security secrets.

**Authority:** *Nixon v. Fitzgerald*, 457 U.S. 731 (1982); *Clinton v. Jones*, 520 U.S. 681 (1997); *United States v. Nixon*, 418 U.S. 683 (1974); *Trump v. Vance*, 591 U.S. 786 (2020); *Trump v. United States* (2024) [VERIFY V-035].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A former employee sues a sitting President for damages over a sexual-harassment incident that occurred before he took office. The suit may proceed (Clinton v. Jones).

### R-CON-042 — Speech or Debate Clause
**Rule (write this):** Members of Congress, and aides performing work that would be protected if done by the Member, are absolutely immune from civil and criminal liability, and from being questioned, for legislative acts — speech, debate, voting, committee reports and hearings.

**Elements:** 1. Defendant is a Member or aide acting as alter ego. 2. The act is an integral part of the legislative process.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Not protected: newsletters and press releases (Hutchinson), private republication, political errands, and taking a bribe (prosecution may proceed if it does not rely on legislative acts, Brewster). The Clause does not protect state legislators in federal criminal prosecutions (Gillock), though state and local legislators have common-law immunity for legislative acts in § 1983 suits (Tenney; Bogan).

**Authority:** U.S. Const. art. I, § 6, cl. 1; *Gravel v. United States*, 408 U.S. 606 (1972); *United States v. Brewster*, 408 U.S. 501 (1972); *Hutchinson v. Proxmire*, 443 U.S. 111 (1979); *United States v. Gillock*, 445 U.S. 360 (1980); *Bogan v. Scott-Harris*, 523 U.S. 44 (1998).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A senator mocks a researcher in a floor speech and repeats it in a press release. The speech is immune; the press release is not (Hutchinson).

### R-CON-043 — Judicial, prosecutorial and qualified immunity
**Rule (write this):** Judges are absolutely immune from damages for judicial acts unless they act in the clear absence of all jurisdiction; prosecutors are absolutely immune for advocacy functions; and other executive officials have qualified immunity, which shields them unless they violated a clearly established statutory or constitutional right of which a reasonable person would have known.

**Elements:** Judicial act (function normally performed by a judge, parties dealt with the judge in a judicial capacity). Qualified immunity: (1) constitutional violation, (2) clearly established at the time; courts may address either prong first. "Clearly established" means existing precedent placed the question beyond debate, so that every reasonable official would have understood the conduct was unlawful; courts must not define the right at a high level of generality (*Ashcroft v. al-Kidd*).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Administrative acts of judges (hiring and firing staff) are not absolutely immune (Forrester). Immunity does not bar prospective injunctive relief.

**Authority:** *Stump v. Sparkman*, 435 U.S. 349 (1978); *Forrester v. White*, 484 U.S. 219 (1988); *Imbler v. Pachtman*, 424 U.S. 409 (1976); *Harlow v. Fitzgerald*, 457 U.S. 800 (1982); *Pearson v. Callahan*, 555 U.S. 223 (2009); *Ashcroft v. al-Kidd*, 563 U.S. 731 (2011).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A judge approves a petition to sterilize a minor without notice. Because approving petitions is a judicial act and the court had general jurisdiction, the judge is immune (Stump).

## III. Federalism

### R-CON-044 — Federal immunity from state regulation and taxation
**Rule (write this):** States may not directly tax or regulate the federal government or its instrumentalities, or discriminate against the federal government or those who deal with it, unless Congress consents; a state may impose a nondiscriminatory tax on private parties who contract with the federal government even if the economic burden passes to it.

**Elements:** 1. Direct tax or regulation of federal operations → invalid (McCulloch; Johnson v. Maryland). 2. Discrimination against the United States or its contractors → invalid (North Dakota, plurality opinion; United States v. Washington (2022) [VERIFY V-200]). 3. Nondiscriminatory tax on private contractors → valid (United States v. New Mexico).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Congress may waive federal immunity.

**Authority:** U.S. Const. art. VI, cl. 2; *McCulloch v. Maryland*, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 316 (1819); *Johnson v. Maryland*, 254 U.S. 51 (1920); *United States v. New Mexico*, 455 U.S. 720 (1982); *North Dakota v. United States*, 495 U.S. 423 (1990) (plurality opinion); *Davis v. Michigan Dep't of Treasury*, 489 U.S. 803 (1989).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A state requires drivers of U.S. Postal Service trucks to obtain state driver's licenses. The state cannot regulate the federal operation this way (Johnson v. Maryland).

### R-CON-045 — State immunity from federal law: Tenth Amendment and generally applicable laws
**Rule (write this):** The Tenth Amendment reserves to the states powers not delegated to the federal government, but Congress may subject states to generally applicable federal laws (such as wage-and-hour laws) that also bind private parties; the states' protection lies mainly in the political process and the anti-commandeering rule.

**Elements:** 1. Is the federal law within an enumerated power? 2. Does it regulate states as states by commandeering (R-CON-046), or apply evenhandedly to states and private actors (valid, Garcia; Reno v. Condon)? 3. Clear statement needed before a statute intrudes on core state functions (Gregory).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Federal nondiscriminatory taxes on states are generally valid (South Carolina v. Baker). Congress must respect the equal sovereignty of the states when it treats them differently (Shelby County).

**Authority:** U.S. Const. amend. X; *Garcia v. San Antonio Metro. Transit Auth.*, 469 U.S. 528 (1985); *Reno v. Condon*, 528 U.S. 141 (2000); *Gregory v. Ashcroft*, 501 U.S. 452 (1991); *South Carolina v. Baker*, 485 U.S. 505 (1988).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** Congress extends the federal minimum wage to all employers, including state transit authorities. Valid under Garcia.

### R-CON-046 — Anti-commandeering
**Rule (write this):** Congress may not compel state legislatures to enact or administer a federal regulatory program, may not conscript state executive officers to enforce federal law, and may not forbid states from repealing or modifying their own laws; it may instead regulate private actors directly, offer funding conditions, or offer states the choice of regulating to federal standards or being preempted.

**Elements:** 1. Directive to state legislature (New York: take-title provision). 2. Directive to state executive officers (Printz: background checks). 3. Prohibition on state legislation (Murphy: sports-betting authorization ban).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Generally applicable laws (Garcia), laws regulating states as owners of databases in the same way as private parties, such as limits on disclosing and selling drivers' personal information (Reno v. Condon), conditional spending (Dole), and conditional preemption are permitted.

**Authority:** U.S. Const. amend. X; *New York v. United States*, 505 U.S. 144 (1992); *Printz v. United States*, 521 U.S. 898 (1997); *Murphy v. NCAA*, 584 U.S. 453 (2018).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A federal act orders county sheriffs to run background checks on gun buyers. Commandeering of state executive officers; invalid (Printz).

### R-CON-047 — Dormant Commerce Clause: discriminatory state laws
**Rule (write this):** A state or local law that discriminates against interstate commerce on its face, in purpose or in practical effect, to benefit in-state economic interests, is virtually per se invalid unless the state shows it serves a legitimate local purpose that cannot be adequately served by reasonable nondiscriminatory alternatives.

**Elements:** 1. Discrimination: differential treatment of in-state and out-of-state economic interests that benefits the former. 2. Justification: legitimate (non-protectionist) purpose. 3. No reasonable nondiscriminatory alternative. Burden on the state.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Market participant, government function, and congressional authorization (R-CON-049, R-CON-052). Maine v. Taylor shows a rare valid justification (baitfish parasites, no alternative test).

**Authority:** U.S. Const. art. I, § 8, cl. 3; *City of Philadelphia v. New Jersey*, 437 U.S. 617 (1978); *Dean Milk Co. v. City of Madison*, 340 U.S. 349 (1951); *Hunt v. Washington State Apple Advertising Comm'n*, 432 U.S. 333 (1977); *Maine v. Taylor*, 477 U.S. 131 (1986); *C & A Carbone, Inc. v. Town of Clarkstown*, 511 U.S. 383 (1994).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A state bans import of out-of-state solid waste into private landfills while allowing in-state waste. Facially discriminatory; health concerns apply equally to in-state waste; invalid (Philadelphia v. New Jersey).

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: Comparable in spirit to PRC rules against local protectionism and abuse of administrative power to restrict competition, though enforced by agencies rather than constitutional courts (approx.).

### R-CON-048 — Dormant Commerce Clause: nondiscriminatory burdens (Pike)
**Rule (write this):** A state law that regulates evenhandedly to serve a legitimate local interest, with only incidental effects on interstate commerce, is upheld unless the burden on interstate commerce is clearly excessive in relation to the putative local benefits.

**Elements:** 1. Nondiscriminatory. 2. Legitimate local interest (safety, health, environment). 3. Balance: burden clearly excessive vs. benefits? Consider less burdensome alternatives.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Safety regulations of trucks and trains with minimal safety benefit and large burdens have failed (Kassel, plurality opinion; Bibb). In National Pork Producers Council v. Ross (2023) the Court rejected a freestanding rule against state laws with extraterritorial effects and affirmed dismissal of a Pike challenge to California's Proposition 12, but no single rationale for rejecting the Pike claim drew a majority (Parts IV-B and IV-C of the principal opinion were each joined by only some Justices) [S-1465] [S-1466].

**Authority:** *Pike v. Bruce Church, Inc.*, 397 U.S. 137 (1970); *Kassel v. Consolidated Freightways Corp.*, 450 U.S. 662 (1981) (plurality opinion); *Bibb v. Navajo Freight Lines, Inc.*, 359 U.S. 520 (1959); *Exxon Corp. v. Governor of Maryland*, 437 U.S. 117 (1978); *National Pork Producers Council v. Ross*, 598 U.S. 356 (2023) (Pike discussion fractured).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A state bans 65-foot trucks on its highways, citing safety, but evidence shows they are as safe as shorter trucks while the ban forces costly rerouting. The burden clearly exceeds the illusory benefit (Kassel, plurality).

### R-CON-049 — Dormant Commerce Clause exceptions: market participant and government function
**Rule (write this):** When a state acts as a market participant — buying, selling or subsidizing with its own funds — rather than as a regulator, it may favor in-state interests without violating the dormant Commerce Clause; laws favoring a state's own public entities performing traditional government functions are also treated as nondiscriminatory.

**Elements:** 1. State's own proprietary activity (Reeves: state cement plant; Alexandria Scrap; White: city-funded construction). 2. No downstream regulation of markets the state is not in (Wunnicke, plurality on this point). 3. Public-entity preference (United Haulers: public waste facility; Davis: state bonds) → Pike at most.

**Exceptions / defenses:** The market-participant exception does not apply to the Article IV Privileges and Immunities Clause (Camden). Tax credits and regulatory measures are regulation, not participation (New Energy).

**Authority:** *Reeves, Inc. v. Stake*, 447 U.S. 429 (1980); *Hughes v. Alexandria Scrap Corp.*, 426 U.S. 794 (1976); *White v. Mass. Council of Constr. Employers*, 460 U.S. 204 (1983); *South-Central Timber Dev., Inc. v. Wunnicke*, 467 U.S. 82 (1984) (Part II majority; Parts III–IV plurality); *United Haulers Ass'n v. Oneida-Herkimer Solid Waste Mgmt. Auth.*, 550 U.S. 330 (2007); *Department of Revenue of Ky. v. Davis*, 553 U.S. 328 (2008); *New Energy Co. of Ind. v. Limbach*, 486 U.S. 269 (1988).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A state-owned railroad reserves scarce rail space for in-state manufacturers. If the state is acting as a seller of its own rail services, the market-participant exception may apply; a corporation still loses under Article IV P&I (not a citizen), but an individual out-of-state grower may press P&I.

### R-CON-050 — State taxation of interstate commerce
**Rule (write this):** A state tax on interstate commerce is valid if it (1) applies to an activity with a substantial nexus with the taxing state, (2) is fairly apportioned, (3) does not discriminate against interstate commerce, and (4) is fairly related to the services the state provides. Physical presence is not required for substantial nexus.

**Elements:** Complete Auto test; Wayfair (economic and virtual contacts suffice); internal consistency for apportionment (Wynne).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Due process separately requires a minimum connection with the taxing state.

**Authority:** *Complete Auto Transit, Inc. v. Brady*, 430 U.S. 274 (1977); *South Dakota v. Wayfair, Inc.*, 585 U.S. 162 (2018); *Comptroller of Treasury of Md. v. Wynne*, 575 U.S. 542 (2015); *West Lynn Creamery, Inc. v. Healy*, 512 U.S. 186 (1994).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A state requires large remote online sellers with substantial in-state sales but no in-state property to collect sales tax. Substantial nexus exists (Wayfair).

### R-CON-051 — Supremacy Clause and preemption
**Rule (write this):** Under the Supremacy Clause, valid federal law preempts state law (1) expressly, (2) impliedly where federal regulation occupies the field, or (3) impliedly where state law conflicts with federal law because compliance with both is impossible or the state law stands as an obstacle to the accomplishment of the full purposes and objectives of Congress.

**Elements:** 1. Valid federal law (statute, regulation within delegated authority, treaty, executive agreement). 2. Type of preemption. 3. Presumption against preemption in fields of traditional state police power (Rice), though not applied to the text of express preemption clauses (Puerto Rico v. Franklin Cal. Tax-Free Trust, 579 U.S. 115 (2016)). 4. State courts of general jurisdiction must hear federal claims of a kind they hear under state law; only a neutral rule of judicial administration is a valid excuse (Testa; Haywood).

**Exceptions / defenses:** States may usually impose stricter standards where federal law sets a floor and there is no conflict (Wyeth). Preemption requires federal law that regulates private actors, not commands to states (Murphy). Savings clauses can preserve state law.

**Authority:** U.S. Const. art. VI, cl. 2; *Rice v. Santa Fe Elevator Corp.*, 331 U.S. 218 (1947); *Hines v. Davidowitz*, 312 U.S. 52 (1941); *Florida Lime & Avocado Growers, Inc. v. Paul*, 373 U.S. 132 (1963); *Pacific Gas & Elec. Co. v. State Energy Res. Conservation & Dev. Comm'n*, 461 U.S. 190 (1983); *Crosby v. National Foreign Trade Council*, 530 U.S. 363 (2000); *Arizona v. United States*, 567 U.S. 387 (2012); *Wyeth v. Levine*, 555 U.S. 555 (2009); *Testa v. Katt*, 330 U.S. 386 (1947); *Haywood v. Drown*, 556 U.S. 729 (2009).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A state makes it a crime for an unlawfully present alien to seek work, though federal law deliberately imposes only civil penalties on employees. The state law is an obstacle to the federal scheme and is preempted (Arizona).

### R-CON-052 — Congressional authorization of otherwise invalid state action
**Rule (write this):** Because the dormant Commerce Clause is a limit Congress can lift, Congress may authorize states to regulate or tax interstate commerce in ways that would otherwise be invalid, including discriminatory ways, if it does so unmistakably clearly; Congress cannot, however, authorize states to violate individual rights such as equal protection or the Fourteenth Amendment right to travel.

**Elements:** 1. Clear and unmistakable congressional consent (Wunnicke). 2. The authorized state law must still satisfy individual-rights provisions.

**Exceptions / defenses:** An insurance tax authorized under McCarran-Ferguson still failed rational-basis equal protection where its only purpose was to favor domestic insurers (Metropolitan Life v. Ward). Congress cannot authorize durational welfare limits that violate the Citizenship/Privileges or Immunities clauses (Saenz).

**Authority:** *Prudential Ins. Co. v. Benjamin*, 328 U.S. 408 (1946); *Western & Southern Life Ins. Co. v. State Bd. of Equalization*, 451 U.S. 648 (1981); *Metropolitan Life Ins. Co. v. Ward*, 470 U.S. 869 (1985); *Saenz v. Roe*, 526 U.S. 489 (1999); *South-Central Timber Dev., Inc. v. Wunnicke*, 467 U.S. 82 (1984).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** Congress expressly allows states to tax out-of-state insurers at higher rates. The dormant Commerce Clause challenge fails, but an equal-protection challenge remains available.

### R-CON-053 — Article IV Privileges and Immunities Clause
**Rule (write this):** Article IV, § 2 prohibits a state from discriminating against citizens of other states with respect to fundamental rights — chiefly the pursuit of a livelihood and other important commercial and civil activities — unless the state has a substantial reason for the difference and the discrimination bears a substantial relationship to that reason, considering less restrictive means.

**Elements:** 1. State or city discriminates based on state (or municipal) residency. 2. Fundamental right bearing on national unity (employment, practicing a profession, owning property, access to courts). 3. Substantial reason (nonresidents are a peculiar source of the evil) and substantial relationship.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Protects only individual U.S. citizens — not corporations or aliens. Recreational activities (elk hunting, Baldwin) are not fundamental. No market-participant exception (Camden).

**Authority:** U.S. Const. art. IV, § 2, cl. 1; *Toomer v. Witsell*, 334 U.S. 385 (1948); *Baldwin v. Fish & Game Comm'n of Mont.*, 436 U.S. 371 (1978); *Hicklin v. Orbeck*, 437 U.S. 518 (1978); *United Bldg. & Constr. Trades Council v. Mayor of Camden*, 465 U.S. 208 (1984); *Supreme Court of N.H. v. Piper*, 470 U.S. 274 (1985); *McBurney v. Young*, 569 U.S. 221 (2013); *Paul v. Virginia*, 75 U.S. (8 Wall.) 168 (1869).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A state charges nonresident commercial shrimpers a license fee 100 times the resident fee. Earning a living is fundamental and the fee is not closely tied to any extra cost nonresidents impose; invalid (Toomer).

## IV. Individual rights

### R-CON-054 — State action: general rule and public function
**Rule (write this):** The Constitution's individual-rights guarantees (except the Thirteenth Amendment) restrain only government, so a plaintiff must show state action; private conduct is state action when a private party performs a function traditionally and exclusively reserved to the state, or when the state is sufficiently entangled with it.

**Elements:** Public function: running elections (Terry, no majority opinion), a company town (Marsh). Not public functions: utilities (Jackson), private schools (Rendell-Baker), nursing homes (Blum), public-access cable channels (Halleck), private self-help remedies (Flagg Bros.).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Mere licensing, regulation, funding or monopoly status is not enough.

**Authority:** *Civil Rights Cases*, 109 U.S. 3 (1883); *Marsh v. Alabama*, 326 U.S. 501 (1946); *Terry v. Adams*, 345 U.S. 461 (1953) (no majority opinion); *Jackson v. Metropolitan Edison Co.*, 419 U.S. 345 (1974); *Rendell-Baker v. Kohn*, 457 U.S. 830 (1982); *Manhattan Community Access Corp. v. Halleck*, 587 U.S. 802 (2019); *Flagg Bros., Inc. v. Brooks*, 436 U.S. 149 (1978).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. CA note: California's free-speech clause reaches the common areas of privately owned shopping centers (R-CON-118), and its privacy clause reaches private actors (see ca-distinctions).

**Example:** A heavily regulated private utility cuts off service without a hearing. Regulation and monopoly status do not make it a state actor; no due-process claim (Jackson).

### R-CON-055 — State action: entanglement, encouragement, coercion and officials' social media
**Rule (write this):** Private conduct is attributable to the state when the state compels or significantly encourages it, jointly participates in it, is entwined with the private entity's management, or judicially enforces private discrimination. A public official's social-media activity is state action only if the official had actual authority to speak for the state on the matter and purported to exercise that authority in the posts at issue.

**Elements:** Judicial enforcement of racially restrictive covenants (Shelley); symbiotic lessee of public building (Burton); joint participation in prejudgment attachment (Lugar); peremptory challenges by private litigants (Edmonson); state athletic association entwined with public schools (Brentwood); a contract physician treating prison inmates, because the state delegated its custodial duty (West); Lindke two-part test [VERIFY V-035].

**Exceptions / defenses:** State acquiescence or approval of private choices is not enough (Moose Lodge; Blum).

**Authority:** *Shelley v. Kraemer*, 334 U.S. 1 (1948); *Burton v. Wilmington Parking Auth.*, 365 U.S. 715 (1961); *Moose Lodge No. 107 v. Irvis*, 407 U.S. 163 (1972); *Blum v. Yaretsky*, 457 U.S. 991 (1982); *Lugar v. Edmondson Oil Co.*, 457 U.S. 922 (1982); *West v. Atkins*, 487 U.S. 42 (1988); *Edmonson v. Leesville Concrete Co.*, 500 U.S. 614 (1991); *Brentwood Academy v. TSSAA*, 531 U.S. 288 (2001); *Lindke v. Freed* (2024) [VERIFY V-035].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A city manager blocks a critic on his personal Facebook page, where he posts both family news and city updates. Liability depends on whether he had authority to speak for the city on those topics and was exercising it in the posts (Lindke).

### R-CON-056 — Incorporation of the Bill of Rights
**Rule (write this):** The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment incorporates against the states nearly all Bill of Rights guarantees that are fundamental to the scheme of ordered liberty or deeply rooted in history and tradition, and an incorporated right applies to the states with the same content as against the federal government.

**Elements:** Incorporated: First, Second (McDonald), Fourth, Fifth (except grand jury), Sixth (including unanimous jury, Ramos), Eighth (including excessive fines, Timbs).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Not incorporated: Fifth Amendment grand-jury indictment (Hurtado), Seventh Amendment civil jury (Bombolis); the Third Amendment has never been squarely decided.

**Authority:** U.S. Const. amend. XIV, § 1; *Hurtado v. California*, 110 U.S. 516 (1884); *McDonald v. City of Chicago*, 561 U.S. 742 (2010); *Timbs v. Indiana*, 586 U.S. 146 (2019); *Ramos v. Louisiana*, 590 U.S. 83 (2020); *Minneapolis & St. Louis R. Co. v. Bombolis*, 241 U.S. 211 (1916).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A state forfeits a defendant's $42,000 vehicle for a crime carrying a $10,000 maximum fine. The Excessive Fines Clause applies to the state (Timbs).

### R-CON-057 — Procedural due process: protected interests
**Rule (write this):** The Due Process Clauses forbid the government from depriving a person of life, liberty or property without due process of law; a property interest exists only where the claimant has a legitimate claim of entitlement created by an independent source such as state law or contract, and a liberty interest includes freedom from bodily restraint, parental rights, and other important interests, but not reputation alone.

**Elements:** 1. Government action. 2. Deprivation (intentional; negligence is not a deprivation, Daniels). 3. Protected property (tenured or for-cause job, welfare benefits, public education, driver's license) or liberty interest.

**Exceptions / defenses:** At-will public employment and a unilateral expectation are not property (Roth; Bishop). Reputation alone is not liberty; "stigma plus" a change in legal status is (Paul v. Davis).

**Authority:** U.S. Const. amends. V, XIV, § 1; *Board of Regents v. Roth*, 408 U.S. 564 (1972); *Perry v. Sindermann*, 408 U.S. 593 (1972); *Goss v. Lopez*, 419 U.S. 565 (1975); *Paul v. Davis*, 424 U.S. 693 (1976); *Daniels v. Williams*, 474 U.S. 327 (1986); *Bell v. Burson*, 402 U.S. 535 (1971).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. CA note: California's due-process clause also protects a dignitary interest (People v. Ramirez) — see ca-distinctions.

**Example:** A teacher on a one-year contract with no tenure is not renewed. No property interest (Roth). A teacher fired mid-contract or with de facto tenure has one (Sindermann).

### R-CON-058 — Procedural due process: what process is due
**Rule (write this):** Once a protected interest is at stake, the process due is determined by balancing (1) the private interest that will be affected by the official action, (2) the risk of an erroneous deprivation of that interest through the procedures used and the probable value, if any, of additional or substitute procedural safeguards, and (3) the government's interest, including the function involved and the fiscal and administrative burdens that the additional or substitute procedures would entail. At minimum due process requires notice and a meaningful opportunity to be heard, at a meaningful time and in a meaningful manner, before a neutral decisionmaker.

**Elements:** Benchmarks: welfare termination → pre-termination evidentiary hearing (Goldberg); disability benefits → no pre-termination evidentiary hearing is required; written notice and a chance to respond in writing before termination, plus a full evidentiary hearing and retroactive payment afterward, suffice (Mathews); tenured public employee → pre-termination notice and chance to respond plus full post-termination hearing (Loudermill); short school suspension → informal notice and chance to explain (Goss).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Emergencies justify post-deprivation process. A decisionmaker with a financial stake or serious probability of bias violates due process (Tumey; Caperton).

**Authority:** *Mathews v. Eldridge*, 424 U.S. 319 (1976); *Goldberg v. Kelly*, 397 U.S. 254 (1970); *Cleveland Bd. of Educ. v. Loudermill*, 470 U.S. 532 (1985); *Goss v. Lopez*, 419 U.S. 565 (1975); *Tumey v. Ohio*, 273 U.S. 510 (1927); *Caperton v. A.T. Massey Coal Co.*, 556 U.S. 868 (2009); *Connecticut v. Doehr*, 501 U.S. 1 (1991).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A tenured teacher is fired by letter with no chance to respond. Loudermill required notice of the charges, an explanation of the evidence and an opportunity to respond before termination.

### R-CON-059 — Personal jurisdiction as a due process limit
**Rule (write this):** Due process permits a court to exercise personal jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant only if the defendant has minimum contacts with the forum such that the suit does not offend traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice; general jurisdiction requires that the defendant be "at home" in the forum, and specific jurisdiction requires purposeful availment and a claim that arises out of or relates to the forum contacts.

**Elements:** See Civil Procedure for full analysis. Also: presence-based (tag) jurisdiction (Burnham) and consent; a state may base jurisdiction on registration-to-do-business consent statutes (Mallory [VERIFY V-035]).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Notice must be reasonably calculated to apprise interested parties (Mullane).

**Authority:** *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310 (1945); *Daimler AG v. Bauman*, 571 U.S. 117 (2014); *Ford Motor Co. v. Mont. Eighth Judicial Dist. Court*, 592 U.S. 351 (2021); *Bristol-Myers Squibb Co. v. Superior Court*, 582 U.S. 255 (2017); *Burnham v. Superior Court*, 495 U.S. 604 (1990); *Mullane v. Central Hanover Bank & Trust Co.*, 339 U.S. 306 (1950).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. CA note: California's long-arm statute reaches the constitutional limit (Code Civ. Proc. § 410.10).

**Example:** Nonresident plaintiffs sue a drug maker in California over injuries suffered elsewhere from pills bought elsewhere. No specific jurisdiction (Bristol-Myers Squibb).

### R-CON-060 — Substantive due process: framework and test for fundamental rights
**Rule (write this):** Substantive due process protects fundamental rights from government infringement regardless of the procedures used. A right is fundamental only if, carefully described, it is deeply rooted in the Nation's history and tradition and implicit in the concept of ordered liberty; laws that substantially burden a fundamental right must satisfy strict scrutiny, and all other laws need only a rational basis.

**Elements:** 1. Careful description of the asserted right (Glucksberg). 2. History and tradition test (Glucksberg; Dobbs). 3. Strict scrutiny: necessary to achieve a compelling interest, narrowly tailored, burden on government. 4. Otherwise rational basis.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Prisoners' rights use the Turner standard (R-CON-116). The Court has said Dobbs concerns abortion only and does not cast doubt on other precedents.

**Authority:** U.S. Const. amends. V, XIV, § 1; *Washington v. Glucksberg*, 521 U.S. 702 (1997); *Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Org.*, 597 U.S. 215 (2022).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A state bans physician-assisted suicide. Carefully described, the right to assistance in committing suicide is not deeply rooted; rational basis applies and the ban is upheld (Glucksberg).

### R-CON-061 — Fundamental rights: marriage, family, parental rights, contraception, intimate conduct
**Rule (write this):** The Court has recognized as fundamental the rights to marry (including same-sex couples), to procreate, to use contraception, to live with one's extended family, to direct the upbringing and education of one's children, and to engage in private consensual intimate conduct; it has assumed a right to refuse unwanted medical treatment.

**Elements:** Marriage (Loving; Zablocki; Obergefell); procreation (Skinner); contraception (Griswold; Eisenstadt); family living arrangements (Moore, plurality); parental rights (Meyer; Pierce; Troxel, plurality); intimate conduct (Lawrence); refusing treatment (Cruzan, assumed).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Reasonable regulations that do not significantly interfere with marriage are valid. No fundamental right to assisted suicide (Glucksberg). Parental rights may yield to compelling child-protection interests.

**Authority:** *Loving v. Virginia*, 388 U.S. 1 (1967); *Zablocki v. Redhail*, 434 U.S. 374 (1978); *Obergefell v. Hodges*, 576 U.S. 644 (2015); *Skinner v. Oklahoma*, 316 U.S. 535 (1942); *Griswold v. Connecticut*, 381 U.S. 479 (1965); *Moore v. City of East Cleveland*, 431 U.S. 494 (1977) (plurality); *Meyer v. Nebraska*, 262 U.S. 390 (1923); *Pierce v. Society of Sisters*, 268 U.S. 510 (1925); *Troxel v. Granville*, 530 U.S. 57 (2000) (plurality); *Lawrence v. Texas*, 539 U.S. 558 (2003); *Cruzan v. Director, Mo. Dep't of Health*, 497 U.S. 261 (1990).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A zoning ordinance forbids a grandmother from living with two grandsons who are cousins. It intrudes on family living arrangements and fails heightened review (Moore, plurality).

### R-CON-062 — Abortion after Dobbs
**Rule (write this):** The Constitution does not confer a right to abortion; Roe and Casey are overruled, and abortion regulations are reviewed under rational basis, leaving the issue to the people and their elected representatives.

**Elements:** Rational relation to legitimate interests (respect for prenatal life, maternal health, integrity of the medical profession).

**Exceptions / defenses:** State constitutions may protect abortion. California does (Cal. Const. art. I, § 1.1) [S-320].

**Authority:** *Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Org.*, 597 U.S. 215 (2022).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same federal rule; on a California-law call, cite art. I, § 1.1.

**Example:** A state bans most abortions after six weeks. Under Dobbs the ban is reviewed for rational basis and survives a federal due-process challenge.

### R-CON-063 — Economic and social regulation: rational basis; punitive damages
**Rule (write this):** Laws regulating economic and social matters that do not burden fundamental rights are upheld under substantive due process if rationally related to any legitimate government interest, and the challenger bears the burden. Grossly excessive punitive damages violate due process.

**Elements:** 1. Rational basis (Lee Optical; West Coast Hotel). 2. Punitive damages guideposts: reprehensibility, ratio to compensatory damages (single-digit ratios presumptively), comparable civil penalties (Gore; State Farm).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Executive action violates substantive due process only if it "shocks the conscience" (Lewis). The state generally has no duty to protect individuals from private violence (DeShaney).

**Authority:** *Williamson v. Lee Optical of Okla., Inc.*, 348 U.S. 483 (1955); *West Coast Hotel Co. v. Parrish*, 300 U.S. 379 (1937); *BMW of N. Am., Inc. v. Gore*, 517 U.S. 559 (1996); *State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co. v. Campbell*, 538 U.S. 408 (2003); *County of Sacramento v. Lewis*, 523 U.S. 833 (1998); *DeShaney v. Winnebago County*, 489 U.S. 189 (1989).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A state forbids opticians to fit lenses without an optometrist's prescription. A legislature could rationally think this protects eye health; valid (Lee Optical).

### R-CON-064 — Right to interstate travel
**Rule (write this):** The right to travel includes the right to enter and leave another state, to be treated as a welcome visitor while temporarily present (Article IV P&I), and, for those who become permanent residents, to be treated like other citizens of that state (Fourteenth Amendment Privileges or Immunities Clause); laws that penalize new residents in the distribution of basic necessities trigger strict scrutiny.

**Elements:** 1. Durational residency requirement or classification by length of residence. 2. Basic necessity (welfare, medical care) or the vote → strict scrutiny. 3. Otherwise a lesser standard (divorce: Sosna; reasonable residency for in-state tuition).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Thirty-day voter registration periods are allowed; one-year voting residency was not (Dunn).

**Authority:** *Saenz v. Roe*, 526 U.S. 489 (1999); *Shapiro v. Thompson*, 394 U.S. 618 (1969); *Memorial Hospital v. Maricopa County*, 415 U.S. 250 (1974); *Dunn v. Blumstein*, 405 U.S. 330 (1972); *Sosna v. Iowa*, 419 U.S. 393 (1975).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** California limited new residents' welfare benefits for their first year to the level of their former state. The limit violated the Privileges or Immunities Clause (Saenz).

### R-CON-065 — Second Amendment
**Rule (write this):** The Second Amendment protects an individual right to keep and bear arms for self-defense, incorporated against the states. When the Amendment's plain text covers the conduct, the government must justify its regulation by showing it is consistent with the Nation's historical tradition of firearm regulation; analogues need not be historical twins, but the modern law must be consistent with the principles underlying the tradition.

**Elements:** 1. Text: "the people," "arms," "keep and bear." 2. Historical tradition: why and how the law burdens the right (Rahimi [VERIFY V-035]). No means-end scrutiny (Bruen).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Presumptively lawful: bans on possession by felons and the mentally ill, sensitive-place restrictions, conditions on commercial sale (Heller). Temporarily disarming a person a court found to pose a credible threat is valid (Rahimi). A default ban on carry onto private property open to the public absent owner consent is invalid (Wolford v. Lopez (2026) [S-311]). 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(3) is invalid as applied to a defendant prosecuted solely because of his marijuana use, without individualized proof that the use made him dangerous; the Court did not decide a prosecution resting on such proof or the statute's application to addicts (United States v. Hemani (June 18, 2026)) [S-316] [S-1452].

**Authority:** U.S. Const. amend. II; *District of Columbia v. Heller*, 554 U.S. 570 (2008); *McDonald v. City of Chicago*, 561 U.S. 742 (2010); *New York State Rifle & Pistol Ass'n v. Bruen*, 597 U.S. 1 (2022); *United States v. Rahimi* (2024) [VERIFY V-035].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A state requires applicants for a public-carry license to show a special need for self-defense. No historical tradition supports a special-need requirement; invalid (Bruen).

### R-CON-066 — Equal protection framework: tiers and burdens
**Rule (write this):** The Equal Protection Clause (and the equal-protection component of Fifth Amendment due process for the federal government) requires that similarly situated persons be treated alike. Classifications based on race, national origin, state alienage, or burdening fundamental rights get strict scrutiny (government must prove necessary to a compelling interest); classifications based on sex or nonmarital birth get intermediate scrutiny (government must prove substantially related to an important interest, with an exceedingly persuasive justification for sex); all others get rational-basis review (challenger must prove no rational relation to any legitimate interest).

**Elements:** 1. State action or federal action. 2. Identify the classification. 3. Choose the tier. 4. Apply ends and means, with the burden on the correct party.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A class-of-one claim is available for irrational, intentional differential treatment (Olech) but not in public employment (Engquist).

**Authority:** U.S. Const. amend. XIV, § 1; *Bolling v. Sharpe*, 347 U.S. 497 (1954); *Craig v. Boren*, 429 U.S. 190 (1976); *United States v. Virginia*, 518 U.S. 515 (1996); *Village of Willowbrook v. Olech*, 528 U.S. 562 (2000); *Engquist v. Oregon Dep't of Agric.*, 553 U.S. 591 (2008).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. CA note: the California Constitution treats sex and sexual orientation as suspect (strict scrutiny) — ca-distinctions.

**Example:** A federal law discriminates by race. The Fourteenth Amendment does not apply to the federal government, but Fifth Amendment due process imposes the same strict scrutiny (Bolling).

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: The PRC Constitution declares all citizens equal before the law, but it has no tiered judicial-scrutiny framework (approx.).

### R-CON-067 — Proving a discriminatory classification
**Rule (write this):** A law classifies on a suspect or quasi-suspect basis if it does so on its face, if it is applied in a discriminatory way, or if it is facially neutral but was enacted with a discriminatory purpose; disparate impact alone is not enough, and purpose means the decisionmaker acted "because of," not merely "in spite of," the effect on the group.

**Elements:** 1. Facial classification. 2. Discriminatory application (Yick Wo). 3. Neutral law: impact plus purpose, shown by Arlington Heights factors (impact, historical background, sequence of events, departures from normal procedure, legislative history).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Once purpose is shown, the government may show it would have made the same decision anyway. At a preliminary stage, officials' statements that were not overtly racial and expressed policy views that could rest on race-neutral justifications did not make it likely that race motivated a decision (Mullin v. Doe (June 25, 2026), 6–3) [S-1454] [S-318].

**Authority:** *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356 (1886); *Washington v. Davis*, 426 U.S. 229 (1976); *Village of Arlington Heights v. Metro. Hous. Dev. Corp.*, 429 U.S. 252 (1977); *Personnel Adm'r of Mass. v. Feeney*, 442 U.S. 256 (1979); *Hunter v. Underwood*, 471 U.S. 222 (1985).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A city requires a verbal-skills test for police recruits that more Black applicants fail. Without proof of discriminatory purpose, rational basis applies and the test is upheld (Washington v. Davis).

### R-CON-068 — Race and national origin: strict scrutiny, including affirmative action
**Rule (write this):** Every government classification based on race or national origin, whether it burdens or benefits a minority group, is subject to strict scrutiny and is valid only if narrowly tailored to achieve a compelling government interest. Remedying identified past discrimination by the same governmental unit can be compelling; remedying general societal discrimination is not, and race-conscious university admissions programs that lack measurable objectives, use race as a negative or a stereotype, and have no logical end point are invalid.

**Elements:** 1. Racial classification. 2. Compelling interest. 3. Narrow tailoring: necessity, race-neutral alternatives considered, limited duration, no quotas.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Racial segregation in prisons is subject to strict scrutiny, not the deferential prison standard (Johnson v. California).

**Authority:** *Brown v. Board of Education*, 347 U.S. 483 (1954); *Loving v. Virginia*, 388 U.S. 1 (1967); *City of Richmond v. J.A. Croson Co.*, 488 U.S. 469 (1989); *Adarand Constructors, Inc. v. Peña*, 515 U.S. 200 (1995); *Parents Involved in Community Schools v. Seattle School Dist. No. 1*, 551 U.S. 701 (2007); *Johnson v. California*, 543 U.S. 499 (2005); *Students for Fair Admissions v. President & Fellows of Harvard College* (2023) [VERIFY V-035].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. CA note: Cal. Const. art. I, § 31 separately bars state preferences by race or sex in public employment, education and contracting.

**Example:** A city sets aside 30% of construction subcontracts for minority-owned firms, citing nationwide discrimination. No evidence of the city's own discrimination; not narrowly tailored; invalid (Croson).

### R-CON-069 — Race in redistricting
**Rule (write this):** A districting plan in which race was the predominant factor in drawing lines is subject to strict scrutiny; plaintiffs must disentangle race from politics, and courts presume the legislature acted in good faith. Partisan gerrymandering claims are nonjusticiable in federal court.

**Elements:** 1. Predominance of race over traditional principles (Shaw; Miller). 2. Strict scrutiny: compliance with the Voting Rights Act was assumed compelling, but after Louisiana v. Callais (2026) VRA § 2 requires a strong inference of intentional discrimination and did not require the challenged majority-minority district [S-309] [S-310].

**Exceptions / defenses:** Alexander v. South Carolina State Conference of NAACP (2024) [VERIFY V-200] stressed the good-faith presumption.

**Authority:** *Shaw v. Reno*, 509 U.S. 630 (1993); *Miller v. Johnson*, 515 U.S. 900 (1995); *Cooper v. Harris*, 581 U.S. 285 (2017); *Rucho v. Common Cause*, 588 U.S. 684 (2019).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A legislature draws a bizarrely shaped district to reach a numerical racial target. Race predominates; strict scrutiny applies and, absent a VRA necessity, the district fails.

### R-CON-070 — Alienage
**Rule (write this):** State and local laws discriminating against lawfully present aliens get strict scrutiny, except that laws excluding aliens from positions involving self-government or important public functions — such as police officers, teachers, probation officers and jurors — get rational-basis review. Federal alienage classifications get rational-basis review because of Congress's plenary immigration power. Undocumented status is not suspect, but a state may not deny undocumented children a free public education without substantial justification.

**Elements:** Who is the actor (state or federal)? Is the job a political or governmental function? Is it the Plyler setting?

**Exceptions / defenses:** Notaries public are not a political function (Bernal).

**Authority:** *Graham v. Richardson*, 403 U.S. 365 (1971); *Sugarman v. Dougall*, 413 U.S. 634 (1973); *Foley v. Connelie*, 435 U.S. 291 (1978); *Ambach v. Norwick*, 441 U.S. 68 (1979); *Cabell v. Chavez-Salido*, 454 U.S. 432 (1982); *Bernal v. Fainter*, 467 U.S. 216 (1984); *Mathews v. Diaz*, 426 U.S. 67 (1976); *Plyler v. Doe*, 457 U.S. 202 (1982).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A state requires public-school teachers to be U.S. citizens. Teaching is a governmental function; rational basis applies; valid (Ambach).

### R-CON-071 — Sex: intermediate scrutiny
**Rule (write this):** Sex-based classifications are valid only if the government shows an exceedingly persuasive justification: the classification must serve important governmental objectives and be substantially related to achieving them, and it may not rest on overbroad generalizations about the different talents, capacities or preferences of men and women.

**Elements:** 1. Facial or purposeful sex classification. 2. Important objective (actual, not post hoc). 3. Substantial relationship.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Upheld: statutory rape law punishing only males (Michael M., plurality opinion), male-only draft registration (Rostker), different citizenship-proof rules for fathers tied to biology (Nguyen). Struck: VMI's male-only admission (Virginia); nursing school excluding men (Hogan); citizenship-transmission rules based on stereotypes (Morales-Santana). Pregnancy classifications are not sex classifications (Geduldig).

**Authority:** *Craig v. Boren*, 429 U.S. 190 (1976); *Mississippi Univ. for Women v. Hogan*, 458 U.S. 718 (1982); *United States v. Virginia*, 518 U.S. 515 (1996); *Michael M. v. Superior Court*, 450 U.S. 464 (1981) (plurality opinion); *Rostker v. Goldberg*, 453 U.S. 57 (1981); *Nguyen v. INS*, 533 U.S. 53 (2001); *Sessions v. Morales-Santana*, 582 U.S. 47 (2017); *Geduldig v. Aiello*, 417 U.S. 484 (1974).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same federal rule. CA note: sex is a suspect class under Cal. Const. art. I, § 7 (Sail'er Inn).

**Example:** A state military college admits only men and offers women a less prestigious parallel program. No exceedingly persuasive justification; invalid (Virginia).

### R-CON-072 — Transgender status and sex-linked medical and athletic classifications
**Rule (write this):** A law that classifies by age and medical use (for example, banning certain treatments for minors' gender dysphoria) is not a sex classification and receives rational-basis review. Laws limiting girls' and women's sports teams to biological females are sex classifications that the Court has upheld under intermediate scrutiny (important interests in safety and competitive fairness, substantially served), while leaving open what standard governs classifications based on transgender status itself.

**Elements:** 1. Identify the actual classification (age, medical purpose, biological sex, transgender status). 2. Apply the corresponding tier.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Whether transgender status is a quasi-suspect class remains unresolved by a majority.

**Authority:** *United States v. Skrmetti* (2025) [VERIFY V-035]; *Little v. Hecox* and *West Virginia v. B.P.J.* (2026) [S-307] [S-308].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same federal rule. On an essay, argue both tiers if the classification could be framed as sex-based.

**Example:** A state bars biological males from girls' high-school track teams. The law classifies by sex; under Hecox/B.P.J. the safety and fairness interests satisfy intermediate scrutiny.

### R-CON-073 — Nonmarital children
**Rule (write this):** Classifications disadvantaging children born outside marriage receive intermediate scrutiny and must be substantially related to an important government interest; laws that penalize the child for the parents' status, such as total bars on inheritance or support, are invalid.

**Elements:** Important interest (orderly estate administration, proof of paternity); substantial relationship.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Reasonable proof-of-paternity requirements are valid (Lalli, plurality opinion).

**Authority:** *Clark v. Jeter*, 486 U.S. 456 (1988); *Trimble v. Gordon*, 430 U.S. 762 (1977); *Lalli v. Lalli*, 439 U.S. 259 (1978) (plurality opinion).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A state bars nonmarital children from inheriting from their fathers by intestacy under any circumstances. Not substantially related to proof concerns; invalid (Trimble).

### R-CON-074 — Rational-basis classifications and animus
**Rule (write this):** Classifications based on age, disability, wealth, economic status and (under federal law) sexual orientation receive rational-basis review: they are upheld if rationally related to any conceivable legitimate interest, even one the legislature did not articulate. A law motivated by a bare desire to harm a politically unpopular group fails even rational-basis review.

**Elements:** 1. Legitimate interest (any conceivable). 2. Rational relationship (over- or under-inclusiveness tolerated).

**Exceptions / defenses:** "Rational basis with bite": animus or irrational prejudice invalidates (Moreno; Cleburne; Romer; Windsor).

**Authority:** *Railway Express Agency v. New York*, 336 U.S. 106 (1949); *FCC v. Beach Communications, Inc.*, 508 U.S. 307 (1993); *Massachusetts Bd. of Retirement v. Murgia*, 427 U.S. 307 (1976); *City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Center*, 473 U.S. 432 (1985); *San Antonio Indep. School Dist. v. Rodriguez*, 411 U.S. 1 (1973); *U.S. Dep't of Agric. v. Moreno*, 413 U.S. 528 (1973); *Romer v. Evans*, 517 U.S. 620 (1996); *United States v. Windsor*, 570 U.S. 744 (2013); *Nordlinger v. Hahn*, 505 U.S. 1 (1992).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same federal rule. CA note: sexual orientation is suspect under the California Constitution (In re Marriage Cases).

**Example:** A city requires a special permit for a group home for people with intellectual disabilities but not for similar group uses. Based only on neighbors' fears, it fails rational basis (Cleburne).

### R-CON-075 — Fundamental rights under equal protection: voting
**Rule (write this):** The right to vote is fundamental; laws that deny the franchise to some citizens or dilute votes trigger strict scrutiny, and legislative districts must have substantially equal populations (one person, one vote). Election regulations are reviewed on a sliding scale: severe burdens get strict scrutiny, while reasonable, nondiscriminatory restrictions are justified by the state's important regulatory interests.

**Elements:** Denials (poll taxes, Harper; property qualifications, Kramer); apportionment (Reynolds; Wesberry); Anderson–Burdick balancing (Crawford upheld voter ID; plurality opinion).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Special-purpose districts may limit voting to affected landowners (Salyer). States may disenfranchise felons (Richardson v. Ramirez, under § 2 of the Fourteenth Amendment).

**Authority:** *Reynolds v. Sims*, 377 U.S. 533 (1964); *Wesberry v. Sanders*, 376 U.S. 1 (1964); *Harper v. Virginia Bd. of Elections*, 383 U.S. 663 (1966); *Kramer v. Union Free School Dist. No. 15*, 395 U.S. 621 (1969); *Salyer Land Co. v. Tulare Lake Basin Water Storage Dist.*, 410 U.S. 719 (1973); *Richardson v. Ramirez*, 418 U.S. 24 (1974); *Burdick v. Takushi*, 504 U.S. 428 (1992); *Crawford v. Marion County Election Bd.*, 553 U.S. 181 (2008) (plurality opinion); *Bush v. Gore*, 531 U.S. 98 (2000).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A school district limits school-board voting to property owners and parents. A general-purpose election; strict scrutiny; invalid (Kramer).

### R-CON-076 — Fundamental rights under equal protection: access to courts; education
**Rule (write this):** The state may not condition access to the courts on ability to pay where a fundamental interest is at stake — such as divorce, termination of parental rights, a criminal transcript on appeal, or counsel on a first appeal as of right. Education is not a fundamental right and wealth is not a suspect class, so school-funding disparities get rational-basis review.

**Elements:** Fee waivers (Boddie; M.L.B.); criminal appeals (Griffin, plurality opinion; Douglas).

**Exceptions / defenses:** No right to counsel on discretionary appeals (Ross v. Moffitt); bankruptcy filing fees valid (Kras). Total denial of education to undocumented children fails (Plyler).

**Authority:** *Griffin v. Illinois*, 351 U.S. 12 (1956) (plurality opinion); *Douglas v. California*, 372 U.S. 353 (1963); *Boddie v. Connecticut*, 401 U.S. 371 (1971); *M.L.B. v. S.L.J.*, 519 U.S. 102 (1996); *Ross v. Moffitt*, 417 U.S. 600 (1974); *United States v. Kras*, 409 U.S. 434 (1973); *San Antonio Indep. School Dist. v. Rodriguez*, 411 U.S. 1 (1973).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same federal rule. CA note: education is fundamental under the California Constitution (Serrano v. Priest).

**Example:** A state funds schools by local property taxes, producing large per-pupil gaps. Rational basis; upheld federally (Rodriguez).

### R-CON-077 — Takings: public use and just compensation
**Rule (write this):** The Takings Clause, applicable to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment, permits the government to take private property only for public use and with just compensation; "public use" means a public purpose, including economic development, and just compensation is the fair market value of the property at the time of the taking.

**Elements:** 1. Property interest. 2. Taking (physical, regulatory, exaction). 3. Public use: rationally related to a conceivable public purpose (Midkiff; Kelo). 4. Just compensation (fair market value to the owner, not the gain to the taker).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Transfer from A to B for no public purpose is invalid. State law may limit eminent domain more strictly (Cal. Const. art. I, § 19(b) [S-321] [S-322]).

**Authority:** U.S. Const. amend. V; *Berman v. Parker*, 348 U.S. 26 (1954); *Hawaii Housing Auth. v. Midkiff*, 467 U.S. 229 (1984); *Kelo v. City of New London*, 545 U.S. 469 (2005).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same federal rule. CA note: California protects property "taken or damaged" (art. I, § 19).

**Example:** A city condemns homes for a private office park under a comprehensive redevelopment plan. Economic development is a public use; valid with just compensation (Kelo).

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: The PRC Constitution also permits expropriation or requisition of private property in the public interest with compensation (approx.).

### R-CON-078 — Physical takings (per se)
**Rule (write this):** A permanent physical occupation of property authorized by the government, however small, and a government-authorized physical invasion or appropriation of property — including regulations granting third parties a right of access — are per se takings requiring just compensation.

**Elements:** 1. Physical occupation or appropriation (Loretto: cable box; Horne: raisin reserve; Cedar Point: union-access regulation). 2. Government authorization.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Not per se: access consistent with longstanding background property-law restrictions, isolated trespasses, and conditions on benefits that satisfy Nollan/Dolan (Cedar Point). Rent control of mobile-home parks is not a physical taking (Yee). Keeping surplus equity after a tax-foreclosure sale beyond the tax debt is a taking (Tyler v. Hennepin County [VERIFY V-035]).

**Authority:** *Loretto v. Teleprompter Manhattan CATV Corp.*, 458 U.S. 419 (1982); *Horne v. Dep't of Agric.*, 576 U.S. 350 (2015); *Cedar Point Nursery v. Hassid*, 594 U.S. 139 (2021); *Yee v. City of Escondido*, 503 U.S. 519 (1992).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A California regulation lets union organizers enter farms three hours a day for 120 days a year. The right of access appropriates the owner's right to exclude; per se taking (Cedar Point).

### R-CON-079 — Regulatory takings: Lucas and Penn Central
**Rule (write this):** A regulation that deprives land of all economically beneficial use is a per se taking unless the use was already prohibited by background principles of nuisance and property law. Any other regulation is evaluated under Penn Central's ad hoc test: (1) the economic impact on the owner, (2) the extent of interference with distinct investment-backed expectations, and (3) the character of the government action.

**Elements:** Lucas total wipeout; Penn Central factors; parcel as a whole (Murr; Tahoe-Sierra; no conceptual severance). Acquiring title after the regulation does not automatically bar a claim (Palazzolo).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Mere diminution in value (even large) is usually not a taking (Euclid; Penn Central). A temporary development moratorium is analyzed under Penn Central (Tahoe-Sierra). The "substantially advances" test is not a takings test (Lingle).

**Authority:** *Pennsylvania Coal Co. v. Mahon*, 260 U.S. 393 (1922); *Penn Central Transp. Co. v. New York City*, 438 U.S. 104 (1978); *Lucas v. South Carolina Coastal Council*, 505 U.S. 1003 (1992); *Palazzolo v. Rhode Island*, 533 U.S. 606 (2001); *Tahoe-Sierra Pres. Council, Inc. v. Tahoe Reg'l Planning Agency*, 535 U.S. 302 (2002); *Lingle v. Chevron U.S.A. Inc.*, 544 U.S. 528 (2005); *Murr v. Wisconsin*, 582 U.S. 383 (2017); *Village of Euclid v. Ambler Realty Co.*, 272 U.S. 365 (1926).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A coastal law bars any building on two beachfront lots bought for homes, leaving them valueless. Lucas per se taking unless background nuisance principles already barred building.

### R-CON-080 — Exactions: Nollan, Dolan, Koontz, Sheetz
**Rule (write this):** When the government conditions a land-use permit on the owner's dedication of property or payment of money, the condition must have an essential nexus to a legitimate state interest that the permit denial would serve, and must be roughly proportional, by an individualized determination, to the impact of the proposed development. The test applies to permit denials for refusing a condition, to monetary exactions, and to conditions imposed by legislation, though the Court left open whether a legislative condition applied to a class of properties needs the same individualized tailoring as an ad hoc one.

**Elements:** 1. Exaction (property or money). 2. Essential nexus (Nollan). 3. Rough proportionality (Dolan). 4. Applies to denials and money (Koontz) and legislative conditions (Sheetz [VERIFY V-035]; degree of tailoring for class-wide fees left open).

**Exceptions / defenses:** General land-use regulations that do not demand property are judged under Penn Central.

**Authority:** *Nollan v. California Coastal Comm'n*, 483 U.S. 825 (1987); *Dolan v. City of Tigard*, 512 U.S. 374 (1994); *Koontz v. St. Johns River Water Mgmt. Dist.*, 570 U.S. 595 (2013); *Sheetz v. County of El Dorado* (2024) [VERIFY V-035].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** The California Coastal Commission conditions a beachfront rebuilding permit on a public lateral-access easement, citing views from the road. No nexus between the easement and the view concern; the condition is a taking (Nollan).

### R-CON-081 — Temporary takings, remedies and when to sue
**Rule (write this):** When a regulation effects a taking, the government must pay just compensation for the period it was in effect, even if later rescinded; a property owner may sue a local government in federal court under 42 U.S.C. § 1983 as soon as it takes property without paying, without first seeking compensation in state court (Knick). A State itself is not a § 1983 "person" and may assert sovereign immunity in federal court (*Will v. Michigan Dep't of State Police*, 491 U.S. 58 (1989)), so a takings claim against a State generally proceeds in state court.

**Elements:** 1. Temporary taking compensable (First English). 2. Knick: no state-litigation requirement. 3. Final decision by the regulator still required for regulatory claims.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Injunctions against takings are generally unavailable when compensation is available.

**Authority:** *First English Evangelical Lutheran Church v. County of Los Angeles*, 482 U.S. 304 (1987); *Knick v. Township of Scott*, 588 U.S. 180 (2019); *Tahoe-Sierra*, 535 U.S. 302 (2002).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same federal rule; California inverse-condemnation actions are the state-court route.

**Example:** A county bars rebuilding in a flood zone for six years before repealing the ban; if the ban denied all use, the owner recovers for the six years (First English).

### R-CON-082 — Privileges or Immunities Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment
**Rule (write this):** The Fourteenth Amendment's Privileges or Immunities Clause protects only the privileges of national citizenship — such as the right to travel and become a citizen of another state, to petition Congress, to vote in federal elections and to use navigable waters — and is rarely a winning argument except for the new-resident component of the right to travel.

**Elements:** 1. Right of national citizenship. 2. State abridgment.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Does not protect corporations or aliens; it is not the vehicle for incorporating the Bill of Rights (Slaughter-House; McDonald plurality).

**Authority:** U.S. Const. amend. XIV, § 1; *Slaughter-House Cases*, 83 U.S. (16 Wall.) 36 (1873); *Saenz v. Roe*, 526 U.S. 489 (1999).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A state law gives newly arrived residents lower welfare benefits than longtime residents. Violates the Privileges or Immunities Clause (Saenz).

### R-CON-083 — Contracts Clause
**Rule (write this):** No state may pass a law impairing the obligation of contracts. A state law that substantially impairs an existing contractual relationship is valid only if it is drawn in an appropriate and reasonable way to advance a significant and legitimate public purpose; courts defer less when the state impairs its own contracts.

**Elements:** 1. State legislation (not a court decision; not federal law). 2. Existing contract (retroactive impairment). 3. Substantial impairment (consider the industry's prior regulation and reliance). 4. Significant and legitimate public purpose and reasonable, appropriate means.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Federal laws impairing contracts are reviewed only under due process (rational basis). Prospective laws affecting future contracts do not implicate the Clause.

**Authority:** U.S. Const. art. I, § 10, cl. 1; *Home Bldg. & Loan Ass'n v. Blaisdell*, 290 U.S. 398 (1934); *United States Trust Co. of N.Y. v. New Jersey*, 431 U.S. 1 (1977); *Allied Structural Steel Co. v. Spannaus*, 438 U.S. 234 (1978); *Energy Reserves Group, Inc. v. Kansas Power & Light Co.*, 459 U.S. 400 (1983); *Sveen v. Melin*, 584 U.S. 811 (2018).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** During a depression, a state temporarily extends mortgage-redemption periods while requiring reasonable rent payments. Reasonable, temporary, for an important purpose; valid (Blaisdell).

### R-CON-084 — Ex post facto laws
**Rule (write this):** Neither Congress nor the states may pass an ex post facto law — a retroactive criminal or penal law that (1) punishes an act innocent when done, (2) aggravates a crime, (3) increases the punishment, or (4) alters the rules of evidence to make conviction easier. The Clauses apply only to punitive measures, not civil regulation.

**Elements:** 1. Retroactive. 2. Criminal or punitive in purpose or effect (Smith v. Doe intent-effects test). 3. Disadvantages the offender within a Calder category.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Civil sex-offender registration is not punitive (Smith v. Doe). Reviving an already-expired limitations period is ex post facto (Stogner). Retroactive judicial expansions of crimes are limited by due process, not the Ex Post Facto Clauses (Bouie; Rogers). MVRA restitution is criminal punishment for Ex Post Facto purposes (Ellingburg v. United States (2026) [VERIFY V-034]).

**Authority:** U.S. Const. art. I, § 9, cl. 3; art. I, § 10, cl. 1; *Calder v. Bull*, 3 U.S. (3 Dall.) 386 (1798); *Smith v. Doe*, 538 U.S. 84 (2003); *Stogner v. California*, 539 U.S. 607 (2003); *Peugh v. United States*, 569 U.S. 530 (2013); *Bouie v. City of Columbia*, 378 U.S. 347 (1964); *Rogers v. Tennessee*, 532 U.S. 451 (2001).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** California extended the limitations period for child-abuse offenses and applied it to crimes whose original period had already run. Ex post facto (Stogner).

### R-CON-085 — Bills of attainder
**Rule (write this):** Neither Congress nor the states may pass a bill of attainder — a legislative act that inflicts punishment on specifically identified persons or groups without a judicial trial.

**Elements:** 1. Specificity (named or easily ascertainable persons). 2. Punishment (historical, functional or motivational test). 3. No judicial trial.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Nonpunitive regulation of a class of one may be valid (Nixon v. Administrator of General Services).

**Authority:** U.S. Const. art. I, § 9, cl. 3; art. I, § 10, cl. 1; *United States v. Lovett*, 328 U.S. 303 (1946); *United States v. Brown*, 381 U.S. 437 (1965); *Nixon v. Adm'r of Gen. Servs.*, 433 U.S. 425 (1977).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** Congress cuts off the salaries of three named federal employees it deems "subversive." Bill of attainder (Lovett).

### R-CON-086 — Unconstitutional conditions
**Rule (write this):** The government may not condition a benefit — employment, a tax exemption, a license, a grant, or a land-use permit — on the recipient's surrender of a constitutional right, even though it could deny the benefit altogether. The government may, however, define the limits of its own program and decline to subsidize speech.

**Elements:** 1. Benefit. 2. Condition requiring waiver of a right. 3. Is the condition defining the program (valid) or leveraging funds to regulate speech or rights outside it (invalid)?

**Exceptions / defenses:** Rust (no abortion counseling within a Title X project) vs. AOSI (cannot require grantees to adopt a policy opposing prostitution as their own view). Land-use exactions follow Nollan/Dolan/Koontz.

**Authority:** *Speiser v. Randall*, 357 U.S. 513 (1958); *Perry v. Sindermann*, 408 U.S. 593 (1972); *Rust v. Sullivan*, 500 U.S. 173 (1991); *Agency for Int'l Dev. v. Alliance for Open Society Int'l, Inc.*, 570 U.S. 205 (2013); *Legal Services Corp. v. Velazquez*, 531 U.S. 533 (2001); *Koontz v. St. Johns River Water Mgmt. Dist.*, 570 U.S. 595 (2013).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** California required veterans to sign a loyalty declaration to receive a property-tax exemption. Conditioning the benefit on speech without procedural safeguards was invalid (Speiser).

### R-CON-087 — Free Exercise: neutral and generally applicable laws
**Rule (write this):** A neutral law of general applicability that incidentally burdens religious practice is valid if rationally related to a legitimate interest. A law that is not neutral (it targets religion or shows hostility to it) or not generally applicable (it allows individualized exemptions or treats any comparable secular activity more favorably than religious activity) must satisfy strict scrutiny.

**Elements:** 1. Sincere religious belief (not its truth or centrality). 2. Burden. 3. Neutral? (object, text, history, hostility). 4. Generally applicable? (exemption system; comparable secular activity judged by the risks the government's interest addresses). 5. If not, strict scrutiny.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Ministerial exception and church autonomy (R-CON-088). Statutory protection under RFRA and RLUIPA (R-CON-089).

**Authority:** U.S. Const. amend. I; *Employment Div. v. Smith*, 494 U.S. 872 (1990); *Church of the Lukumi Babalu Aye, Inc. v. City of Hialeah*, 508 U.S. 520 (1993); *Masterpiece Cakeshop, Ltd. v. Colorado Civil Rights Comm'n*, 584 U.S. 617 (2018); *Fulton v. City of Philadelphia*, 593 U.S. 522 (2021); *Tandon v. Newsom*, 593 U.S. 61 (2021); *Kennedy v. Bremerton School Dist.*, 597 U.S. 507 (2022).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** After one church burns down, a city bans burning candles only "in churches," while restaurants may use candles. The ban targets religion and treats comparable secular activity better; strict scrutiny applies and the ban likely fails.

### R-CON-088 — Free Exercise: religious-status exclusions, parental rights, ministerial exception, sincerity
**Rule (write this):** A state that offers a public benefit may not exclude otherwise eligible recipients because they are religious or will use the benefit for religious purposes, unless the exclusion survives strict scrutiny. Laws that substantially interfere with parents' religious upbringing of their children, as in Yoder, receive strict scrutiny, and religious organizations' selection of ministers is beyond government control.

**Elements:** 1. Status or use exclusion from generally available benefit (Trinity Lutheran; Espinoza; Carson). 2. Parental direction of religious upbringing (Yoder; Mahmoud v. Taylor [VERIFY V-035]). 3. Ministerial exception (Hosanna-Tabor; Our Lady of Guadalupe). 4. Courts examine sincerity, not truth (Ballard).

**Exceptions / defenses:** A state may decline to fund training for the ministry (Locke v. Davey). State "no-aid" constitutional clauses cannot justify exclusions barred by the Free Exercise Clause (Espinoza).

**Authority:** *Wisconsin v. Yoder*, 406 U.S. 205 (1972); *Trinity Lutheran Church of Columbia, Inc. v. Comer*, 582 U.S. 449 (2017); *Espinoza v. Montana Dep't of Revenue*, 591 U.S. 464 (2020); *Carson v. Makin*, 596 U.S. 767 (2022); *Locke v. Davey*, 540 U.S. 712 (2004); *Hosanna-Tabor Evangelical Lutheran Church & School v. EEOC*, 565 U.S. 171 (2012); *Our Lady of Guadalupe School v. Morrissey-Berru*, 591 U.S. 732 (2020); *United States v. Ballard*, 322 U.S. 78 (1944).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. CA note: Cal. Const. art. XVI, § 5 and art. IX, § 8 (no aid to sectarian schools) yield to the federal Free Exercise Clause where they would exclude religious recipients from neutral programs.

**Example:** A state tuition program for rural students pays private schools but excludes "sectarian" ones. Exclusion based on religious use or status triggers strict scrutiny and fails (Carson).

### R-CON-089 — Religious liberty statutes: RFRA and RLUIPA
**Rule (write this):** Under the Religious Freedom Restoration Act (federal government only) and the Religious Land Use and Institutionalized Persons Act (state and local land-use regulation, and persons confined in state or local institutions such as prisons and jails), the government may not substantially burden a person's religious exercise unless it shows that the burden furthers a compelling interest by the least restrictive means.

**Elements:** 1. Sincere religious exercise. 2. Substantial burden. 3. Government's compelling interest applied to the particular claimant. 4. Least restrictive means.

**Exceptions / defenses:** RFRA is unconstitutional as applied to the states (Boerne). Closely held corporations are "persons" under RFRA (Hobby Lobby). Damages: RLUIPA does not authorize damages against a State (Sossamon v. Texas, 563 U.S. 277 (2011)); RFRA permits damages against federal officials in their individual capacities (Tanzin v. Tanvir, 592 U.S. 43 (2020)); whether RLUIPA permits individual-capacity damages was before the Court in Landor v. Louisiana Dep't of Corrections (No. 23-1197, October Term 2025), whose holding is not yet integrated [VERIFY V-982].

**Authority:** 42 U.S.C. § 2000bb-1 (RFRA); 42 U.S.C. §§ 2000cc, 2000cc-1 (RLUIPA); *Gonzales v. O Centro Espírita Beneficente União do Vegetal*, 546 U.S. 418 (2006); *Holt v. Hobbs*, 574 U.S. 352 (2015); *Cutter v. Wilkinson*, 544 U.S. 709 (2005); *Burwell v. Hobby Lobby Stores, Inc.*, 573 U.S. 682 (2014); *City of Boerne v. Flores*, 521 U.S. 507 (1997).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. On an essay about a jail inmate's sacramental tea or religious book, raise RLUIPA alongside the constitutional claim.

**Example:** A state prison bars a Muslim inmate's half-inch beard though other prisons allow it. The state cannot show least restrictive means; RLUIPA violated (Holt).

### R-CON-090 — Establishment Clause: history and tradition; coercion
**Rule (write this):** The Establishment Clause is interpreted by reference to historical practices and understandings; the Lemon test has been abandoned. Government may not coerce anyone to support or participate in religion or its exercise, may not establish an official church, and practices that fit the Nation's historical tradition — such as legislative prayer and longstanding monuments — are permissible.

**Elements:** 1. Historical practice and understanding (Kennedy; Town of Greece). 2. Coercion (direct or indirect pressure on students to participate in prayer: Engel; Lee; Santa Fe). Town of Greece's analysis of whether adults at a town meeting were coerced (Part II-B) was a three-Justice plurality. 3. Longstanding monuments presumed constitutional (American Legion).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Private religious speech by a public employee during a break, not compelled on others, is not government establishment (Kennedy). The continued force of Lemon-era display cases is unsettled: Stone v. Graham (Ten Commandments in classrooms) has not been overruled by the Supreme Court, but the en banc Fifth Circuit has upheld a Texas classroom-display statute, treating Stone as no longer good law, and a certiorari petition was pending on 2026-11-05 [VERIFY V-203] [S-1460] [S-1461].

**Authority:** U.S. Const. amend. I; *Kennedy v. Bremerton School Dist.*, 597 U.S. 507 (2022); *Town of Greece v. Galloway*, 572 U.S. 565 (2014) (Part II-B plurality); *Marsh v. Chambers*, 463 U.S. 783 (1983); *American Legion v. American Humanist Ass'n*, 588 U.S. 29 (2019); *Engel v. Vitale*, 370 U.S. 421 (1962); *Lee v. Weisman*, 505 U.S. 577 (1992); *Santa Fe Indep. School Dist. v. Doe*, 530 U.S. 290 (2000); *Lemon v. Kurtzman*, 403 U.S. 602 (1971) (abandoned).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. On an essay, apply history-and-tradition and coercion; you may note that Lemon was the former test.

**Example:** A public high school invites a rabbi to deliver a prayer at graduation. Students effectively must attend and stand; the practice is coercive and violates the Establishment Clause (Lee).

### R-CON-091 — Establishment Clause: denominational preferences and aid to religion
**Rule (write this):** Government may not prefer one religious denomination over another; a law that discriminates among religions, including by drawing lines based on religious practices or theology, is subject to strict scrutiny. Neutral government aid that reaches religious institutions through the genuine and independent choices of private individuals does not violate the Establishment Clause.

**Elements:** 1. Denominational preference (Larson; Catholic Charities Bureau v. Wisconsin (2025) [VERIFY V-035]). 2. Neutral, private-choice aid (Zelman). 3. Equal access for religious groups to public forums is required, not forbidden (Widmar; Good News Club; Rosenberger).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Property-tax exemptions for religious organizations along with other nonprofits are valid (Walz). Religious accommodation exemptions are permitted if they do not impose significant burdens on third parties.

**Authority:** *Larson v. Valente*, 456 U.S. 228 (1982); *Zelman v. Simmons-Harris*, 536 U.S. 639 (2002); *Walz v. Tax Comm'n*, 397 U.S. 664 (1970); *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263 (1981); *Good News Club v. Milford Central School*, 533 U.S. 98 (2001); *Rosenberger v. Rector & Visitors of Univ. of Va.*, 515 U.S. 819 (1995).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A state denies a Catholic charity an unemployment-tax exemption because it does not proselytize or serve only Catholics, while granting it to groups that do. The theological line discriminates among religions and fails strict scrutiny (Catholic Charities).

### R-CON-092 — Free speech: content-based vs. content-neutral; viewpoint discrimination
**Rule (write this):** A regulation of speech is content-based if, on its face, it targets speech because of its topic, idea or message (including distinctions by function or purpose that act as proxies for subject matter), or if, though facially neutral, it cannot be justified without reference to content or was adopted because of disagreement with the message; a rule is not content-based merely because an official must read the speech to apply it. Viewpoint discrimination is the most egregious form of content discrimination and is presumptively invalid in every forum. Content-neutral regulations get intermediate scrutiny.

**Elements:** 1. Speech (or expressive conduct). 2. Government action. 3. Protected category? 4. Content-based (strict) or content-neutral (intermediate). 5. Forum and speaker context.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Location-based distinctions, such as on-premises vs. off-premises sign rules, are content-neutral even though an official must read a sign to know whether it advertises something on site; they get intermediate scrutiny unless a content-based purpose or justification is shown (City of Austin, remanding for application of that standard). Laws aimed at secondary effects of adult businesses are treated as content-neutral (Renton).

**Authority:** U.S. Const. amend. I; *Police Dep't of Chicago v. Mosley*, 408 U.S. 92 (1972); *Reed v. Town of Gilbert*, 576 U.S. 155 (2015); *City of Austin v. Reagan National Advertising of Austin, LLC*, 596 U.S. 61 (2022); *Rosenberger*, 515 U.S. 819; *Matal v. Tam*, 582 U.S. 218 (2017); *Iancu v. Brunetti*, 588 U.S. 388 (2019); *City of Renton v. Playtime Theatres, Inc.*, 475 U.S. 41 (1986).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A town's sign code lets political signs stay up longer and larger than directional signs for church events. Distinctions by function and message are content-based; strict scrutiny; invalid (Reed).

### R-CON-093 — Strict scrutiny for content-based laws; compelled speech
**Rule (write this):** A content-based restriction on protected speech is valid only if the government proves it is narrowly tailored (the least restrictive means) to serve a compelling interest. The government likewise may not compel a person to express or host a message the person does not wish to convey.

**Elements:** 1. Compelling interest. 2. Necessity and least restrictive means; not over- or under-inclusive. 3. Compelled speech: forced pledge (Barnette), license-plate motto (Wooley), parade inclusion (Hurley), custom expressive services (303 Creative [VERIFY V-035]), mandated notices by pregnancy centers (NIFLA).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Rare survivors: campaign-free zone around polling places (Burson, plurality opinion), judicial-candidate solicitation ban (Williams-Yulee), material support to terrorist groups (Holder v. HLP). A viewpoint-based ban on talk therapy by licensed counselors gets strict scrutiny (Chiles v. Salazar (2026) [S-306]). Age verification for online sexual material obscene for minors gets intermediate scrutiny because it only incidentally burdens adults (Free Speech Coalition v. Paxton (2025) [VERIFY V-035]).

**Authority:** *Brown v. Entertainment Merchants Ass'n*, 564 U.S. 786 (2011); *Burson v. Freeman*, 504 U.S. 191 (1992) (plurality opinion); *Williams-Yulee v. Florida Bar*, 575 U.S. 433 (2015); *West Virginia State Bd. of Educ. v. Barnette*, 319 U.S. 624 (1943); *Wooley v. Maynard*, 430 U.S. 705 (1977); *Hurley v. Irish-American Gay, Lesbian & Bisexual Group of Boston*, 515 U.S. 557 (1995); *NIFLA v. Becerra*, 585 U.S. 755 (2018).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** California banned sale of violent video games to minors. Content-based; no compelling evidence of harm and the law was under- and over-inclusive; invalid (Brown).

### R-CON-094 — Content-neutral regulation: time, place and manner
**Rule (write this):** In a public forum, the government may impose content-neutral time, place and manner restrictions if they are narrowly tailored to serve a significant government interest and leave open ample alternative channels for communication; narrow tailoring here does not require the least restrictive means, but the regulation may not burden substantially more speech than necessary.

**Elements:** 1. Content-neutral. 2. Significant interest (traffic, noise, aesthetics, safety, privacy of home). 3. Narrow tailoring (no substantially greater burden; consider obvious alternatives — McCullen). 4. Ample alternatives.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Total bans on a medium of expression (residential yard signs) often fail (Ladue). Targeted residential picketing bans are valid (Frisby).

**Authority:** *Ward v. Rock Against Racism*, 491 U.S. 781 (1989); *Clark v. Community for Creative Non-Violence*, 468 U.S. 288 (1984); *McCullen v. Coakley*, 573 U.S. 464 (2014); *Frisby v. Schultz*, 487 U.S. 474 (1988); *City of Ladue v. Gilleo*, 512 U.S. 43 (1994); *Members of City Council v. Taxpayers for Vincent*, 466 U.S. 789 (1984).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A city requires park concerts to use the city's sound technician to control volume. Content-neutral, serves noise control, and leaves alternatives; valid (Ward).

### R-CON-095 — Public forum doctrine
**Rule (write this):** In traditional public forums (streets, sidewalks, parks) and designated public forums, content-based restrictions get strict scrutiny and content-neutral restrictions must satisfy the time, place and manner test; in limited public forums and nonpublic forums, restrictions are valid if reasonable in light of the forum's purpose and viewpoint-neutral.

**Elements:** 1. Classify the forum. 2. Apply the matching test. 3. Reasonableness requires objective, workable standards (Mansky).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Examples of nonpublic forums: airport terminals (ISKCON), military bases, government workplace charity drives (Cornelius), polling places (Mansky), mailboxes. A public university's all-comers policy for recognized student groups is a reasonable, viewpoint-neutral limited-forum rule (CLS v. Martinez).

**Authority:** *Hague v. CIO*, 307 U.S. 496 (1939) (plurality opinion); *Perry Educ. Ass'n v. Perry Local Educators' Ass'n*, 460 U.S. 37 (1983); *Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Def. & Educ. Fund*, 473 U.S. 788 (1985); *Int'l Soc'y for Krishna Consciousness v. Lee*, 505 U.S. 672 (1992); *Christian Legal Soc'y v. Martinez*, 561 U.S. 661 (2010); *Minnesota Voters Alliance v. Mansky*, 585 U.S. 1 (2018).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A city library bulletin board open to community groups refuses a flyer because of its political message. Even if a limited forum, rejecting a flyer for its viewpoint is unconstitutional.

### R-CON-096 — Government speech
**Rule (write this):** When the government speaks for itself, the Free Speech Clause does not require viewpoint neutrality; courts decide whether expression is government speech by looking at history, whether the public would perceive the government as speaking, and the extent of the government's control over the messages.

**Elements:** History; public perception; active control (Summum; Walker; Shurtleff).

**Exceptions / defenses:** A program that lets private groups raise flags at city hall without meaningful city control is not government speech, so excluding a religious flag was viewpoint discrimination (Shurtleff). Trademarks are private speech (Tam). The Establishment Clause still limits government religious speech.

**Authority:** *Pleasant Grove City v. Summum*, 555 U.S. 460 (2009); *Walker v. Texas Div., Sons of Confederate Veterans, Inc.*, 576 U.S. 200 (2015); *Shurtleff v. City of Boston*, 596 U.S. 243 (2022); *Matal v. Tam*, 582 U.S. 218 (2017).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A city accepts some donated park monuments and rejects others. Permanent monuments are government speech; the city may choose (Summum).

### R-CON-097 — Incitement
**Rule (write this):** Advocacy of force or law violation may be punished only if it is directed to inciting or producing imminent lawless action and is likely to incite or produce such action.

**Elements:** 1. Intent (directed to). 2. Imminence. 3. Likelihood.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Abstract advocacy and advocacy of illegal action at some indefinite future time are protected (Hess v. Indiana).

**Authority:** *Brandenburg v. Ohio*, 395 U.S. 444 (1969); *Hess v. Indiana*, 414 U.S. 105 (1973); *NAACP v. Claiborne Hardware Co.*, 458 U.S. 886 (1982).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** At a rally, a speaker says "we'll take the street again later." Not directed to imminent lawless action; protected (Hess).

### R-CON-098 — True threats and fighting words
**Rule (write this):** True threats — serious expressions of intent to commit unlawful violence against a particular person or group — are unprotected, but the speaker must have at least recklessly disregarded a substantial risk that the communication would be viewed as threatening violence. Fighting words — personally abusive words, directed face-to-face, likely to provoke an immediate violent reaction — are unprotected, but laws regulating them must be narrowly drawn and may not discriminate by viewpoint within the category.

**Elements:** True threat: serious threat + recklessness mental state (Counterman [VERIFY V-035]). Fighting words: face-to-face, personally abusive, likely to provoke violence.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Cross burning with intent to intimidate may be banned, but a statute may not treat the act itself as prima facie evidence of intent (Black; plurality opinion on this point, with three more Justices finding the statute invalid). Offensive words not directed at a person are protected (Cohen). A ban on fighting words limited to disfavored topics is invalid (R.A.V.).

**Authority:** *Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire*, 315 U.S. 568 (1942); *Cohen v. California*, 403 U.S. 15 (1971); *R.A.V. v. City of St. Paul*, 505 U.S. 377 (1992); *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343 (2003); *Gooding v. Wilson*, 405 U.S. 518 (1972); *Counterman v. Colorado* (2023) [VERIFY V-035].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A man wears a jacket reading a profanity about the draft in a courthouse corridor. Not directed at anyone; not fighting words; protected (Cohen).

### R-CON-099 — Obscenity and child pornography
**Rule (write this):** Obscenity is unprotected: material is obscene if (1) the average person, applying contemporary community standards, would find that the work taken as a whole appeals to the prurient interest, (2) it depicts sexual conduct specifically defined by law in a patently offensive way, and (3) taken as a whole it lacks serious literary, artistic, political or scientific value, judged by a national reasonable-person standard. Child pornography involving real minors is unprotected without regard to the Miller test.

**Elements:** Miller three prongs; value prong is national (Pope). Ferber: real children.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Private possession of obscenity at home is protected (Stanley), but not possession of child pornography (Osborne). Virtual images not involving real children are protected unless obscene (Ashcroft v. Free Speech Coalition). Material obscene for minors may be restricted for minors.

**Authority:** *Miller v. California*, 413 U.S. 15 (1973); *Pope v. Illinois*, 481 U.S. 497 (1987); *Stanley v. Georgia*, 394 U.S. 557 (1969); *New York v. Ferber*, 458 U.S. 747 (1982); *Osborne v. Ohio*, 495 U.S. 103 (1990); *Ashcroft v. Free Speech Coalition*, 535 U.S. 234 (2002).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A prosecutor relies on local juror attitudes to argue a novel lacks literary value. The value prong uses a national reasonable-person standard, not local community standards (Pope).

### R-CON-100 — Defamation and related torts: constitutional limits
**Rule (write this):** A public official or public figure suing for defamation must prove by clear and convincing evidence that the defendant made a false statement of fact with actual malice — knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard for the truth. A private figure on a matter of public concern must prove at least negligence and needs actual malice for presumed or punitive damages; on purely private matters, states may allow presumed and punitive damages without actual malice.

**Elements:** Status of plaintiff; public concern; falsity; fault; damages.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Public figures cannot recover for intentional infliction of emotional distress from parody without a false statement of fact made with actual malice (Hustler). Speech on public issues at a public place cannot ground tort liability for its outrageousness (Snyder). Statements must be provably false; there is no wholesale "opinion" privilege (Milkovich). The private-concern rule rests on Dun & Bradstreet, a plurality opinion joined in the judgment by two more Justices; extension of actual malice to public figures came from Curtis Publishing, which had no majority opinion.

**Authority:** *New York Times Co. v. Sullivan*, 376 U.S. 254 (1964); *Curtis Publishing Co. v. Butts*, 388 U.S. 130 (1967) (no majority opinion); *Gertz v. Robert Welch, Inc.*, 418 U.S. 323 (1974); *Dun & Bradstreet, Inc. v. Greenmoss Builders, Inc.*, 472 U.S. 749 (1985) (plurality opinion); *Hustler Magazine, Inc. v. Falwell*, 485 U.S. 46 (1988); *Milkovich v. Lorain Journal Co.*, 497 U.S. 1 (1990); *Snyder v. Phelps*, 562 U.S. 443 (2011).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule. On a Torts crossover, use these limits as a defense element.

**Example:** A newspaper prints an ad with minor false statements about a city commissioner's police department. Without actual malice, no liability (New York Times v. Sullivan).

### R-CON-101 — Commercial speech
**Rule (write this):** Commercial speech that concerns lawful activity and is not misleading may be regulated only if the government asserts a substantial interest, the regulation directly advances that interest, and it is not more extensive than necessary (a reasonable fit, not the least restrictive means). False, misleading or unlawful-activity advertising is unprotected, and required disclosures of purely factual, uncontroversial information reasonably related to preventing deception are valid.

**Elements:** Commercial speech is speech that does no more than propose a commercial transaction (Virginia Pharmacy); speech mixing commercial and noncommercial elements is judged by its advertising format, reference to a specific product and economic motive taken together (*Bolger v. Youngs Drug Products Corp.*, 463 U.S. 60 (1983)). Central Hudson four steps; Fox reasonable fit; Zauderer disclosures.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Lawyer advertising is protected (Bates), but in-person solicitation for pecuniary gain may be banned (Ohralik), while nonprofit civil-rights solicitation is protected (Primus); a 30-day ban on targeted mail to accident victims was upheld (Went For It). Content- and speaker-based burdens on commercial information get heightened scrutiny (Sorrell).

**Authority:** *Central Hudson Gas & Elec. Corp. v. Public Serv. Comm'n*, 447 U.S. 557 (1980); *Virginia State Bd. of Pharmacy v. Virginia Citizens Consumer Council, Inc.*, 425 U.S. 748 (1976); *Board of Trustees of SUNY v. Fox*, 492 U.S. 469 (1989); *44 Liquormart, Inc. v. Rhode Island*, 517 U.S. 484 (1996) (unanimous judgment; key parts of the principal opinion are plurality); *Zauderer v. Office of Disciplinary Counsel*, 471 U.S. 626 (1985); *Bates v. State Bar of Ariz.*, 433 U.S. 350 (1977); *Ohralik v. Ohio State Bar Ass'n*, 436 U.S. 447 (1978); *Florida Bar v. Went For It, Inc.*, 515 U.S. 618 (1995); *Sorrell v. IMS Health Inc.*, 564 U.S. 552 (2011).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A state bans all retail liquor-price advertising to promote temperance. The ban does not directly and materially advance temperance and less restrictive options exist; invalid (44 Liquormart; these reasons come from plurality parts of the principal opinion).

### R-CON-102 — Public school student speech
**Rule (write this):** Public school students do not shed their free-speech rights at the schoolhouse gate; a school may restrict student speech only if it reasonably forecasts substantial disruption of school activities or invasion of others' rights. Schools may also restrict lewd or vulgar speech at school events, school-sponsored speech for legitimate pedagogical reasons, and speech reasonably viewed as promoting illegal drug use; their power over off-campus speech is diminished.

**Elements:** Tinker (forecast of substantial disruption based on facts, not undifferentiated fear); Fraser; Hazelwood; Morse; Mahanoy.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Off-campus speech may still be regulated for serious bullying, threats or breaches of school security (Mahanoy). A school rule must also satisfy vagueness and overbreadth limits.

**Authority:** *Tinker v. Des Moines Indep. Community School Dist.*, 393 U.S. 503 (1969); *Bethel School Dist. No. 403 v. Fraser*, 478 U.S. 675 (1986); *Hazelwood School Dist. v. Kuhlmeier*, 484 U.S. 260 (1988); *Morse v. Frederick*, 551 U.S. 393 (2007); *Mahanoy Area School Dist. v. B.L.*, 594 U.S. 180 (2021).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A school suspends a student for a visible dove tattoo under a ban on "gang-related symbols," with no evidence the dove is used by gangs at the school. Without facts supporting a forecast of substantial disruption, Tinker likely protects the student; the undefined term also raises vagueness.

### R-CON-103 — Public employee speech and patronage
**Rule (write this):** A public employee's speech is protected only if the employee spoke as a citizen, not pursuant to official duties, on a matter of public concern; if so, the court balances the employee's interest in commenting against the government's interest as employer in efficient public services, and the employer may still prevail by showing it would have taken the same action anyway. Government may not hire, fire or promote based on political affiliation unless affiliation is an appropriate requirement for the job.

**Elements:** 1. Citizen vs. official duties (Garcetti). 2. Public concern (content, form, context — Connick). 3. Pickering balance. 4. Causation (Mt. Healthy). Patronage: Elrod (plurality opinion); Branti; Rutan.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Sworn testimony outside ordinary job duties is citizen speech (Lane). A coach's private, quiet prayer after a game was not government speech (Kennedy). Compelled agency fees from nonmembers violate the First Amendment (Janus).

**Authority:** *Pickering v. Board of Educ.*, 391 U.S. 563 (1968); *Connick v. Myers*, 461 U.S. 138 (1983); *Garcetti v. Ceballos*, 547 U.S. 410 (2006); *Lane v. Franks*, 573 U.S. 228 (2014); *Rankin v. McPherson*, 483 U.S. 378 (1987); *Mt. Healthy City School Dist. Bd. of Educ. v. Doyle*, 429 U.S. 274 (1977); *Elrod v. Burns*, 427 U.S. 347 (1976) (plurality opinion); *Branti v. Finkel*, 445 U.S. 507 (1980); *Rutan v. Republican Party of Ill.*, 497 U.S. 62 (1990); *Janus v. AFSCME*, 585 U.S. 878 (2018).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A teacher writes a letter to the local paper criticizing the school board's budget. Citizen speech on public concern; no evidence of disruption; firing violates the First Amendment (Pickering).

### R-CON-104 — Licenses, benefits, bar admission and loyalty oaths
**Rule (write this):** Government may not deny a license, public employment or other benefit because of a person's protected speech or association, may not punish mere membership in an organization absent knowing membership with specific intent to further its illegal aims, and may require only oaths to support the Constitution and to oppose its overthrow by unlawful means; vague or overbroad oaths are invalid.

**Elements:** Benefits (Perry; Speiser); bar admission inquiries limited to knowing membership with specific intent (Baird, plurality opinion; Wadmond); oaths (Cole; Keyishian; Elfbrandt).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Mandatory state-bar dues may fund only activities germane to regulating the profession (Keller, involving the State Bar of California).

**Authority:** *Baird v. State Bar of Ariz.*, 401 U.S. 1 (1971) (plurality opinion); *Law Students Civil Rights Research Council, Inc. v. Wadmond*, 401 U.S. 154 (1971); *Cole v. Richardson*, 405 U.S. 676 (1972); *Keyishian v. Board of Regents*, 385 U.S. 589 (1967); *Elfbrandt v. Russell*, 384 U.S. 11 (1966); *Keller v. State Bar of Cal.*, 496 U.S. 1 (1990).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A state bar denies admission because the applicant refuses to say whether she was ever a member of any organization advocating overthrow of the government. The broad membership inquiry is invalid (Baird, plurality).

### R-CON-105 — Expressive conduct
**Rule (write this):** Conduct is expressive if the actor intends to convey a particularized message and the audience is likely to understand it. A regulation of expressive conduct is valid if it is within the government's power, furthers an important or substantial interest unrelated to the suppression of expression, and imposes an incidental restriction on expression no greater than essential to that interest; if the interest is related to the message, strict scrutiny applies.

**Elements:** Spence test; O'Brien four-part test.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Flag burning cannot be banned because of its message (Johnson; Eichman). Banning cross burning done with intent to intimidate is valid (Black). Public-indecency laws applied to nude dancing are upheld (Barnes; Pap's), though neither case produced a majority rationale.

**Authority:** *United States v. O'Brien*, 391 U.S. 367 (1968); *Spence v. Washington*, 418 U.S. 405 (1974); *Texas v. Johnson*, 491 U.S. 397 (1989); *United States v. Eichman*, 496 U.S. 310 (1990); *Barnes v. Glen Theatre, Inc.*, 501 U.S. 560 (1991) (plurality opinion); *City of Erie v. Pap's A.M.*, 529 U.S. 277 (2000) (plurality opinion); *Rumsfeld v. FAIR*, 547 U.S. 47 (2006).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A protester burns a draft card in violation of a law requiring cardholders to keep them. The law serves the draft's administration, unrelated to expression; valid (O'Brien).

### R-CON-106 — Prior restraints
**Rule (write this):** A prior restraint — a government order or licensing scheme that suppresses speech before it occurs — bears a heavy presumption against validity and is justified only in exceptional cases, such as a showing of grave, direct, immediate and irreparable harm. A permit scheme for speech is valid only if it serves an important purpose, is content-neutral, and uses narrow, objective, definite standards that leave officials no unbridled discretion; content-based licensing also needs prompt judicial review with the burden on the censor.

**Elements:** Injunctions against publication (Near; Pentagon Papers); trial gag orders on the press (Nebraska Press); licensing standards (Lakewood; Forsyth; Shuttlesworth); Freedman procedures.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A person must obey a court injunction until it is vacated, even if unconstitutional (collateral bar rule, Walker v. Birmingham), but may ignore a facially void licensing statute and challenge it when prosecuted (Shuttlesworth). Content-neutral park-permit schemes need not follow Freedman (Thomas v. Chicago Park District).

**Authority:** *Near v. Minnesota*, 283 U.S. 697 (1931); *New York Times Co. v. United States*, 403 U.S. 713 (1971); *Nebraska Press Ass'n v. Stuart*, 427 U.S. 539 (1976); *Freedman v. Maryland*, 380 U.S. 51 (1965); *City of Lakewood v. Plain Dealer Publishing Co.*, 486 U.S. 750 (1988); *Forsyth County v. Nationalist Movement*, 505 U.S. 123 (1992); *Shuttlesworth v. City of Birmingham*, 394 U.S. 147 (1969); *Walker v. City of Birmingham*, 388 U.S. 307 (1967); *Thomas v. Chicago Park Dist.*, 534 U.S. 316 (2002).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A county sets parade-permit fees by how much police protection the speaker's message is expected to require. Fee varies with content and hostility; invalid (Forsyth).

### R-CON-107 — Vagueness
**Rule (write this):** A law is void for vagueness under due process if it fails to give a person of ordinary intelligence fair notice of what is prohibited or if it invites arbitrary and discriminatory enforcement; the standard is applied most strictly to laws that reach speech.

**Elements:** 1. Fair notice. 2. Minimal enforcement guidelines.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Some imprecision is tolerated; a limiting construction can save a law (Grayned).

**Authority:** *Grayned v. City of Rockford*, 408 U.S. 104 (1972); *Coates v. City of Cincinnati*, 402 U.S. 611 (1971); *Kolender v. Lawson*, 461 U.S. 352 (1983); *City of Chicago v. Morales*, 527 U.S. 41 (1999); *Johnson v. United States*, 576 U.S. 591 (2015).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A city ordinance orders "criminal street gang members" loitering with others to disperse, defining loitering as remaining "with no apparent purpose." It gives police too much discretion; void (Morales).

### R-CON-108 — Overbreadth
**Rule (write this):** A law regulating speech is facially invalid if it prohibits a substantial amount of protected speech relative to its plainly legitimate sweep, and a litigant whose own speech is unprotected may raise the overbreadth claim on behalf of others.

**Elements:** 1. Construe the statute. 2. Compare protected applications with legitimate ones; overbreadth must be substantial (Broadrick; Stevens; Williams). 3. Narrowing construction.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Not available for commercial speech (Bates). Facial challenges require analysis of the law's full range of applications (Moody v. NetChoice [VERIFY V-035]). A criminal "encourages or induces" provision read as solicitation and facilitation was not overbroad (United States v. Hansen (2023) [VERIFY V-200]).

**Authority:** *Broadrick v. Oklahoma*, 413 U.S. 601 (1973); *United States v. Stevens*, 559 U.S. 460 (2010); *United States v. Williams*, 553 U.S. 285 (2008); *Board of Airport Comm'rs v. Jews for Jesus, Inc.*, 482 U.S. 569 (1987).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** An airport bans "all First Amendment activities" in its terminal. The ban reaches virtually all speech; facially overbroad (Jews for Jesus).

### R-CON-109 — Freedom of the press
**Rule (write this):** The press has no greater First Amendment rights than the public generally: it must obey generally applicable laws and has no constitutional privilege to refuse grand-jury subpoenas, but the government may not single out the press or particular publications for special burdens, may not punish the publication of truthful, lawfully obtained information on matters of public significance absent a state interest of the highest order, and must keep criminal trials and related proceedings presumptively open.

**Elements:** Equal treatment (Branzburg; Cohen v. Cowles); differential taxation (Minneapolis Star; Arkansas Writers'); truthful publication (Daily Mail; Florida Star; Bartnicki); trial access (Richmond Newspapers, plurality opinion; Globe; Press-Enterprise).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Closure requires an overriding interest, specific findings and narrow tailoring. Broadcast regulation is more deferential (Red Lion; Pacifica); a right-of-reply law for newspapers is invalid (Tornillo).

**Authority:** *Branzburg v. Hayes*, 408 U.S. 665 (1972); *Minneapolis Star & Tribune Co. v. Minn. Comm'r of Revenue*, 460 U.S. 575 (1983); *Smith v. Daily Mail Publishing Co.*, 443 U.S. 97 (1979); *Florida Star v. B.J.F.*, 491 U.S. 524 (1989); *Richmond Newspapers, Inc. v. Virginia*, 448 U.S. 555 (1980) (plurality opinion); *Globe Newspaper Co. v. Superior Court*, 457 U.S. 596 (1982); *Press-Enterprise Co. v. Superior Court*, 478 U.S. 1 (1986); *Miami Herald Publishing Co. v. Tornillo*, 418 U.S. 241 (1974).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same federal rule. CA note: California's shield law (Cal. Const. art. I, § 2(b); Evid. Code § 1070) gives reporters protection from contempt.

**Example:** A state statute mandates closing the courtroom whenever a minor sex-crime victim testifies. A mandatory rule without case-by-case findings is invalid (Globe).

### R-CON-110 — Freedom of association: membership, disclosure and expressive association
**Rule (write this):** The First Amendment protects the right to associate for expressive purposes. Government may not punish membership in a group unless the member knows of its illegal aims and specifically intends to further them; compelled disclosure of membership or donors is subject to exacting scrutiny, requiring a substantial relation to a sufficiently important interest and narrow tailoring; and laws forcing an expressive association to accept members who would significantly impair its message must survive strict scrutiny.

**Elements:** Membership penalties (Scales); disclosure (NAACP v. Alabama; AFP v. Bonta, whose general statement that exacting scrutiny requires narrow tailoring was in a three-Justice part of the opinion, though the majority applied narrow tailoring to strike California's rule); expressive association (Roberts; Dale); intimate association (small, selective family-like groups).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Antidiscrimination laws applied to large, unselective organizations do not significantly burden expression (Roberts; Rotary). A state donor-information subpoena inflicts present associational injury (First Choice (2026) [S-313]).

**Authority:** *NAACP v. Alabama ex rel. Patterson*, 357 U.S. 449 (1958); *Scales v. United States*, 367 U.S. 203 (1961); *Roberts v. United States Jaycees*, 468 U.S. 609 (1984); *Board of Directors of Rotary Int'l v. Rotary Club of Duarte*, 481 U.S. 537 (1987); *Boy Scouts of America v. Dale*, 530 U.S. 640 (2000); *Americans for Prosperity Found. v. Bonta*, 594 U.S. 595 (2021); *California Democratic Party v. Jones*, 530 U.S. 567 (2000).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** California required charities to give the Attorney General their major-donor lists, though the office rarely used them. Not narrowly tailored; invalid (AFP v. Bonta).

### R-CON-111 — Campaign finance
**Rule (write this):** Limits on campaign expenditures, including independent expenditures by corporations and unions, get strict scrutiny and are generally invalid; limits on contributions to candidates are valid if closely drawn to the important interest in preventing quid pro quo corruption or its appearance; disclosure requirements get exacting scrutiny and are generally upheld.

**Elements:** Expenditures (Buckley; Citizens United); contributions (Buckley; McCutcheon, plurality opinion: aggregate limits invalid); only quid pro quo corruption counts (Cruz).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Federal limits on political parties' coordinated expenditures with candidates violate the First Amendment (NRSC v. FEC (2026) [S-312]).

**Authority:** *Buckley v. Valeo*, 424 U.S. 1 (1976); *Citizens United v. FEC*, 558 U.S. 310 (2010); *McCutcheon v. FEC*, 572 U.S. 185 (2014) (plurality opinion); *FEC v. Ted Cruz for Senate*, 596 U.S. 289 (2022); *Doe v. Reed*, 561 U.S. 186 (2010).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A state bars corporations from spending general-treasury funds on independent ads about candidates. Expenditure limit; strict scrutiny; invalid (Citizens United).

### R-CON-112 — Government pressure on private speakers and platforms
**Rule (write this):** A government official may not coerce a private party into punishing or suppressing a third party's speech on the government's behalf; and when private platforms curate and moderate third-party content to produce their own compilations, that editorial activity is itself protected expression.

**Elements:** Coercion test: would the official's communication reasonably be understood as a threat of adverse action (Bantam Books; NRA v. Vullo [VERIFY V-035]). Editorial discretion (Tornillo; Hurley; Moody v. NetChoice [VERIFY V-035]).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Government may try to persuade and may speak for itself. Plaintiffs still need standing traceable to the government's conduct (Murthy [VERIFY V-035]).

**Authority:** *Bantam Books, Inc. v. Sullivan*, 372 U.S. 58 (1963); *NRA v. Vullo* (2024) [VERIFY V-035]; *Moody v. NetChoice, LLC* (2024) [VERIFY V-035].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A state insurance regulator privately tells insurers she will go easy on their other violations if they stop doing business with an advocacy group because of its views. Coercion of third parties to punish speech; states a First Amendment claim (Vullo).

## Amendments Eleven through Fifteen and other guarantees

### R-CON-113 — Thirteenth Amendment: slavery and involuntary servitude
**Rule (write this):** The Thirteenth Amendment prohibits slavery and involuntary servitude, except as punishment for a crime of which the party has been duly convicted; it applies directly to both government and private conduct. Involuntary servitude means compelled labor through physical or legal coercion, but traditional civic duties such as military service, jury service and certain public-works obligations are not involuntary servitude.

**Elements:** 1. Labor or service compelled. 2. By physical force or legal coercion (Kozminski; psychological coercion alone is not enough). 3. Not a recognized civic duty or criminal punishment.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Military draft (Selective Draft Law Cases); compulsory road work (Butler v. Perry).

**Authority:** U.S. Const. amend. XIII, § 1; *United States v. Kozminski*, 487 U.S. 931 (1988); *Butler v. Perry*, 240 U.S. 328 (1916); *Selective Draft Law Cases*, 245 U.S. 366 (1918).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A statute drafts teenage dropouts into a two-year forestry corps under threat of juvenile detention. They are compelled by legal coercion to labor without a criminal conviction; the state will argue a civic-duty analogy to the draft, but compelled work for a specific age and status group is a strong Thirteenth Amendment argument.

### R-CON-114 — Citizenship Clause
**Rule (write this):** All persons born or naturalized in the United States and subject to its jurisdiction are citizens of the United States and of the state where they reside. Children born in the United States are citizens regardless of their parents' immigration status, apart from narrow traditional exceptions such as children of foreign diplomats; Congress cannot take away citizenship without the citizen's voluntary relinquishment.

**Elements:** 1. Birth or naturalization in the U.S. 2. "Subject to the jurisdiction thereof" (ordinary legal sense).

**Exceptions / defenses:** The executive order limiting birthright citizenship for children of unlawfully or temporarily present parents is invalid (Trump v. Barbara (2026) [S-304] [S-305]).

**Authority:** U.S. Const. amend. XIV, § 1; *United States v. Wong Kim Ark*, 169 U.S. 649 (1898); *Afroyim v. Rusk*, 387 U.S. 253 (1967); *Vance v. Terrazas*, 444 U.S. 252 (1980).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A child is born in San Diego to parents on temporary tourist visas. The child is a U.S. citizen at birth (Wong Kim Ark; Barbara).

### R-CON-115 — Fifteenth Amendment: race and voting
**Rule (write this):** The Fifteenth Amendment forbids the United States and the states from denying or abridging the right to vote on account of race, color or previous condition of servitude; a violation requires purposeful discrimination, and it reaches ancestry used as a proxy for race and private primaries that function as the real election.

**Elements:** 1. Voting right. 2. Denied or abridged. 3. On account of race (purpose).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Facially neutral voting rules with disparate impact alone do not violate the Amendment (Mobile v. Bolden plurality); statutory claims may lie under the VRA.

**Authority:** U.S. Const. amend. XV, § 1; *Terry v. Adams*, 345 U.S. 461 (1953) (no majority opinion); *Rice v. Cayetano*, 528 U.S. 495 (2000); *City of Mobile v. Bolden*, 446 U.S. 55 (1980) (plurality opinion).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A state limits voting for trustees of a native-heritage agency to descendants of pre-contact inhabitants. Ancestry is a proxy for race; violates the Fifteenth Amendment (Rice).

### R-CON-116 — Prisoners' constitutional rights
**Rule (write this):** A prison regulation that impinges on inmates' constitutional rights is valid if it is reasonably related to legitimate penological interests, considering (1) a valid, rational connection to the interest, (2) alternative means for the inmate to exercise the right, (3) the impact of accommodation on guards, other inmates and resources, and (4) the absence of ready alternatives. Racial classifications in prisons still receive strict scrutiny, and statutory claims under RLUIPA receive strict scrutiny.

**Elements:** Turner factors; O'Lone applied them to religious services.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Johnson v. California (race); Holt v. Hobbs (RLUIPA). Establishment Clause limits still apply to jail practices.

**Authority:** *Turner v. Safley*, 482 U.S. 78 (1987); *O'Lone v. Estate of Shabazz*, 482 U.S. 342 (1987); *Johnson v. California*, 543 U.S. 499 (2005); *Holt v. Hobbs*, 574 U.S. 352 (2015).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule.

**Example:** A jail bans an inmate's religious book but allows other books of similar size. The ban has no rational connection to security as applied and fails Turner; it likely also violates RLUIPA.

### R-CON-117 — Enforcing constitutional rights: § 1983, Bivens and relief
**Rule (write this):** Section 1983 gives a cause of action for damages and injunctive relief against any person who, under color of state law, deprives another of federal rights; states and state officials sued in their official capacity for damages are not "persons," and local governments are liable only for their own policies or customs. A plaintiff seeking a TRO or preliminary injunction must show likely success on the merits, likely irreparable harm without relief, a favorable balance of equities and that relief serves the public interest; federal courts may not issue universal injunctions beyond what is needed to give the plaintiffs complete relief.

**Elements:** § 1983: person, color of state law, deprivation, causation. Injunction: Winter factors. Declaratory relief: actual controversy (28 U.S.C. § 2201).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Qualified and absolute immunities (R-CON-043). Local governments have no qualified immunity (*Owen*) and owe no punitive damages under § 1983 (*City of Newport v. Fact Concerts*); an official-capacity suit against a local officer is a suit against the entity (*Kentucky v. Graham*); a single decision by an official with final policymaking authority can be municipal policy (*Pembaur*). A prevailing plaintiff may recover a reasonable attorney's fee (42 U.S.C. § 1988(b)); prevailing requires a judgment or court-ordered change in the parties' legal relationship, not the defendant's voluntary change of conduct (*Buckhannon*), and a preliminary injunction alone does not make a plaintiff a prevailing party (*Lackey v. Stinnie* (2025)) [VERIFY V-035]. Bivens damages against federal officers will not be extended to new contexts if there is any reason to think Congress is better suited to create a remedy (Egbert). Loss of First Amendment freedoms, even briefly, is irreparable injury (Elrod plurality). Trump v. CASA (2025) [VERIFY V-035].

**Authority:** 42 U.S.C. § 1983; *Monell v. Department of Social Servs.*, 436 U.S. 658 (1978); *Will v. Michigan Dep't of State Police*, 491 U.S. 58 (1989); *Owen v. City of Independence*, 445 U.S. 622 (1980); *City of Newport v. Fact Concerts, Inc.*, 453 U.S. 247 (1981); *Kentucky v. Graham*, 473 U.S. 159 (1985); *Pembaur v. City of Cincinnati*, 475 U.S. 469 (1986); 42 U.S.C. § 1988(b); *Buckhannon Board & Care Home, Inc. v. West Virginia Dep't of Health & Human Resources*, 532 U.S. 598 (2001); *Bivens v. Six Unknown Named Agents*, 403 U.S. 388 (1971); *Egbert v. Boule*, 596 U.S. 482 (2022); *Winter v. NRDC*, 555 U.S. 7 (2008); *Elrod v. Burns*, 427 U.S. 347 (1976).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** same rule; on a Con Law + Remedies essay, pair this with the Remedies TRO/PI template.

**Example:** A church seeks a TRO against a candle ban two days before a holy-day service. Likely success (non-neutral law), irreparable harm (loss of free exercise), equities and public interest favor relief; the TRO should issue against the city officials.

## California constitutional supplement

### R-CON-118 — California free speech on private shopping-center property (Pruneyard)
**Rule (write this):** The liberty-of-speech and petition clauses of the California Constitution (art. I, §§ 2, 3) protect speech and petitioning, reasonably exercised, in privately owned shopping centers that are open to the public, subject to the owner's reasonable time, place and manner rules (*Robins v. Pruneyard Shopping Center*). A state may grant this broader right: requiring the owner to allow such speech is neither a taking of the owner's property nor a violation of the owner's own First Amendment rights (*PruneYard Shopping Center v. Robins*).

**Elements:** 1. Privately owned property open to the public that serves as a public gathering place: the common areas of a shopping center (Robins). 2. Speech or petitioning, reasonably exercised (leafleting, gathering signatures, peaceful picketing). 3. The owner may impose only reasonable time, place and manner rules; a rule that bars speech because of its content, such as a ban on urging customers to boycott a mall store, gets strict scrutiny and fails (Fashion Valley).

**Exceptions / defenses:** The right does not reach the private sidewalk and entrance area of an individual retail store, even one inside a shopping center: an area is a public forum under Pruneyard only if it is designed and furnished to induce shoppers to congregate for entertainment, relaxation or conversation (Ralphs) [S-1456] [S-1457]. Courts of Appeal have likewise held that stand-alone stores are not Pruneyard forums (e.g., *Trader Joe's Co. v. Progressive Campaigns, Inc.* (1999) 73 Cal.App.4th 425) [VERIFY V-980]. There is **no** federal right to speak on private shopping-center property, because the owner is not a state actor (*Hudgens*; *Lloyd Corp.*; R-CON-054).

**Authority:** Cal. Const. art. I, §§ 2(a), 3 [S-320]; *Robins v. Pruneyard Shopping Center* (1979) 23 Cal.3d 899; *PruneYard Shopping Center v. Robins*, 447 U.S. 74 (1980); *Fashion Valley Mall, LLC v. National Labor Relations Bd.* (2007) 42 Cal.4th 850 [S-1458] [S-1459]; *Ralphs Grocery Co. v. United Food & Commercial Workers Union Local 8* (2012) 55 Cal.4th 1083 [S-1456] [S-1457]; *Hudgens v. NLRB*, 424 U.S. 507 (1976); *Lloyd Corp. v. Tanner*, 407 U.S. 551 (1972).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** differs. On the MBE, and on any essay call limited to the U.S. Constitution, the shopping-center owner is not a state actor and the speaker's First Amendment claim fails (*Hudgens*). Only if the call asks about the California Constitution (or a California state-court suit leaves the law open) add that art. I, § 2 protects reasonable speech in the center's common areas, subject to time, place and manner rules and the Ralphs limit for store entrances (see ca-distinctions).

**Example:** Students gather petition signatures at a table in the central courtyard of a large regional mall that bans all petitioning. Their First Amendment claim fails for lack of state action (*Hudgens*). Under *Robins*, the California Constitution protects their reasonable petitioning in the courtyard, subject to the mall's reasonable time, place and manner rules; had they set up on the entrance apron of a single supermarket, *Ralphs* would defeat the state claim.

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: There is no PRC counterpart; PRC constitutional speech rights are not applied by courts against private property owners (approx.).
