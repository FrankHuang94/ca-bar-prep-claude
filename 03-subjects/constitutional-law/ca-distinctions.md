# Constitutional Law — California distinctions

## The headline: federal law governs Con Law essays

- California Constitutional Law essays test the **U.S. Constitution**. Every Con Law call indexed from Feb 2012 to Jul 2026 asks about the U.S. Constitution, a named federal provision (First Amendment, Fourteenth Amendment, Article IV), or federal justiciability (`06-past-exams/index.csv`) [VERIFY V-071].
- The essay default is "legal theories and principles of general application" unless the call asks for California law [S-077]. For Con Law, that means federal constitutional doctrine as the U.S. Supreme Court states it.
- **What to do on the exam:** write the federal rule. Do **not** add California constitutional analysis unless the call asks for it ("under the U.S. and California Constitutions") or the facts are set in a California state court and the call is open-ended. If you add it, keep it to one sentence per issue, after the federal analysis.
- **On the MBE:** never apply a California rule; "Constitution" means the federal Constitution unless the question says otherwise [S-040].

The table below lists the **real** differences that could matter if a call ever opens the door. Most are cases where the California Constitution protects **more** than the federal floor; a state may always give more protection under its own constitution, and such a ruling rests on an independent state ground (R-CON-018).

## Recurring California facts in federal cases

Many leading federal cases arose from California laws. They are **federal** law, so cite them on any essay:
- *Saenz v. Roe* (durational welfare limit, right to travel)
- *Tandon v. Newsom* (COVID limits on religious gatherings)
- *Cedar Point Nursery v. Hassid* (union access as a per se taking)
- *Nollan v. California Coastal Comm'n* (exactions)
- *Sheetz v. County of El Dorado* (legislative impact fees) [VERIFY V-035]
- *Brown v. Entertainment Merchants Ass'n* (violent video games)
- *NIFLA v. Becerra* (compelled notices)
- *Americans for Prosperity Found. v. Bonta* (donor disclosure)
- *Speiser v. Randall* (loyalty declaration for a tax exemption)
- *Hollingsworth v. Perry* (initiative proponents' standing)
- *National Pork Producers Council v. Ross*, 598 U.S. 356 (2023) (Proposition 12; dormant Commerce Clause; Pike reasoning fractured) [S-1465]
- *Nordlinger v. Hahn* (Proposition 13; rational basis)
- *Garamendi* (state law preempted by executive agreements)
- *Christian Legal Soc'y v. Martinez* (limited forum at a public law school)
- *California Democratic Party v. Jones* (blanket primary)
- *Kolender v. Lawson* (vagueness)
- *Johnson v. California* (racial segregation in prisons)

California's own analogue to 42 U.S.C. § 1983 is the Tom Bane Civil Rights Act (Civ. Code § 52.1). It belongs in a remedies discussion only if the call asks about state-law claims.
