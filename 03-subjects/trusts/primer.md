# Trusts — primer

Read this first. It gives the map; [`outline.md`](outline.md) gives the detail.

## 1. What a trust is, for a civil-law reader

A trust **splits ownership in two**. The **trustee** holds **legal title**: the trustee's name is on the deed or account, and the trustee can sell, invest and sign contracts. The **beneficiaries** hold **equitable title**: the economic benefit, enforced against the trustee in a court of equity. The person who sets it up is the **settlor** (also "trustor" or "grantor"). The property is the **res** or **corpus**.

Civil-law systems never fully adopted this split, because they treat ownership (所有权) as a single, indivisible right. The common law instead developed the trust in the English Court of Chancery. The trustee is the owner "at law," but equity forces the trustee to use the property only for the beneficiaries. In US law, everything about trusts follows from that idea:
- the trustee owes **fiduciary duties**;
- beneficiaries can **trace** trust property into the trustee's hands and beyond;
- trust property is **separate from the trustee's own estate**, so the trustee's personal creditors cannot take it.

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: the PRC Trust Law (信托法, 2001) borrowed the trust but describes the settlor "entrusting" (委托给) property rights to the trustee, which leaves title transfer unclear; US law plainly puts legal title in the trustee (approx.).

## 2. Why Californians use trusts

The typical bar-exam trust is a **revocable living trust**. A married couple signs a trust, names themselves trustees and lifetime beneficiaries, names their children as remainder beneficiaries, deeds their house to themselves "as trustees," and signs **pour-over wills** that send anything left outside the trust into it at death.

The point is to **avoid probate**, California's slow and costly court-supervised estate administration. When the settlor dies, the trust becomes **irrevocable** and the successor trustee distributes the assets privately. Trusts are also used:
- to manage money for minors or for a spendthrift child;
- to protect a beneficiary's interest from creditors (spendthrift trusts);
- to give to **charity** permanently.

## 4. Key vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Settlor / trustor | Creates the trust |
| Trustee | Holds legal title; owes fiduciary duties |
| Beneficiary | Holds the equitable interest; may be **income** (present) or **remainder** (future) |
| Inter vivos (living) trust | Created during life; avoids probate if funded |
| Testamentary trust | Created by will; goes through probate first |
| Revocable / irrevocable | Whether the settlor can cancel it. **California default: revocable** unless the instrument expressly says irrevocable (§ 15400) |
| Pour-over will | Will that gives probate assets to the trustee of an existing trust (§ 6300) |
| Spendthrift clause | Bars the beneficiary from assigning, and creditors from reaching, the interest before payment |
| Discretionary trust | Trustee decides whether and how much to pay |
| Support trust | Trustee must pay what is needed for support or education |
| Charitable trust | For a public charitable purpose; enforced by the Attorney General; can last forever |
| Cy pres | "As near as possible": redirecting a failed charitable trust to a similar purpose |
| Resulting trust | Implied return of property to the settlor (or estate) when an express trust fails |
| Constructive trust | Remedy forcing a wrongdoer to hand over property obtained wrongfully |
| Self-dealing | Trustee transacts with the trust for personal benefit, a breach whatever the price |
| Surcharge | Court order that the trustee personally pay for a loss |

## 5. How the pieces connect

- **Creation feeds everything.** If no valid trust exists, there are no trustee duties, and the property passes by resulting trust, will or intestacy. Wills + Trusts crossovers often turn on whether a will is valid, because a testamentary trust or a pour-over depends on it.
- **Revocability decides who controls.** While a trust is revocable, the settlor is in charge and the trustee's duties run to the settlor (§ 15800). The settlor's creditors can reach the assets (§ 18200). Beneficiaries cannot complain until the settlor dies (*Estate of Giraldin*).
- **Death is the turning point.** The trust becomes irrevocable, the trustee must send the § 16061.7 notice, contests must be filed within the 120/60-day window, and the full set of trustee duties to beneficiaries switches on.
- **Duties and remedies are a pair.** Every breach you find should end with a remedy: surcharge, rescission, tracing, removal or reduced compensation.

## 6. The ideas that matter most

1. **California presumes revocability.** Unless the instrument expressly says "irrevocable," the settlor may revoke (§ 15400). The traditional rule is the reverse; state both.
2. **Revoke or amend by the trust's method or the statutory method.** The statutory method is a signed writing, not a will, delivered to the trustee (§ 15401(a)(2)). The trust's own method excludes the statutory method only if the instrument makes it **explicitly exclusive**. *Haggerty v. Thornton* (2024) applies the same rule to amendments.
3. **Loyalty is absolute.** Self-dealing is a breach even at a fair price (§ 16004). Investing trust money in the trustee's own business breaches both loyalty and prudence.
4. **Invest the portfolio, not the asset.** Under the Uniform Prudent Investor Act (§§ 16045–16054), the trustee must diversify and suit risk to the trust's purposes, judged at the time and not by hindsight.
5. **Spendthrift protection is real but leaky in California.** Support claims, felony restitution, public support, and general creditors (up to 25%, or the excess over support needs) can get through (§§ 15305–15307). A settlor can never protect himself from his own creditors (§ 15304).
6. **Termination depends on who consents and on material purpose.** Settlor plus all beneficiaries can end any trust (§ 15404). Beneficiaries alone face the material-purpose limit (*Claflin*; California balances it under § 15403). Courts can modify for unforeseen changes (§ 15409) or end uneconomic trusts (§ 15408, now $100,000 for trustee action).
7. **Charitable trusts don't fail easily.** If a charitable purpose becomes impossible and the donor had a general charitable intent, the court applies **cy pres**; otherwise a resulting trust returns the property.
8. **Failed trusts go back.** Resulting trust to the settlor or estate, then the will's residue or intestacy. End every failure analysis by naming who takes.

## 7. Writing tips specific to Trusts

- **Organize by the call**, then by **issue** (validity, revocation, each duty, each remedy). For duties, use **one heading per duty**; graders look for separate headings.
- **Cite the Probate Code section** with each rule, and use the case names you know: *Haggerty*, *Giraldin*, *Claflin*.
- **Two-sided analysis** is rewarded. In Feb 2016, two passing answers reached opposite conclusions on whether a will revoked the trust (`00-exam-intel/grader-expectations.md` §5).
- **Always finish with distribution.** Who gets the property, and in what shares?
