# Civil Procedure — rule statements

**How to use.** Each block gives a 1–3 sentence rule you can write verbatim, then elements, exceptions, authority, the MBE/California split and a short worked example. Rules R-CIV-001 to R-CIV-102 are federal (FRCP and Title 28, the MBE law and the law of every recent federal-forum essay). Rules R-CIV-103 to R-CIV-120 are California CCP rules for essays that set the case in state court or ask you to compare. Statutes are cited to the current text; FRCP amendments through Dec. 1, 2025 are reflected, and there were no civil-rule amendments on Dec. 1, 2026 [S-103].

## I. Jurisdiction and venue

### R-CIV-001 — Federal courts have limited, non-waivable subject-matter jurisdiction
**Rule (write this):** Federal courts are courts of limited jurisdiction: every case must fall within Article III and a statutory grant, usually 28 U.S.C. § 1331 or § 1332. The party invoking federal jurisdiction bears the burden, and lack of subject-matter jurisdiction cannot be waived, forfeited or conferred by consent; the court must dismiss whenever the defect appears, even on appeal.

**Elements:** 1. A constitutional basis (Art. III, § 2). 2. A statutory grant. 3. The objection may be raised by any party or by the court at any time (Rule 12(h)(3)).
**Exceptions / defenses:** Jurisdictional facts are tested at filing (R-CIV-004). After a final judgment, a party who litigated (or could have litigated) jurisdiction generally cannot attack it collaterally (Restatement (Second) of Judgments § 12); a direct attack on a void judgment under Rule 60(b)(4) must still come within a reasonable time (R-CIV-090).
**Authority:** U.S. Const. art. III, § 2; 28 U.S.C. §§ 1331, 1332; FRCP 12(b)(1), 12(h)(3); Louisville & Nashville R.R. v. Mottley, 211 U.S. 149 (1908); Insurance Corp. of Ireland v. Compagnie des Bauxites de Guinee, 456 U.S. 694 (1982).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule. Open every federal-forum SMJ answer with it. California superior courts, by contrast, have general jurisdiction (R-CIV-103).
**Example:** D litigates for two years, loses at trial, then points out on appeal that P and D are both Nevada citizens. The court of appeals must order dismissal; D's delay does not waive the defect.
> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: Like PRC level and exclusive jurisdiction (级别管辖、专属管辖), the court checks it on its own motion, but a US federal court has no residual jurisdiction: every case needs a statutory "ticket" (approx.).

### R-CIV-002 — Federal-question jurisdiction and the well-pleaded complaint rule
**Rule (write this):** District courts have original jurisdiction of all civil actions arising under the Constitution, laws or treaties of the United States. Under the well-pleaded complaint rule, the federal issue must appear on the face of the plaintiff's own properly pleaded claim; an anticipated federal defense, or a federal issue raised in a reply or counterclaim, does not confer jurisdiction.

**Elements:** 1. The claim is created by federal law (the usual route), or fits R-CIV-003. 2. The federal element is part of the plaintiff's claim, not a defense. 3. No amount-in-controversy requirement.
**Exceptions / defenses:** Complete preemption converts certain state claims into federal ones (LMRA § 301; ERISA § 502(a)): Avco Corp. v. Machinists, 390 U.S. 557 (1968); Metropolitan Life Ins. Co. v. Taylor, 481 U.S. 58 (1987). For declaratory-judgment actions, ask whether the hypothetical coercive suit would arise under federal law (Skelly Oil Co. v. Phillips Petroleum Co., 339 U.S. 667 (1950)). Most federal claims are concurrent and may also be heard in state court; patent and copyright claims are exclusively federal (28 U.S.C. § 1338(a)).
**Authority:** 28 U.S.C. § 1331; Louisville & Nashville R.R. v. Mottley, 211 U.S. 149 (1908); American Well Works Co. v. Layne & Bowler Co., 241 U.S. 257 (1916).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.
**Example:** P sues in federal court for breach of a settlement agreement and alleges that D will argue a federal statute excused performance. No § 1331 jurisdiction: the claim is state contract law and the federal statute is only D's anticipated defense (Mottley).

### R-CIV-003 — State-law claims with an embedded federal issue (Grable / Gunn)
**Rule (write this):** A state-law claim arises under federal law only in a special and small category: when a federal issue is (1) necessarily raised, (2) actually disputed, (3) substantial to the federal system as a whole, and (4) capable of resolution in federal court without disrupting the federal–state balance approved by Congress.

**Elements:** The four Grable/Gunn factors above.
**Exceptions / defenses:** A fact-bound, backward-looking application of federal law in a private dispute is usually not "substantial" (Gunn: legal-malpractice claim about a patent case belongs in state court). The absence of a federal private right of action weighs against jurisdiction but is not decisive (Merrell Dow).
**Authority:** Grable & Sons Metal Products, Inc. v. Darue Engineering & Mfg., 545 U.S. 308 (2005); Gunn v. Minton, 568 U.S. 251 (2013); Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals Inc. v. Thompson, 478 U.S. 804 (1986).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.
**Example:** P's state quiet-title action turns on whether the IRS gave the notice of a tax sale that a federal statute requires. The federal issue is necessary, disputed, important to federal tax collection and rarely litigated: § 1331 jurisdiction exists (Grable).

### R-CIV-004 — Diversity: complete diversity, measured at filing
**Rule (write this):** Under 28 U.S.C. § 1332(a)(1), district courts have jurisdiction of civil actions between citizens of different States where the amount in controversy exceeds $75,000, exclusive of interest and costs. Diversity must be complete—no plaintiff may share citizenship with any defendant—and it is measured when the action is filed (and, in removed cases, also at removal); later changes of domicile neither create nor destroy it.

**Elements:** 1. Complete diversity between every plaintiff and every defendant. 2. Amount in controversy exceeds $75,000 (R-CIV-008). 3. Both tested at commencement.
**Exceptions / defenses:** Only minimal diversity is needed for statutory interpleader (§ 1335) and CAFA (§ 1332(d)). Nominal and fraudulently joined parties are disregarded; collusive assignments to create diversity are ineffective (§ 1359). A court may cure a defect by dropping a dispensable nondiverse party under Rule 21 (Newman-Green), but a court's erroneous dismissal of a properly joined nondiverse defendant after removal does not cure the defect—the judgment must be vacated (Hain Celestial).
**Authority:** 28 U.S.C. § 1332(a)(1); Strawbridge v. Curtiss, 7 U.S. (3 Cranch) 267 (1806); Grupo Dataflux v. Atlas Global Group, L.P., 541 U.S. 567 (2004); Newman-Green, Inc. v. Alfonzo-Larrain, 490 U.S. 826 (1989); Hain Celestial Group, Inc. v. Palmquist (2026) [S-251] [VERIFY V-034].
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.
**Example:** P1 (California) and P2 (Oregon) sue D1 (Washington) and D2 (Oregon). No diversity jurisdiction: P2 and D2 are both Oregon citizens, even though every other pairing is diverse.

### R-CIV-005 — Citizenship of natural persons; alienage
**Rule (write this):** A U.S. citizen is a citizen of the State of domicile—the place where the person is physically present with the intent to remain indefinitely; everyone has exactly one domicile, which continues until a new one is acquired. Alienage jurisdiction covers suits between citizens of a State and citizens or subjects of a foreign state, but not suits between aliens alone, and a lawful permanent resident is treated as non-diverse from a citizen of the State where both are domiciled.

**Elements:** 1. Physical presence. 2. Intent to remain indefinitely (evidence: home, job, voter registration, driver's licence, taxes, property, family). 3. For aliens: § 1332(a)(2) (State citizens v. aliens) or (a)(3) (citizens of different States with aliens as additional parties).
**Exceptions / defenses:** A U.S. citizen domiciled abroad is "stateless" and cannot sue or be sued in diversity (Newman-Green). Students and service members usually keep their prior domicile. Aliens on both sides defeat jurisdiction unless (a)(3) applies.
**Authority:** 28 U.S.C. § 1332(a)(1)–(3); Newman-Green, Inc. v. Alfonzo-Larrain, 490 U.S. 826 (1989); Mas v. Perry, 489 F.2d 1396 (5th Cir. 1974).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.
**Example:** P moved from Texas to San Diego on a two-year contract, keeps her Texas house and plans to return: she is still a Texas citizen. A Canadian green-card holder domiciled in California cannot use alienage jurisdiction to sue a California citizen.

### R-CIV-006 — Citizenship of corporations (place of incorporation and nerve center)
**Rule (write this):** A corporation is a citizen of every State and foreign state by which it has been incorporated and of the State or foreign state where it has its principal place of business. The principal place of business is the corporation's "nerve center"—the place where its high-level officers direct, control and coordinate its activities, normally its actual headquarters.

**Elements:** 1. Every place of incorporation. 2. One principal place of business (nerve center).
**Exceptions / defenses:** In a direct action against a liability insurer, the insurer also takes the insured's citizenship (§ 1332(c)(1)). A "headquarters" that is a mere mail drop or sham does not count (Hertz).
**Authority:** 28 U.S.C. § 1332(c)(1); Hertz Corp. v. Friend, 559 U.S. 77 (2010).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.
**Example:** Delta Inc. is incorporated in Delaware and run from its executive offices in Phoenix, although most of its plants are in California. Delta is a citizen of Delaware and Arizona, not California.

### R-CIV-007 — Unincorporated entities, representatives and class actions
**Rule (write this):** Partnerships, LLCs, unions and other unincorporated associations take the citizenship of every one of their members, traced through each layer of ownership. The legal representative of a decedent's estate, an infant or an incompetent takes the citizenship of the person represented; in a class action outside CAFA, only the named representatives' citizenship counts.

**Elements:** 1. Identify the entity type. 2. Corporation → R-CIV-006; any other entity → all members. 3. Representatives → the represented person.
**Exceptions / defenses:** Under CAFA, an unincorporated association is a citizen of the State of its principal place of business and of organization (§ 1332(d)(10)). A trustee of a traditional trust who holds and manages the assets sues in its own name and uses its own citizenship (Navarro Savings Ass'n v. Lee, 446 U.S. 458 (1980)).
**Authority:** Carden v. Arkoma Associates, 494 U.S. 185 (1990); 28 U.S.C. § 1332(c)(2); Supreme Tribe of Ben-Hur v. Cauble, 255 U.S. 356 (1921).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.
**Example:** Green LLC has two members, one a Californian and one a New Yorker. Green LLC is a citizen of both States and cannot sue a New York defendant in diversity.

### R-CIV-008 — Amount in controversy, aggregation and the domestic-relations/probate exceptions
**Rule (write this):** The matter in controversy must exceed $75,000, exclusive of interest and costs; the plaintiff's good-faith demand controls unless it appears to a legal certainty that the claim is really for $75,000 or less. A single plaintiff may aggregate all claims against a single defendant, but multiple plaintiffs may not aggregate separate claims, and a plaintiff may not aggregate claims against several defendants unless they are jointly liable.

**Elements:** 1. Good-faith demand. 2. Not legally certain to be ≤ $75,000. 3. Aggregation rules applied.
**Exceptions / defenses:** Exactly $75,000 is not enough. Plaintiffs with a common, undivided interest may aggregate. Punitive damages count if recoverable; attorney's fees count only if recoverable by statute or contract. A later recovery below the threshold does not defeat jurisdiction (but see costs, § 1332(b)). Supplemental jurisdiction may cover co-plaintiffs below the amount (Exxon Mobil, R-CIV-011). Federal courts do not issue divorce, alimony or custody decrees or probate wills even with diversity (Ankenbrandt v. Richards, 504 U.S. 689 (1992); Marshall v. Marshall, 547 U.S. 293 (2006)).
**Authority:** 28 U.S.C. § 1332(a), (b); St. Paul Mercury Indemnity Co. v. Red Cab Co., 303 U.S. 283 (1938); Snyder v. Harris, 394 U.S. 332 (1969).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.
**Example:** P sues D for $50,000 on a loan and $30,000 on an unrelated tort: $80,000 aggregates and suffices. If P1 claims $50,000 and P2 claims $40,000 against D, neither claim qualifies on its own.

### R-CIV-009 — Class Action Fairness Act (CAFA)
**Rule (write this):** Federal courts have jurisdiction over class actions with at least 100 members in which the aggregate amount in controversy exceeds $5 million, exclusive of interest and costs, and any class member is diverse from any defendant (minimal diversity), subject to exceptions for primarily local controversies.

**Elements:** 1. Class action (Rule 23 or state analogue). 2. ≥ 100 members. 3. Aggregate > $5,000,000. 4. Minimal diversity.
**Exceptions / defenses:** The court must decline when two-thirds or more of the class and the primary defendants are forum citizens (home-state and local-controversy exceptions) and may decline when more than one-third but less than two-thirds are; States and officials as primary defendants and certain securities and corporate-governance claims are excluded. Under § 1453, any defendant may remove without the others' consent, the forum-defendant rule and the one-year limit do not apply, and remand orders may be appealed by permission. A named plaintiff's pre-certification stipulation to recover less than $5 million cannot defeat CAFA (Standard Fire).
**Authority:** 28 U.S.C. §§ 1332(d), 1453; Standard Fire Ins. Co. v. Knowles, 568 U.S. 588 (2013).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.
**Example:** 5,000 consumers nationwide sue a Delaware–Texas company in California superior court for $20 million. The defendant may remove under CAFA alone, even though one named plaintiff is a Texan.

### R-CIV-010 — Supplemental jurisdiction (§ 1367(a))
**Rule (write this):** In any civil action within the court's original jurisdiction, the court has supplemental jurisdiction over all other claims so related that they form part of the same Article III case or controversy—that is, they derive from a common nucleus of operative fact such that a plaintiff would ordinarily be expected to try them in one proceeding—including claims that involve joining or intervening additional parties.

**Elements:** 1. An anchor claim within original jurisdiction. 2. Common nucleus of operative fact. 3. Not barred by § 1367(b). 4. Not declined under § 1367(c).
**Exceptions / defenses:** See R-CIV-011 and R-CIV-012. Compulsory counterclaims and crossclaims normally qualify; permissive counterclaims traditionally need an independent basis.
**Authority:** 28 U.S.C. § 1367(a); United Mine Workers v. Gibbs, 383 U.S. 715 (1966).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.
**Example:** P (California) sues D (California) for overtime under the federal Fair Labor Standards Act and adds a California wage-statement claim based on the same paychecks. Supplemental jurisdiction covers the state claim.

### R-CIV-011 — Limits in diversity-only cases (§ 1367(b))
**Rule (write this):** When original jurisdiction rests solely on diversity, § 1367(b) denies supplemental jurisdiction over claims by plaintiffs against persons made parties under Rules 14, 19, 20 or 24, and over claims by persons joined as plaintiffs under Rule 19 or intervening as plaintiffs under Rule 24, when exercising it would be inconsistent with § 1332. Because the bar targets plaintiffs, claims by defendants—impleader, counterclaims, crossclaims—are unaffected.

**Elements:** 1. Diversity-only anchor. 2. Claim by a plaintiff (or Rule 19/24 plaintiff). 3. Against a Rule 14/19/20/24 party. 4. Claim lacks independent diversity or amount.
**Exceptions / defenses:** Rule 20 co-plaintiffs and Rule 23 class members who do not meet the amount are not listed, so they may join if at least one plaintiff satisfies the amount and diversity is complete (Exxon Mobil); a non-diverse co-plaintiff still destroys jurisdiction over the whole action. A third-party defendant's claim against the plaintiff is allowed.
**Authority:** 28 U.S.C. § 1367(b); Owen Equipment & Erection Co. v. Kroger, 437 U.S. 365 (1978); Exxon Mobil Corp. v. Allapattah Services, Inc., 545 U.S. 546 (2005).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.
**Example:** P (Iowa) sues D (Nebraska) in diversity; D impleads T (Iowa) for contribution—proper. P then asserts a direct $100,000 claim against T—barred by § 1367(b) and lacking diversity (Kroger).

### R-CIV-012 — Declining supplemental jurisdiction; post-removal amendments; tolling (§ 1367(c), (d))
**Rule (write this):** A court may decline supplemental jurisdiction if the claim raises a novel or complex issue of state law, substantially predominates, all original-jurisdiction claims have been dismissed, or exceptional circumstances present compelling reasons. If, after removal, the plaintiff amends to drop every federal claim, the court loses supplemental jurisdiction over the remaining state claims and must remand them. The state limitations period is tolled while the claim is pending and for 30 days after dismissal, unless state law gives longer.

**Elements:** 1. A § 1367(c) ground. 2. Discretion weighed by economy, convenience, fairness and comity.
**Exceptions / defenses:** If diversity exists independently, dropping the federal claims changes nothing. Section 1367(d) stops the limitations clock rather than merely giving a grace period (Artis v. District of Columbia (2018)).
**Authority:** 28 U.S.C. § 1367(c), (d); Carnegie-Mellon Univ. v. Cohill, 484 U.S. 343 (1988); Royal Canin U.S.A., Inc. v. Wullschleger (2025) [VERIFY V-035].
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.
**Example:** D removes P's federal and state employment claims; P amends to delete the federal claim. With no diversity, the court must remand the state claims (Royal Canin).

### R-CIV-013 — Removal: what may be removed, by whom and to where
**Rule (write this):** A defendant may remove any civil action brought in state court over which the federal district courts have original jurisdiction, to the district and division embracing the place where the action is pending. Only defendants to the plaintiff's claims may remove, and a case removable only on diversity grounds may not be removed if any properly joined and served defendant is a citizen of the forum State.

**Elements:** 1. Original federal jurisdiction (judged by the complaint). 2. Removing party is a defendant. 3. Forum-defendant rule (diversity cases only). 4. Not a non-removable action. 5. Venue: the federal district embracing the state court.
**Exceptions / defenses:** A plaintiff cannot remove, even when facing a counterclaim (Shamrock Oil); a third-party counterclaim defendant cannot remove under § 1441(a) (Home Depot U.S.A., Inc. v. Jackson (2019)). A federal defense does not support removal (Caterpillar v. Williams). Most courts treat the forum-defendant rule as procedural, so it is waived if not raised within 30 days. If a federal-question claim is joined with claims outside federal jurisdiction, the whole case may be removed and the unrelated state claims severed and remanded (§ 1441(c)). State workers'-compensation claims and FELA claims against railroads are non-removable (§ 1445). General venue rules (§ 1391) do not apply to removed cases (Polizzi).
**Authority:** 28 U.S.C. §§ 1441(a), (b)(2), (c), 1445; Shamrock Oil & Gas Corp. v. Sheets, 313 U.S. 100 (1941); Caterpillar Inc. v. Williams, 482 U.S. 386 (1987); Polizzi v. Cowles Magazines, Inc., 345 U.S. 663 (1953).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule. A case removed from San Diego County Superior Court goes to the Southern District of California.
**Example:** P (Arizona) sues D (California) in San Diego Superior Court for $200,000. D cannot remove: D is a forum-State citizen (§ 1441(b)(2)). Had P sued in Arizona state court, D could remove to the District of Arizona.

### R-CIV-014 — Removal procedure: timing, unanimity, one-year limit
**Rule (write this):** A defendant removes by filing in federal court a notice of removal, signed under Rule 11 and containing a short and plain statement of the grounds, within 30 days after formal service of the initial pleading—or within 30 days after receiving an amended pleading, motion, order or other paper from which removability first appears. In § 1441(a) removals all properly joined and served defendants must join in or consent; each defendant has its own 30 days. A diversity case may not be removed more than one year after commencement unless the plaintiff acted in bad faith to prevent removal.

**Elements:** 1. Timely notice (30 days). 2. Unanimity. 3. One-year cap (diversity). 4. Prompt notice to adverse parties and the state court, which then proceeds no further.
**Exceptions / defenses:** Informal receipt of the complaint does not start the clock (Murphy Bros.). An earlier-served defendant may consent to a later-served defendant's timely removal (§ 1446(b)(2)(C)). Unanimity is not required for § 1441(c), § 1442 or § 1453 removals. The notice need only plausibly allege the amount in controversy (Dart Cherokee).
**Authority:** 28 U.S.C. § 1446(a)–(d); Murphy Bros. v. Michetti Pipe Stringing, Inc., 526 U.S. 344 (1999); Dart Cherokee Basin Operating Co. v. Owens, 574 U.S. 81 (2014).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.
**Example:** D1 is served March 1 and D2 March 20. D2 may remove by April 19 with D1's consent, even though D1's own 30 days have run.

### R-CIV-015 — Remand and curing jurisdictional defects
**Rule (write this):** A motion to remand based on any defect other than lack of subject-matter jurisdiction—untimeliness, lack of unanimity, the forum-defendant rule—must be made within 30 days after the notice of removal is filed; lack of subject-matter jurisdiction requires remand at any time before final judgment. A remand order is generally not reviewable on appeal. A jurisdictional defect actually cured before judgment does not require vacatur (Caterpillar v. Lewis), but a court's erroneous dismissal of a properly joined non-diverse defendant does not cure the defect, and the resulting judgment must be vacated and the case remanded (Hain Celestial).

**Elements:** 1. Classify the defect (jurisdictional or procedural). 2. Timing of the motion. 3. Fees only if removal lacked an objectively reasonable basis.
**Exceptions / defenses:** Remand orders are reviewable in § 1442 and § 1443 cases (and then the whole order is reviewable: BP p.l.c. v. Mayor & City Council of Baltimore (2021)) and, by permission, under CAFA (§ 1453(c)). If a plaintiff seeks to join a non-diverse defendant after removal, the court may deny joinder or permit it and remand (§ 1447(e)).
**Authority:** 28 U.S.C. § 1447(c)–(e); Caterpillar Inc. v. Lewis, 519 U.S. 61 (1996); Martin v. Franklin Capital Corp., 546 U.S. 132 (2005); Hain Celestial Group, Inc. v. Palmquist (2026) [S-251] [VERIFY V-034].
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.
**Example:** D (California) removes a diversity case from California state court; P moves to remand 45 days later under the forum-defendant rule. Too late: the procedural defect is waived. If P and a defendant were both Nevadans, remand is required whenever the defect is raised.

### R-CIV-016 — Federal-officer removal (§ 1442)
**Rule (write this):** A federal officer or agency, or a person "acting under" one, may remove a suit "for or relating to" any act under color of federal office if it raises a colorable federal defense. The "relating to" element is met by a plausible allegation that the challenged conduct is closely related to the performance of federal duties; a strict causal nexus is not required.

**Elements:** 1. Federal officer or person acting under one. 2. Conduct for or relating to the federal duties. 3. Colorable federal defense.
**Exceptions / defenses:** The well-pleaded complaint rule and unanimity do not apply; a remand order in a § 1442 case is appealable (§ 1447(d)).
**Authority:** 28 U.S.C. § 1442(a)(1); Mesa v. California, 489 U.S. 121 (1989); Chevron USA Inc. v. Plaquemines Parish (2026) [S-254] [VERIFY V-034].
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule (low frequency).
**Example:** A parish sues an oil company in state court over wartime production that the company says served federal aviation-fuel contracts. The company may remove if it plausibly alleges a close relationship between that production and its federal duties.

### R-CIV-017 — Personal jurisdiction: the two-step framework
**Rule (write this):** A court may exercise personal jurisdiction over a defendant only if (1) a statute authorizes it—in federal court, ordinarily the forum State's long-arm statute, borrowed through Rule 4(k)(1)(A)—and (2) the exercise is consistent with due process. Because California's long-arm statute reaches any basis not inconsistent with the state and federal constitutions, in a California forum the analysis collapses into the due-process test.

**Elements:** 1. Statutory authorization. 2. Due process: a traditional basis (R-CIV-018–019) or minimum contacts plus fairness (R-CIV-020–025).
**Exceptions / defenses:** Federal statutes authorizing nationwide service, the 100-mile bulge and Rule 4(k)(2) expand federal reach (R-CIV-027). Where a State's long-arm statute lists specific acts, analyze the statute first.
**Authority:** FRCP 4(k)(1)(A); CCP § 410.10; U.S. Const. amend. XIV, § 1 (state courts); amend. V (federal statutes); Pennoyer v. Neff, 95 U.S. 714 (1878); International Shoe Co. v. Washington, 326 U.S. 310 (1945).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule; on California essays always cite CCP § 410.10.
**Example:** P sues D in the Northern District of California on a state-law claim. Rule 4(k)(1)(A) borrows CCP § 410.10, which goes to the constitutional limit, so the only question is due process.
> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: PRC territorial jurisdiction (地域管辖) starts from the defendant's domicile; US personal jurisdiction is a constitutional fairness limit tested defendant by defendant (approx.).

### R-CIV-018 — Traditional bases: presence, domicile, consent, waiver
**Rule (write this):** A court has general jurisdiction over an individual who is personally served while voluntarily present in the forum ("tag" jurisdiction) or who is domiciled there. Any defendant may consent to jurisdiction—by contract, appearance or stipulation—or waive its objection by omitting it from its first Rule 12 motion or responsive pleading.

**Elements:** Presence + in-state service; or domicile; or consent; or waiver.
**Exceptions / defenses:** Presence procured by fraud or force is generally invalid, and many courts immunize persons who enter the State only to attend litigation. Most courts do not obtain general jurisdiction over a corporation merely by serving an officer who is in the State.
**Authority:** Burnham v. Superior Court, 495 U.S. 604 (1990); Milliken v. Meyer, 311 U.S. 457 (1940); Insurance Corp. of Ireland v. Compagnie des Bauxites de Guinee, 456 U.S. 694 (1982); FRCP 12(h)(1).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.
**Example:** D, a New Jersey resident, is handed the summons during a layover at San Francisco airport in a suit over a New Jersey contract. California has jurisdiction: D was voluntarily present and served there (Burnham).

### R-CIV-019 — Consent by corporate registration (Mallory)
**Rule (write this):** A State may require an out-of-state corporation, as a condition of registering to do business, to consent to general jurisdiction, and due process is satisfied where the registration statute expressly says so. Whether registration amounts to consent therefore depends on the forum's statute.

**Elements:** 1. Defendant registered. 2. The statute expressly conditions registration on consent to general jurisdiction.
**Exceptions / defenses:** A dormant Commerce Clause challenge was left open (Mallory, Alito, J., concurring). California's registration statute is not generally read as an express consent to general jurisdiction [VERIFY V-156], so in a California forum argue Daimler and specific jurisdiction instead.
**Authority:** Mallory v. Norfolk Southern Ry., 600 U.S. 122 (2023) [VERIFY V-035]; Pennsylvania Fire Ins. Co. v. Gold Issue Mining & Milling Co., 243 U.S. 93 (1917).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: apply Mallory when the facts give an express-consent statute. California essay: mention Mallory, then note California's statute and move on.
**Example:** Pennsylvania law states that registered foreign corporations consent to general jurisdiction. A Virginia railroad registered there can be sued in Pennsylvania by a Virginia worker injured in Virginia.

### R-CIV-020 — General jurisdiction: "at home"
**Rule (write this):** A court may hear any claim against a defendant that is "at home" in the forum. For an individual that is the domicile; for a corporation the paradigm forums are its place of incorporation and its principal place of business. Elsewhere, only in an exceptional case will a corporation's operations be so substantial as to make it at home; continuous and systematic business, even very large sales, is not enough.

**Elements:** 1. Individual: domicile. 2. Corporation: incorporation State or principal place of business, or an exceptional case.
**Exceptions / defenses:** Consent (R-CIV-019) and tag (R-CIV-018). The exceptional case is illustrated by a company temporarily run from the forum during wartime (Perkins).
**Authority:** Goodyear Dunlop Tires Operations, S.A. v. Brown, 564 U.S. 915 (2011); Daimler AG v. Bauman, 571 U.S. 117 (2014); BNSF Ry. v. Tyrrell, 581 U.S. 402 (2017); Perkins v. Benguet Consolidated Mining Co., 342 U.S. 437 (1952).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.
**Example:** A carmaker incorporated and headquartered in Michigan has huge California sales. It is not at home in California, so a Californian injured in Germany cannot sue it there on general jurisdiction.

### R-CIV-021 — Specific jurisdiction: minimum contacts and purposeful availment
**Rule (write this):** Specific jurisdiction requires that the defendant have minimum contacts with the forum such that the suit does not offend traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice. The defendant must have purposefully availed itself of the privilege of conducting activities in the forum, or purposefully directed activities at it, so that it should reasonably anticipate being haled into court there; the contacts must be the defendant's own, not the unilateral acts of the plaintiff or third parties.

**Elements:** 1. Contacts created by the defendant. 2. Purposeful (not random, fortuitous or attenuated). 3. Claim arises out of or relates to them (R-CIV-022). 4. Fair and reasonable (R-CIV-023).
**Exceptions / defenses:** A contract with a forum resident is not automatically enough; examine prior negotiations, contemplated future consequences, the contract's terms and the course of dealing (Burger King). A single deliberate contact can suffice when the claim arises from it (McGee). Unilateral acts of family members do not count (Kulko).
**Authority:** International Shoe Co. v. Washington, 326 U.S. 310 (1945); Hanson v. Denckla, 357 U.S. 235 (1958); McGee v. International Life Ins. Co., 355 U.S. 220 (1957); Kulko v. Superior Court, 436 U.S. 84 (1978); World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson, 444 U.S. 286 (1980); Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz, 471 U.S. 462 (1985).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.
**Example:** A New York dealer sells a car to New Yorkers, who drive it to Oklahoma and crash. The dealer has no Oklahoma contacts of its own; foreseeability that the car might travel there is not enough (World-Wide Volkswagen).

### R-CIV-022 — Relatedness: "arise out of or relate to"
**Rule (write this):** The plaintiff's claim must arise out of or relate to the defendant's forum contacts. Strict causation is not required—"relate to" is satisfied when the defendant systematically serves a forum market for the very product that injured a forum resident there—but there must be an adequate link between the forum and the specific claims, and non-residents' claims cannot borrow the link supplied by residents' similar claims.

**Elements:** 1. Defendant's forum contacts. 2. Causal connection ("arise out of") or close relationship ("relate to").
**Exceptions / defenses:** Ford reserved isolated or sporadic sales and internet sellers; in such cases relatedness is harder.
**Authority:** Ford Motor Co. v. Montana Eighth Judicial District Court, 592 U.S. 351 (2021); Bristol-Myers Squibb Co. v. Superior Court, 582 U.S. 255 (2017).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.
**Example:** Ford built and first sold a car elsewhere; it was resold into Montana and crashed there, killing a Montana resident. Ford's Montana advertising, sales and servicing of that model satisfy relatedness. Non-Californians who bought a drug outside California cannot sue in California merely because Californians bring the same claim (BMS).

### R-CIV-023 — Fairness (reasonableness) factors
**Rule (write this):** Once minimum contacts are shown, jurisdiction is presumptively reasonable; the defendant must present a compelling case that it is unreasonable, considering the burden on the defendant, the forum State's interest, the plaintiff's interest in convenient and effective relief, the interstate judicial system's interest in efficient resolution, and the shared interest of the States in furthering substantive social policies.

**Elements:** The five factors above, weighed together.
**Exceptions / defenses:** Inconvenience alone rarely defeats jurisdiction; the usual cure is transfer or forum non conveniens.
**Authority:** Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz, 471 U.S. 462 (1985); World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson, 444 U.S. 286 (1980); Asahi Metal Industry Co. v. Superior Court, 480 U.S. 102 (1987).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.
**Example:** In Asahi only a Taiwanese manufacturer's indemnity claim against a Japanese valve maker remained. The burden on the foreign defendant was severe and California's interest slight, so jurisdiction was unreasonable whatever the contacts.

### R-CIV-024 — Stream of commerce
**Rule (write this):** Placing a product into the stream of commerce with awareness that it may reach the forum may not, by itself, be purposeful availment. One view requires "something more" aimed at the forum—designing the product for its market, advertising there, establishing channels for customer advice, or marketing through a distributor that serves as the forum sales agent—while another accepts a regular, anticipated flow of products into the forum; no view has commanded a majority, so essays should apply both.

**Elements:** 1. Awareness of flow into forum. 2. Additional targeting conduct (O'Connor/Kennedy view). 3. Volume and regularity (Brennan view).
**Exceptions / defenses:** A single isolated sale is insufficient under any view (Nicastro, Breyer, J., concurring).
**Authority:** Asahi Metal Industry Co. v. Superior Court, 480 U.S. 102 (1987); J. McIntyre Machinery, Ltd. v. Nicastro, 564 U.S. 873 (2011).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule; on a California essay argue both views and conclude.
**Example:** An English machine maker sells through an Ohio distributor nationwide; one machine ends up in New Jersey. No New Jersey jurisdiction: no targeting and only one sale (Nicastro).

### R-CIV-025 — Intentional conduct aimed at the forum; internet contacts
**Rule (write this):** Intentional conduct expressly aimed at the forum, causing harm the defendant knows will be suffered there, can support specific jurisdiction (the Calder effects test). But the plaintiff cannot be the only link between the defendant and the forum: the analysis looks to the defendant's contacts with the forum itself, not with persons who reside there, and a website merely accessible from the forum is not purposeful availment.

**Elements:** 1. Intentional act. 2. Expressly aimed at the forum. 3. Harm the defendant knows is likely to be suffered in the forum.
**Exceptions / defenses:** Lower courts differ on interactive websites and targeted online sales.
**Authority:** Calder v. Jones, 465 U.S. 783 (1984); Keeton v. Hustler Magazine, Inc., 465 U.S. 770 (1984); Walden v. Fiore, 571 U.S. 277 (2014).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.
**Example:** Florida editors publish an article about a California actress, drawn from California sources, in a magazine with its largest circulation in California: California jurisdiction (Calder). A Georgia officer who seizes Nevadans' cash in Atlanta has no Nevada contacts merely because the victims live there (Walden).

### R-CIV-026 — In rem and quasi in rem jurisdiction
**Rule (write this):** Every assertion of state-court jurisdiction, including in rem and quasi in rem, must satisfy International Shoe's minimum-contacts standard. Claims about the forum property itself (title, possession, liens) will usually satisfy it, but attaching unrelated forum property to adjudicate an unrelated claim is valid only if the defendant independently has minimum contacts.

**Elements:** 1. Property in forum, seized or attached at the outset. 2. Relationship between property and claim. 3. Minimum contacts.
**Exceptions / defenses:** Recovery is limited to the property's value. A State may attach property to enforce a valid judgment rendered elsewhere.
**Authority:** Shaffer v. Heitner, 433 U.S. 186 (1977); Pennoyer v. Neff, 95 U.S. 714 (1878); FRCP 4(n).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.
**Example:** Delaware sequestered non-resident directors' shares in a Delaware corporation to force them to defend an unrelated derivative suit. Invalid without minimum contacts (Shaffer).

### R-CIV-027 — Federal reach of service: Rule 4(k)
**Rule (write this):** Serving a summons establishes personal jurisdiction over a defendant (1) who is subject to the jurisdiction of a court of general jurisdiction in the State where the district court sits; (2) who is joined under Rule 14 or 19 and served within a judicial district and not more than 100 miles from where the summons issued; (3) when a federal statute authorizes it; or (4) on a federal-law claim, if the defendant is not subject to any State's courts of general jurisdiction and exercising jurisdiction is consistent with the U.S. Constitution and laws.

**Elements:** Identify which of the four routes applies; the last uses the defendant's contacts with the United States as a whole.
**Exceptions / defenses:** The bulge rule applies only to Rule 14 and 19 parties.
**Authority:** FRCP 4(k)(1)(A)–(C), 4(k)(2); 28 U.S.C. § 2361 (nationwide service in statutory interpleader).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.
**Example:** P sues D in the Southern District of New York; D impleads T, served in Newark, 15 miles away. Jurisdiction over T exists under the 100-mile bulge even without New York contacts.

### R-CIV-028 — Notice must be reasonably calculated to inform
**Rule (write this):** Due process requires notice reasonably calculated, under all the circumstances, to apprise interested parties of the action and give them an opportunity to object. Publication alone is insufficient for persons whose names and addresses are known or reasonably ascertainable, and when the sender learns that notice failed, it must take further reasonable steps if practicable.

**Elements:** 1. Method reasonably calculated to reach the party. 2. Follow-up if the method is known to have failed.
**Exceptions / defenses:** Publication can suffice for unknown or missing persons.
**Authority:** Mullane v. Central Hanover Bank & Trust Co., 339 U.S. 306 (1950); Jones v. Flowers, 547 U.S. 220 (2006).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.
**Example:** A trustee notifies known beneficiaries of a judicial accounting only by newspaper notice. Inadequate for those whose addresses it had (Mullane).

### R-CIV-029 — Service of process: who serves, how, and when
**Rule (write this):** A summons must be served with the complaint, within 90 days after filing, by any non-party at least 18 years old. An individual in the United States may be served by personal delivery, by leaving copies at the individual's dwelling or usual place of abode with someone of suitable age and discretion who resides there, by delivery to an agent authorized by appointment or law, or by any method allowed by the law of the forum State or the State where service is made. A corporation or association may be served by delivery to an officer, a managing or general agent, or an agent authorized by appointment or law, or by a state-law method.

**Elements:** 1. Proper server. 2. Proper method for the defendant type. 3. Within 90 days.
**Exceptions / defenses:** If service is late the court must dismiss without prejudice or order service within a set time, and must extend for good cause (4(m)). Foreign defendants are served by internationally agreed means such as the Hague Service Convention (4(f)); the United States under 4(i). Rule 4 itself does not authorize service by ordinary mail (use waiver or state law). Defects in process or service are waived if not raised in the first Rule 12 response.
**Authority:** FRCP 4(c)(1)–(2), 4(e), 4(f), 4(h), 4(i), 4(m); FRCP 12(b)(4)–(5), 12(h)(1).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same in federal court; a federal court in California may also use California methods (R-CIV-104) through Rule 4(e)(1).
**Example:** A process server leaves the papers with D's mature 17-year-old son at D's home, where the son lives. Probably valid (suitable age and discretion). Leaving them with D's neighbour or at D's office is not valid under 4(e)(2), though it might be under state law.

### R-CIV-030 — Waiver of service
**Rule (write this):** A plaintiff may send an individual, corporate or association defendant a request to waive service, giving at least 30 days (60 if outside the United States) to return it. A defendant who timely waives has 60 days from the date the request was sent to answer (90 if outside the United States); a defendant located in the United States who fails without good cause to waive must pay the expenses later incurred in making service and the fees of any motion to collect them. Waiving service does not waive objections to personal jurisdiction or venue.

**Elements:** 1. Written request with the complaint and a prepaid means of return. 2. Defendant's response. 3. Cost-shifting for unjustified refusal.
**Exceptions / defenses:** Not available for minors, incompetents, the United States or foreign governments.
**Authority:** FRCP 4(d)(1)–(5); FRCP 12(a)(1)(A)(ii).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same in federal court. California's analogue is service by mail with notice and acknowledgment (CCP § 415.30).
**Example:** D ignores a proper waiver request and is then personally served. D must pay P's service costs absent good cause, and D's answer is due 21 days after service.

### R-CIV-031 — Federal venue (§ 1391(b)) and removal venue
**Rule (write this):** A civil action may be brought in (1) a judicial district where any defendant resides, if all defendants reside in the same State; (2) a district in which a substantial part of the events or omissions giving rise to the claim occurred, or a substantial part of the property at issue is situated; or (3) only if no district qualifies under (1) or (2), any district in which any defendant is subject to personal jurisdiction with respect to the action.

**Elements:** Test (1), then (2); use (3) only as a fallback. Venue is assessed claim by claim.
**Exceptions / defenses:** A removed case goes to the district embracing the state court, and § 1391 does not apply (§ 1441(a); Polizzi). Special venue statutes govern some claims (e.g., § 1397 for statutory interpleader). Venue objections are waivable (12(h)(1)). Several districts may qualify under (b)(2).
**Authority:** 28 U.S.C. §§ 1391(b), 1441(a); Polizzi v. Cowles Magazines, Inc., 345 U.S. 663 (1953).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.
**Example:** P sues D1 (resides in N.D. Cal.) and D2 (resides in S.D. Cal.) over a Nevada crash. Venue is proper in N.D. Cal. or S.D. Cal. (all defendants reside in California) or in D. Nev. (events).

### R-CIV-032 — Residence for venue purposes
**Rule (write this):** A natural person resides in the district of domicile. An entity defendant resides in any district where it is subject to personal jurisdiction with respect to the action; in a State with several districts, a corporation resides in any district whose contacts would support personal jurisdiction if that district were a separate State, or otherwise in the district with the most significant contacts. A defendant not resident in the United States may be sued in any district and is disregarded when applying § 1391(b)(1) to the other defendants.

**Elements:** Classify each defendant; apply § 1391(c)(1), (c)(2), (c)(3) or (d).
**Exceptions / defenses:** An entity plaintiff resides only where it has its principal place of business (§ 1391(c)(2)), but plaintiff residence does not set venue in any event.
**Authority:** 28 U.S.C. § 1391(c)(1)–(3), (d).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.
**Example:** Acme Corp. (Delaware; HQ Texas) sells into San Diego. On a claim arising from those San Diego sales, Acme resides in the Southern District of California because it would be subject to specific jurisdiction there.

### R-CIV-033 — Transfer for convenience (§ 1404(a)) and the law that follows
**Rule (write this):** For the convenience of parties and witnesses and in the interest of justice, a court may transfer a case to any district where it might have been brought—where venue and personal jurisdiction would have existed without the defendant's consent—or to any district to which all parties consent. The court weighs private factors (access to proof, availability of witnesses, cost) and public factors (congestion, local interest, familiarity with governing law) with deference to the plaintiff's choice, and the transferee court applies the law, including choice-of-law rules, that the transferor court would have applied.

**Elements:** 1. Venue proper in the transferor court. 2. Transferee is a district where the action might have been brought (or all parties consent). 3. Balance of factors.
**Exceptions / defenses:** A valid forum-selection clause changes the analysis (R-CIV-035). Transfer moves cases only between federal districts.
**Authority:** 28 U.S.C. § 1404(a); Hoffman v. Blaski, 363 U.S. 335 (1960); Van Dusen v. Barrack, 376 U.S. 612 (1964); Ferens v. John Deere Co., 494 U.S. 516 (1990).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.
**Example:** P sues in D. Mass., a proper venue; nearly all witnesses are in Arizona. The court may transfer to D. Ariz., which then applies Massachusetts choice-of-law rules—even if P sought the transfer (Ferens).

### R-CIV-034 — Improper venue (§ 1406(a))
**Rule (write this):** If venue is improper, the court must dismiss or, if it is in the interest of justice, transfer the case to any district in which it could have been brought, and it may transfer even if it lacks personal jurisdiction over the defendant. An improper-venue objection is waived if not raised in the defendant's first Rule 12 motion or answer.

**Elements:** 1. Timely 12(b)(3) objection. 2. Venue improper under § 1391 or a special statute. 3. Dismiss or transfer.
**Exceptions / defenses:** Courts usually transfer rather than dismiss when a new suit would be time-barred. Most courts hold that after a § 1406 transfer the transferee applies its own choice-of-law rules. Section 1631 allows transfer to cure a want of jurisdiction.
**Authority:** 28 U.S.C. §§ 1406(a)–(b), 1631; Goldlawr, Inc. v. Heiman, 369 U.S. 463 (1962); FRCP 12(b)(3), 12(h)(1).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.
**Example:** P sues in D. Or., where no defendant resides and nothing happened. On D's timely 12(b)(3) motion the court should transfer to a proper district rather than dismiss if the limitations period has run.

### R-CIV-035 — Forum-selection clauses (Atlantic Marine)
**Rule (write this):** A valid forum-selection clause pointing to another federal district is enforced through a § 1404(a) transfer—or, for a state or foreign forum, through forum non conveniens—and should control except in extraordinary circumstances unrelated to the parties' convenience. The plaintiff's choice of forum gets no weight, the parties' private-interest arguments are treated as waived, only public-interest factors count, and the original court's choice-of-law rules do not travel with the case.

**Elements:** 1. Valid, mandatory clause covering the dispute. 2. Public-interest factors only. 3. Burden on the party resisting the clause.
**Exceptions / defenses:** A clause procured by fraud or overreaching, or whose enforcement would contravene a strong public policy of the forum, is not enforced (Bremen). A clause does not make venue "wrong" under § 1406.
**Authority:** Atlantic Marine Construction Co. v. U.S. District Court, 571 U.S. 49 (2013); Stewart Organization, Inc. v. Ricoh Corp., 487 U.S. 22 (1988); M/S Bremen v. Zapata Off-Shore Co., 407 U.S. 1 (1972); Carnival Cruise Lines, Inc. v. Shute, 499 U.S. 585 (1991).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule in federal court.
**Example:** A subcontract requires suit in Virginia; P sues in W.D. Tex. On D's § 1404(a) motion, transfer to Virginia is nearly automatic, and the Virginia court applies its own choice-of-law rules.

### R-CIV-036 — Forum non conveniens
**Rule (write this):** A court may dismiss (or stay) a case when an adequate alternative forum exists—typically abroad—and the private and public interest factors strongly favor trial there. A plaintiff's choice of its home forum gets strong deference and a foreign plaintiff's choice less; that the alternative forum's law is less favorable to the plaintiff ordinarily does not defeat dismissal unless the remedy is so clearly inadequate that it is no remedy at all.

**Elements:** 1. Adequate, available alternative forum. 2. Private factors (evidence, witnesses, cost, enforceability). 3. Public factors (local interest, congestion, foreign law, jury burden). 4. Deference to plaintiff's choice.
**Exceptions / defenses:** Courts often condition dismissal on the defendant's consent to jurisdiction and waiver of limitations defenses abroad. A court may dismiss for FNC before resolving its own jurisdiction (Sinochem).
**Authority:** Gulf Oil Corp. v. Gilbert, 330 U.S. 501 (1947); Piper Aircraft Co. v. Reyno, 454 U.S. 235 (1981); Sinochem Int'l Co. v. Malaysia Int'l Shipping Corp., 549 U.S. 422 (2007).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same doctrine; California's statutory version is CCP § 410.30 (R-CIV-105).
**Example:** The estates of Scottish passengers sue U.S. manufacturers in Pennsylvania over a crash in Scotland. FNC dismissal is proper even though Scottish law is less favorable (Piper).

## II. Law applied by federal courts

### R-CIV-037 — Erie: state substantive law, federal procedure
**Rule (write this):** A federal court hearing a state-law claim—in diversity or supplemental jurisdiction—applies state substantive law, including the State's judicial decisions, because there is no federal general common law; it applies federal law to matters of procedure. Under the Rules of Decision Act, state laws are the rules of decision except where the Constitution, treaties or Acts of Congress otherwise require.

**Elements:** 1. Is the claim governed by state law? 2. Is there a valid federal statute or Rule directly on point? If yes → R-CIV-038. 3. If no → R-CIV-039.
**Exceptions / defenses:** Federal common law governs in limited enclaves (R-CIV-041).
**Authority:** Erie R.R. v. Tompkins, 304 U.S. 64 (1938) (overruling Swift v. Tyson, 41 U.S. (16 Pet.) 1 (1842)); 28 U.S.C. § 1652; Guaranty Trust Co. v. York, 326 U.S. 99 (1945).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule. On an essay: "The federal court applies California substantive law and federal procedural law."
**Example:** A pedestrian walking beside the track is struck by a passing train. The federal diversity court must apply Pennsylvania's trespasser rule, not a judge-made "general" rule (Erie).
> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: No PRC analogue—PRC civil law is national; Erie problems exist because each US State has its own private law alongside federal law (approx.).

### R-CIV-038 — A valid Federal Rule or statute on point controls (Hanna, Shady Grove, Berk)
**Rule (write this):** If a Federal Rule of Civil Procedure is sufficiently broad to answer the question in dispute, the federal court applies it, notwithstanding contrary state law, so long as the Rule is valid under the Rules Enabling Act—that is, it really regulates procedure, the manner and means of enforcing rights—regardless of the substantive purpose of the competing state law. A valid federal statute on point likewise controls under the Supremacy Clause.

**Elements:** 1. Direct collision: is the Rule broad enough to cover the issue? 2. Validity: does it really regulate procedure (28 U.S.C. § 2072(a)–(b))? 3. If yes to both, apply the Rule.
**Exceptions / defenses:** Courts read Rules to avoid needless collisions—Rule 3 does not govern when a state limitations period is tolled (Walker); Rule 59 does not set the excessiveness standard (Gasperini). In Shady Grove a concurring Justice would let a procedural-looking state rule survive if it is part of the State's definition of substantive rights.
**Authority:** Hanna v. Plumer, 380 U.S. 460 (1965); Sibbach v. Wilson & Co., 312 U.S. 1 (1941); Burlington Northern R.R. v. Woods, 480 U.S. 1 (1987); Stewart Organization, Inc. v. Ricoh Corp., 487 U.S. 22 (1988); Shady Grove Orthopedic Associates, P.A. v. Allstate Ins. Co., 559 U.S. 393 (2010); Berk v. Choy (2026) [S-250] [VERIFY V-034].
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.
**Example:** Delaware requires a malpractice plaintiff to file an expert affidavit of merit with the complaint. In federal diversity court, Rule 8 governs what a complaint must contain, so no affidavit is required (Berk v. Choy).

### R-CIV-039 — No federal rule on point: outcome, twin aims, countervailing federal interests
**Rule (write this):** Where no federal statute or Rule governs, the court asks whether disregarding the state rule would be outcome-determinative in a way that would promote forum shopping or the inequitable administration of the laws—Erie's twin aims. If so, it applies state law, unless the state rule is a form-and-mode rule not bound up with state-created rights and an important countervailing federal interest—such as the Seventh Amendment's allocation of functions between judge and jury—favors the federal practice.

**Elements:** 1. Would the difference change the outcome at the start of the case (forum-shopping test)? 2. Is the state rule bound up with state rights? 3. Countervailing federal interests (Byrd).
**Exceptions / defenses:** The jury-trial right in federal court is always federal (Byrd; Simler).
**Authority:** Guaranty Trust Co. v. York, 326 U.S. 99 (1945); Byrd v. Blue Ridge Rural Electric Cooperative, Inc., 356 U.S. 525 (1958); Hanna v. Plumer, 380 U.S. 460 (1965); Gasperini v. Center for Humanities, Inc., 518 U.S. 415 (1996).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.
**Example:** A state statute requires new trials when an award "deviates materially" from reasonable compensation. The federal trial judge applies that standard (it affects outcomes and would invite forum shopping), but appellate review is only for abuse of discretion, respecting the Seventh Amendment (Gasperini).

### R-CIV-040 — Recurring Erie answers
**Rule (write this):** In diversity cases federal courts apply state law to statutes of limitations and to when an action is commenced for limitations purposes, to choice of law (the forum State's rules, or the transferor State's after a § 1404(a) transfer), to the burden of proof on state claims, and to the elements and defenses of state claims; federal law governs service, pleading, joinder, class actions, discovery, the right to a jury and the standard for judgment as a matter of law. Where the State's highest court has not decided a question, the federal court predicts how it would rule or certifies the question if the State allows.

**Elements:** Classify the issue and apply the standard answer.
**Exceptions / defenses:** Choice-of-law rules do not travel with a transfer that enforces a forum-selection clause (Atlantic Marine).
**Authority:** Guaranty Trust Co. v. York, 326 U.S. 99 (1945); Walker v. Armco Steel Corp., 446 U.S. 740 (1980); Klaxon Co. v. Stentor Electric Mfg. Co., 313 U.S. 487 (1941); Van Dusen v. Barrack, 376 U.S. 612 (1964); Cities Service Oil Co. v. Dunlap (1939); Simler v. Conner, 372 U.S. 221 (1963); Commissioner v. Estate of Bosch, 387 U.S. 456 (1967).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.
**Example:** State law says an action is "commenced" for limitations purposes on service, not filing. P files in federal court before the deadline but serves D afterwards. The claim is time-barred (Walker).

### R-CIV-041 — Federal common law
**Rule (write this):** Although there is no federal general common law, federal courts make federal common law in limited enclaves where uniquely federal interests are at stake and applying state law would significantly conflict with federal policy—e.g., the rights and obligations of the United States under its contracts and commercial paper, the government-contractor defense, interstate water and pollution disputes, foreign relations, admiralty, and the preclusive effect of federal judgments. Federal common law binds state courts under the Supremacy Clause, and claims founded on it arise under federal law for § 1331.

**Elements:** 1. Uniquely federal interest. 2. Significant conflict with state law (or need for uniformity). 3. Court adopts a federal rule—or borrows state law as the federal rule.
**Exceptions / defenses:** Where no national uniformity is needed, courts adopt state law as the federal rule of decision (Kimbell Foods; Semtek).
**Authority:** Clearfield Trust Co. v. United States, 318 U.S. 363 (1943); Hinderlider v. La Plata River & Cherry Creek Ditch Co., 304 U.S. 92 (1938); Textile Workers Union v. Lincoln Mills, 353 U.S. 448 (1957); Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino, 376 U.S. 398 (1964); Illinois v. City of Milwaukee, 406 U.S. 91 (1972); United States v. Kimbell Foods, Inc., 440 U.S. 715 (1979); Boyle v. United Technologies Corp., 487 U.S. 500 (1988); Semtek Int'l Inc. v. Lockheed Martin Corp., 531 U.S. 497 (2001).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule (low essay frequency).
**Example:** A Marine's estate sues a helicopter contractor under state design-defect law. Federal common law supplies a defense if the government approved reasonably precise specifications, the equipment conformed, and the contractor warned of dangers it knew and the government did not (Boyle).

## III. Pretrial procedures

### R-CIV-042 — Preliminary injunction
**Rule (write this):** A preliminary injunction is an extraordinary remedy that may issue only on notice to the adverse party; the movant must show that it is likely to succeed on the merits, likely to suffer irreparable harm without preliminary relief, that the balance of equities tips in its favor, and that an injunction is in the public interest. The court must state findings and conclusions supporting its ruling and may consolidate the hearing with trial on the merits.

**Elements:** 1. Likelihood of success. 2. Likely irreparable harm (money damages inadequate). 3. Balance of hardships. 4. Public interest. 5. Notice and findings.
**Exceptions / defenses:** A mere possibility of irreparable harm is not enough (Winter); some circuits still use a sliding scale ("serious questions" plus a sharply favorable balance). Many circuits require a stronger showing for mandatory injunctions that change the status quo (e.g., reinstatement), and a temporary loss of income ordinarily is not irreparable harm (*Sampson v. Murray*, 415 U.S. 61 (1974)). Orders granting or denying preliminary injunctions are immediately appealable (§ 1292(a)(1)).
**Authority:** FRCP 65(a), 52(a)(2); Winter v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc., 555 U.S. 7 (2008).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Federal/MBE: the Winter test. California: the court weighs likelihood of prevailing and the relative interim harm to the parties (IT Corp. v. County of Imperial (1983) 35 Cal.3d 63), and an undertaking is required (CCP § 529).
**Example:** A fired engineer seeks a preliminary injunction ordering reinstatement. Lost pay is compensable in damages, so irreparable harm is usually missing (*Sampson*), and many courts require a heightened showing for a mandatory injunction.

### R-CIV-043 — TROs, security and the scope of injunctions
**Rule (write this):** A TRO may issue without notice only if specific facts in an affidavit or verified complaint clearly show immediate and irreparable injury before the adverse party can be heard, and the movant's attorney certifies in writing the efforts made to give notice and why it should not be required. An ex parte TRO expires at the time the court sets, not exceeding 14 days, unless extended once for good cause for a like period or by consent, and the adverse party may move to dissolve it on 2 days' notice. The movant must give security in an amount the court considers proper, and every injunction must state its reasons and terms specifically and binds only the parties, their officers, agents, servants, employees and attorneys, and others in active concert who receive actual notice.

**Elements:** 1. Specific facts showing immediate irreparable injury. 2. Attorney certification. 3. Duration ≤ 14 days. 4. Security. 5. Specific, binding terms.
**Exceptions / defenses:** TROs are generally not appealable, but a TRO extended beyond Rule 65(b) limits or issued after an adversary hearing may be treated as an appealable preliminary injunction (Sampson v. Murray, 415 U.S. 61 (1974)).
**Authority:** FRCP 65(b)–(d).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same in federal court; California TROs: CCP §§ 526, 527.
**Example:** On Monday the court freezes D's account ex parte, with no certification about notice, and leaves the order in place for 30 days. Defective: no certification and longer than 14 days without a good-cause extension.

### R-CIV-044 — The complaint: short and plain statement; plausibility
**Rule (write this):** A complaint must contain a short and plain statement of the grounds for the court's jurisdiction, a short and plain statement of the claim showing that the pleader is entitled to relief, and a demand for the relief sought. To survive a Rule 12(b)(6) motion it must allege factual content—not labels, legal conclusions or a formulaic recitation of the elements—that, accepted as true, allows the court to draw the reasonable inference that the defendant is liable: the claim must be plausible, not merely conceivable.

**Elements:** 1. Jurisdictional statement. 2. Claim statement. 3. Demand for relief. 4. Plausibility, tested in two steps: disregard conclusions, then ask whether the well-pleaded facts plausibly show liability.
**Exceptions / defenses:** Claims may be pleaded in the alternative or inconsistently (8(d)(2)–(3)); no heightened pleading beyond Rule 9 (Swierkiewicz).
**Authority:** FRCP 8(a), 8(d), 8(e); Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly, 550 U.S. 544 (2007); Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662 (2009); Swierkiewicz v. Sorema N.A., 534 U.S. 506 (2002).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Federal = notice pleading with plausibility. California = fact pleading (CCP § 425.10; R-CIV-106).
**Example:** "Defendants conspired to restrain trade," supported only by allegations of parallel pricing, is not plausible without facts suggesting an actual agreement (Twombly).
> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: A PRC complaint must state specific claims, facts and reasons (具体的诉讼请求和事实、理由), closer to California fact pleading than to federal notice pleading (approx.).

### R-CIV-045 — Pleading fraud or mistake with particularity (Rule 9(b))
**Rule (write this):** In alleging fraud or mistake, a party must state with particularity the circumstances constituting the fraud or mistake—usually the who, what, when, where and how of the misrepresentation—although malice, intent, knowledge and other conditions of mind may be alleged generally. Items of special damage must be specifically stated.

**Elements:** 1. Speaker. 2. Content. 3. Time and place. 4. Why it was false and how relied on. 5. Scienter may be general (but still plausible).
**Exceptions / defenses:** The remedy is dismissal under 12(b)(6) (usually with leave to amend) or a 12(e) motion.
**Authority:** FRCP 9(b), 9(g); Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662 (2009).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same in federal court; California also requires fraud to be pleaded specifically.
**Example:** "Seller lied about the watch" fails Rule 9(b). "On June 3 in Los Angeles, Seller's agent told Buyer in a signed letter that the watch was an unaltered 1960s original" satisfies it; Seller's knowledge of falsity may be alleged generally.

### R-CIV-046 — The answer: admissions, denials, affirmative defenses, timing
**Rule (write this):** A defendant must serve an answer within 21 days after service of the summons and complaint (60 days after a waiver request was sent; 14 days after notice of the court's ruling on a Rule 12 motion). The answer must admit or deny each allegation—lack of knowledge or information sufficient to form a belief operates as a denial—and an allegation other than one about damages is admitted if not denied. Affirmative defenses such as the statute of limitations, res judicata, statute of frauds, release, payment, estoppel and arbitration and award must be stated in the answer.

**Elements:** 1. Timely answer. 2. Admit/deny/lack knowledge for each allegation. 3. Affirmative defenses pleaded.
**Exceptions / defenses:** A general denial is proper only if the party in good faith denies everything. An omitted affirmative defense may be added by amendment (Rule 15).
**Authority:** FRCP 7(a), 8(b)(1)–(6), 8(c)(1), 12(a)(1), 12(a)(4).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** California gives 30 days to respond and allows a general denial to an unverified complaint (R-CIV-107).
**Example:** D's answer says nothing about P's allegation that D owned the truck. Ownership is admitted (8(b)(6)).

### R-CIV-047 — Rule 12 defenses: consolidation and waiver
**Rule (write this):** Lack of subject-matter jurisdiction, lack of personal jurisdiction, improper venue, insufficient process, insufficient service of process, failure to state a claim and failure to join a Rule 19 party may be raised by pre-answer motion or in the answer. The personal-jurisdiction, venue, process and service defenses are waived if omitted from a party's first Rule 12 motion or, if no motion is made, from its answer or an amendment as of course; failure to state a claim, failure to join a required party and failure to state a legal defense may be raised as late as trial; and lack of subject-matter jurisdiction is never waived.

**Elements:** 1. Identify the defense. 2. Was it available when the first Rule 12 motion or answer was served? 3. Apply 12(g)(2)/12(h).
**Exceptions / defenses:** A defense not available at the time (e.g., a party added later) is not waived.
**Authority:** FRCP 12(b)(1)–(7), 12(g)(2), 12(h)(1)–(3).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** California: a general appearance waives personal jurisdiction; challenge it by motion to quash (CCP § 418.10; R-CIV-104).
**Example:** D moves to dismiss for improper venue and loses, then moves to dismiss for lack of personal jurisdiction. Waived: the defense was available but omitted from the first motion.

### R-CIV-048 — Amending and supplementing pleadings
**Rule (write this):** A party may amend once as a matter of course within 21 days after serving the pleading or, if a responsive pleading is required, within 21 days after service of the responsive pleading or of a Rule 12(b), (e) or (f) motion, whichever is earlier. Otherwise it may amend only with the opposing party's written consent or the court's leave, which the court should freely give when justice so requires unless there is undue delay, bad faith or dilatory motive, repeated failure to cure, undue prejudice to the opponent, or futility. Issues tried by express or implied consent are treated as if pleaded, and supplemental pleadings may set out events occurring after the pleading.

**Elements:** 1. As of course (timing). 2. Consent. 3. Leave (Foman factors). 4. After a scheduling-order deadline, also good cause (Rule 16(b)(4)).
**Exceptions / defenses:** If a party objects at trial that evidence is outside the pleadings, the court may permit amendment and grant a continuance (15(b)(1)).
**Authority:** FRCP 15(a)(1)–(3), 15(b), 15(d), 16(b)(4); Foman v. Davis, 371 U.S. 178 (1962).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** California: CCP §§ 472, 473(a) (R-CIV-107).
**Example:** D answers on day 30; P amends on day 45 without leave. Permitted—within 21 days after service of the answer.

### R-CIV-049 — Relation back of amendments
**Rule (write this):** An amendment relates back to the date of the original pleading when (A) the law that provides the limitations period allows it; (B) it asserts a claim or defense arising out of the conduct, transaction or occurrence set out—or attempted to be set out—in the original pleading; or (C) it changes the party or the naming of the party against whom a claim is asserted, if (B) is satisfied and, within the Rule 4(m) period, the new party received such notice of the action that it will not be prejudiced in defending and knew or should have known that it would have been sued but for a mistake concerning the proper party's identity.

**Elements:** New claim → same conduct/transaction. New defendant → same conduct + notice within 90 days + knowledge of the mistake.
**Exceptions / defenses:** A deliberate, informed choice to sue someone else is not a "mistake." Many federal courts hold that replacing a "John Doe" is not a mistake about identity (contrast California, R-CIV-107).
**Authority:** FRCP 15(c)(1)(A)–(C); Krupski v. Costa Crociere S.p.A., 560 U.S. 538 (2010).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same in federal court; California Doe practice differs.
**Example:** P sues "Costa Cruise Lines" just before limitations run; the true carrier, an affiliate, learns of the suit within 90 days and knows P meant to sue it. Substitution relates back; the question is what the new defendant knew, not P's diligence (Krupski).

### R-CIV-050 — Rule 11: what signing certifies
**Rule (write this):** By presenting a pleading, written motion or other paper to the court, an attorney or unrepresented party certifies that, to the best of the person's knowledge, information and belief formed after an inquiry reasonable under the circumstances, (1) it is not presented for an improper purpose such as harassment, delay or needless cost; (2) its claims, defenses and legal contentions are warranted by existing law or by a nonfrivolous argument for extending, modifying or reversing existing law or establishing new law; (3) its factual contentions have evidentiary support or, if specifically so identified, will likely have it after a reasonable opportunity for investigation or discovery; and (4) its denials are warranted on the evidence or reasonably based on belief or a lack of information.

**Elements:** The four certifications, judged objectively at the time of presenting (including later advocating) the paper.
**Exceptions / defenses:** Rule 11 does not apply to discovery papers (Rule 11(d)); those are governed by Rules 26(g) and 37.
**Authority:** FRCP 11(a)–(b), 11(d); Cooter & Gell v. Hartmarx Corp., 496 U.S. 384 (1990).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** California counterpart: CCP § 128.7 (R-CIV-119).
**Example:** A lawyer sues five trucking companies, alleging each owned the truck, without checking registration records available online. The factual contentions lack evidentiary support after reasonable inquiry.

### R-CIV-051 — Rule 11: sanctions procedure and limits
**Rule (write this):** A Rule 11 motion must be made separately from other motions and served on the offending party, but it may not be filed until 21 days after service, and only if the challenged paper has not been withdrawn or appropriately corrected within that safe harbor; the court may also act on its own through an order to show cause. A sanction must be limited to what suffices to deter repetition—nonmonetary directives, a penalty paid into court or, on motion, reasonable attorney's fees caused by the violation—and a law firm is jointly responsible for its lawyers' violations absent exceptional circumstances.

**Elements:** 1. Separate motion. 2. Service + 21-day safe harbor. 3. Violation. 4. Deterrence-limited sanction.
**Exceptions / defenses:** No monetary sanctions against a represented party for violating 11(b)(2) (frivolous legal contentions) (Rule 11(c)(5)(A)); no court-initiated monetary sanctions unless the show-cause order issued before voluntary dismissal or settlement. 28 U.S.C. § 1927 and the court's inherent power are separate sources.
**Authority:** FRCP 11(c)(1)–(6).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same structure under CCP § 128.7.
**Example:** D's lawyer serves and files a Rule 11 motion on the same day. The court should deny it for failure to honor the 21-day safe harbor.

### R-CIV-052 — Joinder of claims, counterclaims and crossclaims
**Rule (write this):** A party asserting a claim may join as many claims as it has against an opposing party. A counterclaim is compulsory if it arises out of the transaction or occurrence that is the subject matter of the opposing party's claim and does not require adding a party over whom the court cannot acquire jurisdiction, and it is barred in later litigation if not pleaded; other counterclaims are permissive. A party may—but need not—assert a crossclaim against a coparty if it arises out of the transaction or occurrence that is the subject of the original action or a counterclaim, or relates to property at issue.

**Elements:** 1. Rule 18 joinder (no relatedness needed). 2. Counterclaim: same transaction or occurrence (logical-relationship test) → compulsory. 3. Crossclaim: coparty + same transaction → permissive. 4. Each claim needs SMJ.
**Exceptions / defenses:** A counterclaim is not compulsory if it was already the subject of another pending action, or if the court obtained jurisdiction only by attachment and the pleader asserts no counterclaim (13(a)(2)); the rule applies only to a party who serves a pleading. Compulsory counterclaims and crossclaims normally fit within § 1367(a); permissive counterclaims traditionally need their own jurisdictional basis.
**Authority:** FRCP 13(a)–(b), 13(g)–(h), 18(a); 28 U.S.C. § 1367.
**MBE vs. CA essay:** California's counterpart is the compulsory cross-complaint (CCP § 426.30; R-CIV-109).
**Example:** P sues D for injuries from a collision. D's claim for her own injuries in the same collision is compulsory; if D answers without it, a later suit by D is barred.

### R-CIV-053 — Permissive joinder of parties; misjoinder
**Rule (write this):** Persons may join as plaintiffs, or be joined as defendants, if (1) a right to relief is asserted by or against them jointly, severally or in the alternative with respect to or arising out of the same transaction, occurrence, or series of transactions or occurrences, and (2) any question of law or fact common to all of them will arise. Misjoinder is not a ground for dismissal; the court may add or drop parties, sever claims, or order separate trials.

**Elements:** Same transaction/series + one common question; plus SMJ, PJ and venue for each party.
**Exceptions / defenses:** In diversity cases § 1367(b) bars supplemental jurisdiction over a plaintiff's claims against Rule 20 defendants; Exxon Mobil allows Rule 20 co-plaintiffs below the amount.
**Authority:** FRCP 20(a)(1)–(2), 20(b), 21, 42(b).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** California: CCP §§ 378, 379 (same test in substance).
**Example:** Three passengers hurt in one bus crash sue the bus company and the other driver together: same occurrence and common questions; joinder proper (check complete diversity).

### R-CIV-054 — Required parties (Rule 19(a))
**Rule (write this):** A person who is subject to service and whose joinder will not deprive the court of subject-matter jurisdiction must be joined if (A) in that person's absence the court cannot accord complete relief among the existing parties, or (B) the person claims an interest relating to the subject of the action and is so situated that disposing of the action in the person's absence may (i) as a practical matter impair or impede the person's ability to protect the interest, or (ii) leave an existing party subject to a substantial risk of double, multiple or otherwise inconsistent obligations.

**Elements:** 1. Complete relief, or 2. impairment of the absentee's interest, or 3. risk of inconsistent obligations.
**Exceptions / defenses:** Joint tortfeasors and jointly and severally liable obligors are merely permissive parties (Temple). A joined party who objects to venue, making it improper, must be dismissed (19(a)(3)). Raised by 12(b)(7).
**Authority:** FRCP 19(a)(1)–(3), 12(b)(7); Temple v. Synthes Corp., 498 U.S. 5 (1990).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** California: CCP § 389 (same structure).
**Example:** A buyer sues a tire maker over a blowout; the maker moves to dismiss for failure to join the retailer that mounted the tire. Denied: a possible joint tortfeasor is not a required party.

### R-CIV-055 — When joinder is not feasible (Rule 19(b))
**Rule (write this):** If a required person cannot be joined, the court must determine whether, in equity and good conscience, the action should proceed among the existing parties or be dismissed, considering (1) the extent to which a judgment without the person might prejudice that person or the existing parties; (2) the extent to which protective provisions, shaping of relief or other measures could lessen that prejudice; (3) whether a judgment without the person would be adequate; and (4) whether the plaintiff would have an adequate remedy if the action were dismissed for nonjoinder.

**Elements:** The four factors; no single factor is decisive.
**Exceptions / defenses:** Dismissal is favored where an absent sovereign's non-frivolous immunity claim would be prejudiced (Pimentel).
**Authority:** FRCP 19(b); Provident Tradesmens Bank & Trust Co. v. Patterson, 390 U.S. 102 (1968); Republic of Philippines v. Pimentel, 553 U.S. 851 (2008).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same in substance (CCP § 389(b)).
**Example:** P asks the court to cancel a license held jointly by D and X; X is non-diverse and cannot be joined. Cancellation would destroy X's rights and relief cannot be shaped around it, and a state court can hear everyone: dismiss.

### R-CIV-056 — Impleader (Rule 14)
**Rule (write this):** A defending party may, as third-party plaintiff, bring in a non-party who is or may be liable to it for all or part of the claim against it—derivative liability such as indemnity, contribution or subrogation—without leave if it files the third-party complaint within 14 days after serving its original answer, and with leave afterwards. The third-party defendant must assert its Rule 12 defenses and compulsory counterclaims against the third-party plaintiff, may assert crossclaims and any claim against the plaintiff arising out of the same transaction or occurrence, and the plaintiff may assert such claims against it.

**Elements:** 1. Defending party. 2. Derivative liability ("liable to me if I am liable to P"). 3. Timing/leave. 4. SMJ and PJ for the new claim.
**Exceptions / defenses:** A defendant cannot implead someone who is liable only to the plaintiff ("it was him, not me")—that is a defense. In diversity cases the plaintiff's claims against the third-party defendant need independent jurisdiction (§ 1367(b); Kroger). The 100-mile bulge applies (Rule 4(k)(1)(B)).
**Authority:** FRCP 14(a)(1)–(5), 14(b); 28 U.S.C. § 1367(b); Owen Equipment & Erection Co. v. Kroger, 437 U.S. 365 (1978).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** California uses a cross-complaint against a new party (CCP § 428.10(b); R-CIV-109).
**Example:** A patient sues a hospital; the hospital impleads its staffing agency under an indemnity clause—proper. It may not implead a surgeon merely to argue that the surgeon alone caused the harm.

### R-CIV-057 — Intervention (Rule 24)
**Rule (write this):** On timely motion, the court must permit anyone to intervene who has an unconditional statutory right, or who claims an interest relating to the property or transaction that is the subject of the action and is so situated that disposing of the action may as a practical matter impair or impede its ability to protect that interest, unless existing parties adequately represent it. The court may permit intervention by anyone with a conditional statutory right or with a claim or defense that shares a common question of law or fact with the main action, considering undue delay and prejudice.

**Elements:** As of right: timeliness + interest + practical impairment + inadequate representation. Permissive: timeliness + common question + no undue delay or prejudice.
**Exceptions / defenses:** In diversity cases, § 1367(b) bars supplemental jurisdiction over claims by persons seeking to intervene as plaintiffs.
**Authority:** FRCP 24(a)–(c); 28 U.S.C. § 1367(b).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** California: CCP § 387 (similar).
**Example:** Neighbors seek to intervene in a developer's suit to overturn a city's zoning denial. They have an interest that may be impaired, but the city may adequately represent it—the contested factor.

### R-CIV-058 — Interpleader
**Rule (write this):** A stakeholder facing multiple, possibly inconsistent claims to the same fund or property may join all claimants and require them to litigate entitlement. Statutory interpleader requires only $500 or more in controversy and minimal diversity between two or more adverse claimants, allows nationwide service and venue where any claimant resides, and requires the stakeholder to deposit the property or a bond; Rule 22 interpleader requires ordinary subject-matter jurisdiction (complete diversity between stakeholder and claimants and more than $75,000, or a federal question), ordinary personal jurisdiction and venue.

**Elements:** 1. Stake. 2. Adverse claimants. 3. Statutory or rule route with its jurisdictional requirements.
**Exceptions / defenses:** Interpleader is not a device to force every tort claim arising from an accident into one forum (Tashire).
**Authority:** 28 U.S.C. §§ 1335, 1397, 2361; FRCP 22; State Farm Fire & Casualty Co. v. Tashire, 386 U.S. 523 (1967).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** California: CCP § 386.
**Example:** An insurer holding a $20,000 policy faces claims from a Californian and an Oregonian. Statutory interpleader works (minimal diversity, at least $500) even though the insurer is a California citizen; Rule 22 would fail on amount and diversity.

### R-CIV-059 — Class actions: Rule 23(a) prerequisites
**Rule (write this):** One or more members of a class may sue or be sued as representatives of all members only if (1) the class is so numerous that joinder of all members is impracticable, (2) there are questions of law or fact common to the class, (3) the representatives' claims or defenses are typical of the class's, and (4) the representatives will fairly and adequately protect the class's interests—and the action also fits a Rule 23(b) category. Commonality requires a common contention whose truth or falsity will resolve an issue central to each member's claim in one stroke.

**Elements:** Numerosity, commonality, typicality, adequacy (of representatives and class counsel, 23(g)).
**Exceptions / defenses:** Certification requires a rigorous analysis that may overlap the merits (Dukes). Intra-class conflicts defeat adequacy (Amchem).
**Authority:** FRCP 23(a), 23(c)(1), 23(g); Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes, 564 U.S. 338 (2011); Amchem Products, Inc. v. Windsor, 521 U.S. 591 (1997).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** California: CCP § 382 (ascertainable class and well-defined community of interest; courts look to Rule 23 for guidance).
**Example:** 1.5 million employees challenge thousands of managers' discretionary pay decisions with no common policy. No commonality (Dukes).

### R-CIV-060 — Class actions: Rule 23(b) types and notice
**Rule (write this):** A class must also fit one type: (b)(1) separate actions would create a risk of incompatible standards for the opposing party or would as a practical matter dispose of absent members' interests (e.g., a limited fund); (b)(2) the opposing party has acted or refused to act on grounds that apply generally, so final injunctive or declaratory relief is appropriate for the class as a whole; or (b)(3) common questions predominate over individual ones and a class action is superior to other methods. Members of a (b)(3) class must receive the best notice practicable, including individual notice to all members identifiable through reasonable effort, and the right to opt out; the plaintiff ordinarily bears the cost of notice (Eisen), though a court may direct a defendant to perform tasks it can do more cheaply, such as identifying members (Oppenheimer Fund, Inc. v. Sanders, 437 U.S. 340 (1978)).

**Elements:** For (b)(3): predominance + superiority (members' interest in individual control, existing litigation, desirability of the forum, manageability) + notice and opt-out.
**Exceptions / defenses:** Individualized monetary claims cannot proceed under (b)(2) (Dukes). The damages model must measure only damages attributable to the class-wide theory (Comcast).
**Authority:** FRCP 23(b)(1)–(3), 23(c)(2)–(3); Eisen v. Carlisle & Jacquelin, 417 U.S. 156 (1974); Comcast Corp. v. Behrend, 569 U.S. 27 (2013).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Similar in California (CCP § 382).
**Example:** Customers seek damages for a uniform hidden fee. A (b)(3) class: common questions predominate; notice by mail or email to identified customers with an opt-out right.

### R-CIV-061 — Class actions: jurisdiction, settlement, appeal and binding effect
**Rule (write this):** Outside CAFA, only the named representatives' citizenship counts for diversity, and at least one named plaintiff must satisfy the amount in controversy. A certified class's claims may be settled, voluntarily dismissed or compromised only with court approval, after notice to the class and a hearing, on a finding that the deal is fair, reasonable and adequate. A court of appeals may permit an appeal from an order granting or denying certification if a petition is filed within 14 days after the order. A class judgment binds absent members who were adequately represented and, in a (b)(3) class, to whom the Rule 23(c)(2) notice was directed and who did not opt out (FRCP 23(c)(3)(B)); absent plaintiffs need not have minimum contacts with the forum.

**Elements:** Jurisdiction; settlement approval; Rule 23(f) appeal; preclusive effect.
**Exceptions / defenses:** Voluntarily dismissing individual claims to manufacture an appealable final judgment after certification is denied is not allowed (Microsoft v. Baker). Every class member must have Article III standing to recover individual damages (TransUnion).
**Authority:** FRCP 23(e), 23(f); Supreme Tribe of Ben-Hur v. Cauble, 255 U.S. 356 (1921); Exxon Mobil Corp. v. Allapattah Services, Inc., 545 U.S. 546 (2005); Hansberry v. Lee, 311 U.S. 32 (1940); Phillips Petroleum Co. v. Shutts, 472 U.S. 797 (1985); Microsoft Corp. v. Baker, 582 U.S. 23 (2017); TransUnion LLC v. Ramirez, 594 U.S. 413 (2021).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** California: an order denying certification is appealable under the "death knell" doctrine (R-CIV-118).
**Example:** Certification is denied; the named plaintiffs dismiss their own claims and appeal. Dismissed for want of a final judgment; the proper route was a Rule 23(f) petition within 14 days.

### R-CIV-062 — Initial disclosures (Rule 26(a)(1))
**Rule (write this):** Without awaiting a discovery request, a party must disclose (i) the name and, if known, address and telephone number of each individual likely to have discoverable information—with the subjects of that information—that the party may use to support its claims or defenses; (ii) a copy, or a description by category and location, of documents, ESI and tangible things in its possession, custody or control that it may use to support its claims or defenses; (iii) a computation of each category of damages claimed, with the supporting documents; and (iv) any insurance agreement that may satisfy a judgment. Unless otherwise stipulated or ordered, disclosures are due within 14 days after the Rule 26(f) conference.

**Elements:** The four categories; timing; based on information reasonably available.
**Exceptions / defenses:** Material to be used solely for impeachment; categories of proceedings exempt under 26(a)(1)(B). A party is not excused because its investigation is incomplete. Failure to disclose → exclusion unless substantially justified or harmless (37(c)(1)).
**Authority:** FRCP 26(a)(1)(A)–(E), 37(c)(1).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Federal disclosures are automatic. California disclosures are due only on demand (CCP § 2016.090; R-CIV-111).
**Example:** D never discloses an eyewitness whose account helps D, then calls her at trial. The court should exclude her unless the failure was substantially justified or harmless.

### R-CIV-063 — Expert and pretrial disclosures; duty to supplement
**Rule (write this):** A party must disclose the identity of any expert it may use at trial, and an expert retained or specially employed to testify must provide a written report containing all opinions and their basis, the facts or data considered, exhibits, qualifications (including publications in the previous 10 years), cases in which the expert testified in the previous 4 years, and compensation. Absent an order, expert disclosures are due at least 90 days before trial (rebuttal within 30 days after the other side's), pretrial disclosures of witnesses, deposition designations and exhibits at least 30 days before trial (objections within 14 days), and every party must timely supplement disclosures and responses that it learns are materially incomplete or incorrect.

**Elements:** Identity; report (retained experts) or summary (non-retained, e.g., treating physicians); timing; supplementation.
**Exceptions / defenses:** Late or missing disclosure → exclusion under 37(c)(1) unless substantially justified or harmless.
**Authority:** FRCP 26(a)(2)(A)–(E), 26(a)(3), 26(e), 37(c)(1).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** California exchanges expert information on demand (CCP § 2034.210 et seq.).
**Example:** D's retained engineer will testify; her report is due at least 90 days before trial. P's treating surgeon, not retained, needs only a summary disclosure of subject matter and opinions.

### R-CIV-064 — Scope of discovery and proportionality
**Rule (write this):** Parties may obtain discovery regarding any nonprivileged matter that is relevant to any party's claim or defense and proportional to the needs of the case, considering the importance of the issues at stake, the amount in controversy, the parties' relative access to relevant information, the parties' resources, the importance of the discovery in resolving the issues, and whether the burden or expense of the proposed discovery outweighs its likely benefit. Information need not be admissible in evidence to be discoverable, and the court must limit discovery that is unreasonably cumulative or duplicative or obtainable from a more convenient, less burdensome or less expensive source.

**Elements:** 1. Nonprivileged. 2. Relevant to a claim or defense. 3. Proportional (six factors).
**Exceptions / defenses:** Privilege (R-CIV-065), work product (R-CIV-066), experts (R-CIV-067), protective orders (R-CIV-072). Signing a discovery request certifies it is proportional (26(g)).
**Authority:** FRCP 26(b)(1), 26(b)(2)(C), 26(g).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** California's scope is broader: matter relevant to the subject matter of the action and admissible or reasonably calculated to lead to admissible evidence (CCP § 2017.010; R-CIV-110).
**Example:** In a single slip-and-fall case, P demands every injury report from all of the chain's 2,000 stores for 20 years. Some reports are relevant, but the request is disproportionate; the court will likely limit it to the same store, similar displays and a few years.

### R-CIV-065 — Privilege, privilege logs and clawback
**Rule (write this):** Privileged matter is not discoverable. A party withholding otherwise discoverable information as privileged or as trial-preparation material must expressly make the claim and describe the nature of the documents, communications or things withheld in a manner that, without revealing the protected information, will enable the other parties to assess the claim; since December 1, 2025, the Rule 26(f) discovery plan should address, and the scheduling order may address, the timing and method for doing so. If privileged material is produced, the producing party may notify the recipient, who must promptly return, sequester or destroy it until the claim is resolved, and an inadvertent disclosure is not a waiver if the holder took reasonable steps to prevent it and promptly to rectify it.

**Elements:** 1. Privilege applies (attorney–client, etc.). 2. Claim made expressly with a log. 3. Waiver analysis (FRE 502).
**Exceptions / defenses:** Facts are not privileged even if communicated to counsel (Upjohn). In diversity cases, state privilege law governs privileges for state-law claims (FRE 501). Failure to provide an adequate log can result in waiver in the court's discretion.
**Authority:** FRCP 26(b)(5)(A)–(B), 26(f)(3)(D), 16(b)(3)(B) [S-107] [S-276]; FRE 501, 502(b), 502(d); Upjohn Co. v. United States, 449 U.S. 383 (1981).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** California also requires enough information to evaluate the objection, including a privilege log if necessary (CCP § 2031.240).
**Example:** D refuses to produce any emails between its engineers and in-house counsel and serves no log. On a motion to compel, the court should order a log; a blanket assertion does not satisfy Rule 26(b)(5)(A).

### R-CIV-066 — Work product
**Rule (write this):** Documents and tangible things prepared in anticipation of litigation or for trial by or for a party or its representative—including its attorney, consultant, insurer or agent—are discoverable only if they are otherwise discoverable and the requesting party shows substantial need and that it cannot, without undue hardship, obtain their substantial equivalent by other means. Even then the court must protect against disclosure of the mental impressions, conclusions, opinions or legal theories of an attorney or other representative, and any party or witness may obtain its own previous statement on request.

**Elements:** 1. Document or tangible thing. 2. Prepared in anticipation of litigation (because of the prospect of litigation). 3. By or for a party or its representative. 4. Ordinary work product: substantial need + undue hardship. 5. Opinion work product: near-absolute protection.
**Exceptions / defenses:** Routine business records prepared regardless of litigation are not work product. The underlying facts remain discoverable by interrogatory or deposition. Substantial need is typically shown when the witness has died, is unavailable or cannot recall, and the statement was contemporaneous.
**Authority:** FRCP 26(b)(3)(A)–(C); Hickman v. Taylor, 329 U.S. 495 (1947); Upjohn Co. v. United States, 449 U.S. 383 (1981).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** California protects attorney work product (absolute for impressions; otherwise qualified on unfair prejudice or injustice), the protection belongs to the attorney, and attorney-obtained witness statements get at least qualified protection (R-CIV-112).
**Example:** D's insurer interviewed an eyewitness the day after the crash; the witness has since died. P can obtain the factual statement on a showing of substantial need, but the adjuster's notes evaluating liability stay protected.

### R-CIV-067 — Discovery from experts
**Rule (write this):** A party may depose any expert whose opinions may be presented at trial, after any required report is provided. Drafts of expert reports, and communications between a party's attorney and a retained testifying expert, are protected, except communications about compensation, facts or data the attorney provided and the expert considered, and assumptions the attorney provided and the expert relied on. Facts known and opinions held by an expert retained in anticipation of litigation who is not expected to testify (a consulting expert) may be discovered only as provided in Rule 35(b) or on a showing of exceptional circumstances making it impracticable to obtain facts or opinions on the same subject by other means.

**Elements:** Classify the expert: testifying, consulting, or a percipient witness who happens to be an expert (ordinary discovery).
**Exceptions / defenses:** Exceptional circumstances include destruction of the object examined or no other available expert. The party seeking discovery pays the expert a reasonable fee.
**Authority:** FRCP 26(b)(4)(A)–(E).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same in federal court; California protects consultants through work product and exchanges testifying-expert information on demand.
**Example:** After a scaffold collapse, the contractor hires an engineer to examine the wreckage, then scraps it; she will not testify. The injured worker may discover her findings: exceptional circumstances, because the wreckage is gone.

### R-CIV-068 — Depositions, subpoenas and use of depositions at trial
**Rule (write this):** A party may depose any person on reasonable written notice, compelling a non-party's attendance by Rule 45 subpoena; without leave or stipulation each side may take 10 depositions, each deponent may be deposed once, and each deposition is limited to one day of 7 hours. A Rule 30(b)(6) notice requires an organization to designate witnesses to testify about information reasonably available to it. At trial, any deposition may be used to impeach the deponent; an adverse party's deposition (or that of its officer, director, managing agent or 30(b)(6) designee) may be used for any purpose; and any witness's deposition may be used if the witness is dead, more than 100 miles away or outside the United States (unless the offering party procured the absence), unable to attend because of age, illness, infirmity or imprisonment, or not procurable by subpoena, or in exceptional circumstances—subject to the rules of evidence.

**Elements:** Notice/subpoena; numerical and time limits; use rules.
**Exceptions / defenses:** Objections are stated concisely and non-suggestively; counsel may instruct a witness not to answer only to preserve a privilege, enforce a court-ordered limit or present a 30(d)(3) motion. A subpoena may command attendance only within 100 miles of where the person resides, is employed or regularly transacts business in person (or within the State for a party or party officer, or for trial without substantial expense).
**Authority:** FRCP 30(a)–(d), 32(a)(1)–(4), 45(a)–(d).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** California depositions are also time-limited (R-CIV-110).
**Example:** P's eyewitness now lives 300 miles from the courthouse. Her deposition may be read at trial instead of live testimony (32(a)(4)(B)).

### R-CIV-069 — Interrogatories, document requests and requests for admission
**Rule (write this):** Interrogatories may be served only on parties, are limited to 25 including discrete subparts, and must be answered separately and fully in writing under oath within 30 days, with objections stated specifically. Requests to produce documents, ESI or tangible things, or to inspect land, may be served on parties (non-parties by subpoena) and must be answered within 30 days, stating objections with specificity and whether responsive material is being withheld. A matter in a request for admission is admitted unless answered or objected to within 30 days, and an admission conclusively establishes the matter unless the court permits withdrawal because it would promote presentation of the merits without prejudicing the requesting party.

**Elements:** Device, limits, timing, form of response.
**Exceptions / defenses:** A party may answer an interrogatory by producing business records where the burden is equal (33(d)). Expenses for proving a matter wrongly denied (37(c)(2)).
**Authority:** FRCP 33(a)–(d), 34(a)–(c), 36(a)–(b), 37(c)(2).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** California limits specially prepared interrogatories and requests for admission to 35 each (R-CIV-110).
**Example:** D ignores P's request to admit that D's driver ran the red light. After 30 days it is admitted; D must move under 36(b) to withdraw.

### R-CIV-070 — Physical and mental examinations (Rule 35)
**Rule (write this):** On motion for good cause, with notice to all parties and the person to be examined, the court may order a party whose mental or physical condition is in controversy—or a person in its custody or legal control—to submit to an examination by a suitably licensed or certified examiner, and the order must specify the time, place, manner, conditions and scope of the examination and the examiner. "In controversy" and "good cause" require an affirmative showing, not conclusory allegations, but a plaintiff who asserts a physical or mental injury places that condition in controversy.

**Elements:** 1. Party (or person in its control). 2. Condition in controversy. 3. Good cause. 4. Court order specifying terms.
**Exceptions / defenses:** Courts divide over whether ordinary "garden-variety" emotional-distress damages put mental condition in controversy; most require more (a separate distress tort, a claimed psychiatric injury, or expert testimony). A party who requests the examiner's report must on request provide earlier reports of the same condition and waives privilege as to them (35(b)).
**Authority:** FRCP 35(a)–(b); Schlagenhauf v. Holder, 379 U.S. 104 (1964).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** California permits one physical examination of a personal-injury plaintiff on demand, without a court order; a mental examination needs leave and good cause (CCP §§ 2032.220, 2032.310–.320; R-CIV-110).
**Example:** P sues for a broken leg and "humiliation"; D seeks orthopedic and psychiatric exams. Orthopedic: granted. Psychiatric: likely denied unless P claims ongoing psychiatric injury or will call a mental-health expert.

### R-CIV-071 — ESI and failure to preserve (Rule 37(e))
**Rule (write this):** A party need not provide ESI from sources it identifies as not reasonably accessible because of undue burden or cost, but the court may order it for good cause. If ESI that should have been preserved in anticipation or conduct of litigation is lost because a party failed to take reasonable steps and it cannot be restored or replaced through additional discovery, the court, on finding prejudice, may order measures no greater than necessary to cure the prejudice; only on finding that the party acted with intent to deprive another party of the information's use may it presume the information was unfavorable, instruct the jury that it may or must so presume, or dismiss the action or enter default.

**Elements:** 1. Duty to preserve arose. 2. Failure to take reasonable steps. 3. Lost and irreplaceable. 4. Prejudice → curative measures; intent to deprive → severe measures.
**Exceptions / defenses:** Spoliation of tangible (non-ESI) evidence is governed by the court's inherent power.
**Authority:** FRCP 26(b)(2)(B), 26(f)(3)(C), 34(b)(2)(E), 37(e)(1)–(2).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same in federal court.
**Example:** After receiving a demand letter, D lets its auto-delete policy erase relevant emails through carelessness. The court may let P present evidence of the loss, but may not give an adverse-inference instruction without intent to deprive.

### R-CIV-072 — Discovery timing, the Rule 26(f) conference and protective orders
**Rule (write this):** A party may not seek discovery from any source before the Rule 26(f) conference, except by stipulation, court order or early Rule 34 requests. The parties must confer as soon as practicable, at least 21 days before the scheduling conference or scheduling order is due, to discuss their claims and defenses, settlement, disclosures, preservation, privilege issues and a proposed discovery plan. On motion certifying a good-faith attempt to resolve the dispute, the court may for good cause issue an order protecting a party or person from annoyance, embarrassment, oppression or undue burden or expense—for example, forbidding the discovery, limiting its scope or protecting trade secrets.

**Elements:** Timing; conference topics; protective order (certification + good cause + tailored relief).
**Exceptions / defenses:** Proceedings exempt from initial disclosure are exempt from the 26(d) moratorium.
**Authority:** FRCP 26(c)(1), 26(d)(1)–(2), 26(f)(1)–(3) [S-107].
**MBE vs. CA essay:** California protective orders: CCP §§ 2025.420, 2031.060 (good cause).
**Example:** A competitor-plaintiff demands D's secret formula. The court may limit disclosure to outside counsel's eyes only (26(c)(1)(G)).

### R-CIV-073 — Motion to compel and discovery sanctions
**Rule (write this):** After certifying a good-faith attempt to confer, a party may move to compel disclosure or discovery; an evasive or incomplete answer counts as a failure to answer. If the motion is granted, or the material is provided after filing, the court must award the movant reasonable expenses, including attorney's fees, unless the movant failed to confer first, the opposing position was substantially justified, or other circumstances make an award unjust. Disobeying a discovery order permits sanctions that include treating facts as established, precluding evidence, striking pleadings, staying the case, dismissal, default judgment and contempt; failing altogether to attend one's own deposition or to respond to interrogatories or document requests permits sanctions without a prior order; and undisclosed witnesses or information are excluded unless the failure was substantially justified or harmless.

**Elements:** 1. Meet-and-confer certification. 2. Failure or evasive response. 3. Order and expenses. 4. Graduated sanctions for violation.
**Exceptions / defenses:** Terminating sanctions generally require willfulness, bad faith or fault and consideration of lesser sanctions; contempt is not available for refusing a Rule 35 examination. Due process limits sanctions to matters related to the misconduct.
**Authority:** FRCP 37(a)(1), (a)(3)–(5), 37(b)(2)(A)–(C), 37(c)(1), 37(d); 26(g)(3); National Hockey League v. Metropolitan Hockey Club, Inc., 427 U.S. 639 (1976).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** California has a 45-day deadline for motions to compel further responses, a meet-and-confer declaration requirement and a mandatory $1,000 sanction for specified misconduct (R-CIV-110, R-CIV-111).
**Example:** The store refuses to produce maintenance logs without explanation; P's lawyer calls and writes twice, then moves to compel. The court should grant the motion and award P's fees.

### R-CIV-074 — Voluntary dismissal (Rule 41(a))
**Rule (write this):** A plaintiff may dismiss without a court order by filing a notice of dismissal before the opposing party serves either an answer or a motion for summary judgment, or by a stipulation signed by all parties who have appeared. Such a dismissal is without prejudice unless it states otherwise, except that a notice of dismissal operates as an adjudication on the merits if the plaintiff previously dismissed any federal- or state-court action based on or including the same claim. Otherwise the action may be dismissed only by court order on terms the court considers proper, and over a defendant's objection only if its earlier-pleaded counterclaim can remain pending for independent adjudication.

**Elements:** Timing (before answer/SJ motion); stipulation; court order; two-dismissal rule.
**Exceptions / defenses:** Class and derivative actions require court approval. A Rule 12(b)(6) motion does not cut off the right to dismiss by notice. The court may order payment of costs of a previously dismissed action (41(d)). The proposed December 2027 amendment to Rule 41(a) is not testable; learn the current rule [S-110].
**Authority:** FRCP 41(a)(1)(A)–(B), 41(a)(2), 41(c), 41(d).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** California lets a plaintiff dismiss any time before the actual commencement of trial (CCP § 581(c)).
**Example:** D files a 12(b)(6) motion; P files a notice of dismissal. Effective without court order and without prejudice—unless P had earlier dismissed the same claim in state court.

### R-CIV-075 — Involuntary dismissal (Rule 41(b))
**Rule (write this):** If the plaintiff fails to prosecute or to comply with the rules or a court order, a defendant may move to dismiss the action or any claim against it. Unless the dismissal order states otherwise, a dismissal under Rule 41(b)—and any dismissal not under Rule 41, except one for lack of jurisdiction, improper venue or failure to join a party under Rule 19—operates as an adjudication on the merits.

**Elements:** Ground (failure to prosecute or obey); effect (on the merits unless stated).
**Exceptions / defenses:** Courts usually consider lesser sanctions and warnings first. Dismissals for lack of jurisdiction, venue or joinder are without prejudice. "On the merits" in Rule 41(b) chiefly bars refiling in the same federal court; preclusion elsewhere follows Semtek (R-CIV-098).
**Authority:** FRCP 41(b); Semtek Int'l Inc. v. Lockheed Martin Corp., 531 U.S. 497 (2001).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** California has mandatory dismissal for failure to serve within 3 years or bring to trial within 5 years (CCP §§ 583.210, 583.310).
**Example:** P ignores three orders to attend status conferences; the court dismisses under 41(b) without saying "without prejudice." The dismissal is with prejudice.

### R-CIV-076 — Default and default judgment
**Rule (write this):** When a party against whom affirmative relief is sought has failed to plead or otherwise defend, the clerk must enter its default. The clerk may then enter a default judgment for a sum certain on affidavit against a defendant who has not appeared and is neither a minor nor incompetent; otherwise the court enters it, and a party who has appeared must be served with written notice of the application at least 7 days before the hearing. A default judgment must not differ in kind from, or exceed in amount, what is demanded in the pleadings; the court may set aside an entry of default for good cause and a default judgment under Rule 60(b).

**Elements:** Entry of default → default judgment (clerk or court) → limits → relief.
**Exceptions / defenses:** Good-cause factors include willfulness, prejudice to the plaintiff and a meritorious defense. A judgment entered without personal jurisdiction or proper service is void (60(b)(4)), but the motion must be made within a reasonable time (R-CIV-090).
**Authority:** FRCP 55(a)–(c), 54(c), 60(b).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** California relief from default: CCP § 473(b) (R-CIV-107).
**Example:** P's complaint demands $100,000; after D defaults, the court enters judgment for $250,000. The excess violates Rule 54(c).

### R-CIV-077 — Pretrial conferences, scheduling and pretrial orders
**Rule (write this):** Except in exempted categories, the judge must issue a scheduling order as soon as practicable and, absent good cause for delay, within the earlier of 90 days after any defendant has been served or 60 days after any defendant has appeared; it must limit the time to join parties, amend pleadings, complete discovery and file motions, and may address disclosure, ESI, the timing and method of privilege logs, and Rule 502 agreements. A schedule may be modified only for good cause and with the judge's consent; an order after a pretrial conference controls the course of the action, and the order after a final pretrial conference may be modified only to prevent manifest injustice. The court may sanction a party or attorney who fails to appear, is substantially unprepared, does not participate in good faith, or disobeys a pretrial order. In multidistrict litigation the transferee court should hold an initial management conference and may order a pre-conference report (Rule 16.1).

**Elements:** Scheduling order; modification standards; sanctions; MDL management.
**Exceptions / defenses:** Rule 16.1 is flexible—it does not mandate specific deadlines.
**Authority:** FRCP 16(a)–(f), 16.1 [S-107] [S-275].
**MBE vs. CA essay:** California uses case management conferences under the California Rules of Court.
**Example:** The final pretrial order lists the issues for trial. At trial D tries to add a limitations defense never listed. The court may refuse: modification only to prevent manifest injustice.

## IV. Jury trials

### R-CIV-078 — Seventh Amendment right to a civil jury
**Rule (write this):** In federal court the Seventh Amendment preserves the right to jury trial in suits at common law—actions analogous to those tried in English courts of law in 1791, rather than equity or admiralty. The court compares the action to 18th-century actions and, more importantly, asks whether the remedy sought is legal (generally money damages) or equitable (injunction, specific performance, rescission, accounting); the right extends to new statutory claims seeking legal relief, but not to matters within the public-rights exception.

**Elements:** 1. Historical analogue. 2. Nature of the remedy (weighted more heavily). 3. Claim by claim.
**Exceptions / defenses:** The Seventh Amendment does not bind state courts. Judges decide some issues within jury cases (e.g., patent claim construction—Markman; the amount of a statutory civil penalty—Tull).
**Authority:** U.S. Const. amend. VII; Curtis v. Loether, 415 U.S. 189 (1974); Tull v. United States, 481 U.S. 412 (1987); Chauffeurs, Teamsters & Helpers Local No. 391 v. Terry, 494 U.S. 558 (1990); Markman v. Westview Instruments, Inc., 517 U.S. 370 (1996); SEC v. Jarkesy (2024).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Federal: 1791 test. California: common law as of 1850 under the state constitution (R-CIV-116).
**Example:** A buyer sues for fraud damages and for specific performance of a land contract. Jury on the fraud damages; specific performance is equitable and tried to the judge.
> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: PRC people's assessors (人民陪审员) sit with judges; there is no lay jury deciding facts alone, so the US legal/equitable split has no PRC counterpart (approx.).

### R-CIV-079 — Mixed legal and equitable claims
**Rule (write this):** When legal and equitable claims share common issues of fact, the jury must decide the legal claims and the common issues first, so that the judge's ruling on the equitable claims does not preclude the jury's determination, except in the most imperative circumstances; a party cannot lose the jury right through the order of trial or by labeling a legal claim equitable. In a shareholder derivative suit, the shareholder has a jury right on the corporation's underlying legal claims.

**Elements:** 1. Identify legal and equitable claims. 2. Common factual issues. 3. Sequence: jury first.
**Exceptions / defenses:** An "accounting" for money owed is legal if the accounts are not so complicated that only equity can handle them (Dairy Queen).
**Authority:** Beacon Theatres, Inc. v. Westover, 359 U.S. 500 (1959); Dairy Queen, Inc. v. Wood, 369 U.S. 469 (1962); Ross v. Bernhard, 396 U.S. 531 (1970).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** California courts often try equitable issues first and may let those findings bind the legal issues—the opposite of Beacon Theatres [VERIFY V-158].
**Example:** A landowner seeks an injunction against a neighbor's encroachment and damages for trespass. Ownership of the strip is common to both; the jury decides it first on the damages claim.

### R-CIV-080 — Jury demand, waiver and removed cases
**Rule (write this):** A party must serve a written jury demand no later than 14 days after the last pleading directed to the issue is served, and file it; failure to do so waives the right. A demand may be limited to specified issues and, once made, may be withdrawn only if the parties consent; the court may on motion order a jury on any issue despite a missed demand, or use an advisory jury. In a removed case, a party who expressly demanded a jury under state law before removal need not renew the demand, and if all pleadings were served before removal a party must demand within 14 days after it files, or is served with, the notice of removal.

**Elements:** Timely written demand; scope; waiver; Rule 39(b) discretion; removal rules.
**Exceptions / defenses:** The proposed December 2027 amendment to Rule 81(c) is not testable; apply the current rule [S-110].
**Authority:** FRCP 38(b)–(d), 39(a)–(c), 81(c)(3).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** California waiver occurs by, among other things, failing to demand a jury or to deposit advance jury fees on time (CCP § 631).
**Example:** The last pleading (the answer to a counterclaim) is served April 1; P demands a jury April 25. Untimely; P must ask the court to exercise its Rule 39(b) discretion.

### R-CIV-081 — Jury size, unanimity and polling
**Rule (write this):** A federal civil jury must begin with at least 6 and no more than 12 members, and each juror participates in the verdict unless excused for good cause. Unless the parties stipulate otherwise, the verdict must be unanimous and returned by a jury of at least 6. After a verdict is returned and before the jury is discharged, the court must poll the jurors individually on a party's request, and if a lack of unanimity or assent appears it may direct further deliberation or order a new trial.

**Elements:** Size; unanimity; polling.
**Exceptions / defenses:** Parties may stipulate to a non-unanimous verdict or a smaller jury.
**Authority:** FRCP 47(c), 48(a)–(c).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** California civil verdicts require only three-fourths of the jury (Cal. Const. art. I, § 16 [S-260]; R-CIV-116).
**Example:** Seven jurors are seated; one falls ill and is excused; six return a unanimous verdict. Valid.

### R-CIV-082 — Jury selection: voir dire, challenges for cause, peremptory challenges
**Rule (write this):** The court may examine prospective jurors itself or permit the parties to do so. A prospective juror may be removed for cause for actual bias, shown by admission or by facts, or for implied bias, such as a relationship with a party or a financial interest in the outcome; challenges for cause are unlimited. Each party has three peremptory challenges, with multiple plaintiffs or defendants treated as a single party or given additional challenges in the court's discretion, but peremptory challenges may not be exercised on the basis of race or sex, and an objection is resolved by Batson's three steps: a prima facie showing, a neutral explanation, and the court's determination of purposeful discrimination.

**Elements:** Voir dire; cause (actual/implied bias); peremptories (number; Batson).
**Exceptions / defenses:** Erroneously denying a challenge for cause is less likely to be reversible if the party cured it with a peremptory and no biased juror sat; preserve the objection.
**Authority:** FRCP 47(a)–(c); 28 U.S.C. §§ 1861, 1865, 1870; Batson v. Kentucky, 476 U.S. 79 (1986); Edmonson v. Leesville Concrete Co., 500 U.S. 614 (1991); J.E.B. v. Alabama ex rel. T.B., 511 U.S. 127 (1994).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** California civil cases give each party six peremptory challenges in a two-party case, with additional challenges allocated by side when there are more parties (CCP § 231(c) [S-271] [VERIFY V-154]); CCP § 231.7 creates a statutory procedure for bias-based peremptory challenges, reported to apply in civil trials from 2026 [VERIFY V-029].
**Example:** A prospective juror says she worked for D's parent company for ten years and "would find it hard to rule against them." A challenge for cause should be granted; refusing it is error.

### R-CIV-083 — Jury instructions: requests and objections
**Rule (write this):** A party may file written requests for jury instructions at the close of the evidence or at an earlier reasonable time the court orders. Before instructing the jury and before final arguments, the court must inform the parties of its proposed instructions and its action on their requests and give them an opportunity to object on the record and out of the jury's hearing. A party who objects must state distinctly the matter objected to and the grounds; an instructional error is preserved only by a proper objection (or, for a refused instruction, a proper request plus an objection unless the court definitively rejected it on the record), and an unpreserved error is reviewed only for plain error affecting substantial rights.

**Elements:** Request; notice of proposed instructions; specific, timely objection; preservation.
**Exceptions / defenses:** Plain-error review is narrow.
**Authority:** FRCP 51(a)–(d).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same in federal court.
**Example:** D's counsel thinks the comparative-fault instruction misstates the law but says nothing until after the verdict. On appeal only plain-error review is available.

## V. Motions

### R-CIV-084 — Motions addressed to the face of the pleadings
**Rule (write this):** A Rule 12(b)(6) motion tests whether the complaint, taking its well-pleaded facts as true and drawing reasonable inferences for the plaintiff, states a plausible claim; the court may consider the complaint, documents attached to or incorporated in it, and matters subject to judicial notice. After the pleadings close, a Rule 12(c) motion for judgment on the pleadings applies the same standard, and if matters outside the pleadings are presented and not excluded, the motion must be treated as one for summary judgment, with a reasonable opportunity for all parties to present pertinent material. A party may also move for a more definite statement of a pleading so vague or ambiguous that it cannot reasonably respond, and to strike an insufficient defense or redundant, immaterial, impertinent or scandalous matter.

**Elements:** 12(b)(6)/(c): plausibility; 12(d) conversion; 12(e); 12(f).
**Exceptions / defenses:** An affirmative defense such as limitations supports 12(b)(6) dismissal only if it is clear from the face of the complaint. Dismissal is usually with leave to amend unless amendment would be futile.
**Authority:** FRCP 12(b)(6), 12(c)–(f); Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly, 550 U.S. 544 (2007); Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662 (2009).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** California equivalents: general demurrer, special demurrer for uncertainty, motion to strike and motion for judgment on the pleadings (R-CIV-106, R-CIV-107).
**Example:** D moves to dismiss and attaches a release P signed. The court must exclude the release or convert the motion to summary judgment and let P respond.

### R-CIV-085 — Summary judgment: the standard
**Rule (write this):** The court shall grant summary judgment if the movant shows that there is no genuine dispute as to any material fact and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. A fact is material if it might affect the outcome under the governing law, and a dispute is genuine if the evidence is such that a reasonable jury could return a verdict for the non-moving party; the court views the evidence in the light most favorable to the non-movant, draws all reasonable inferences in its favor, and does not weigh evidence or judge credibility.

**Elements:** 1. Materiality (substantive law). 2. Genuineness (reasonable jury, applying the actual evidentiary burden). 3. Entitlement to judgment as a matter of law.
**Exceptions / defenses:** A version of events blatantly contradicted by the record (e.g., a video) need not be credited (Scott v. Harris). Implausible inferences need not be drawn (Matsushita).
**Authority:** FRCP 56(a); Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242 (1986); Matsushita Elec. Indus. Co. v. Zenith Radio Corp., 475 U.S. 574 (1986); Scott v. Harris, 550 U.S. 372 (2007); Tolan v. Cotton, 572 U.S. 650 (2014).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** California's standard is similar ("no triable issue as to any material fact"), but its procedure differs sharply (R-CIV-113).
**Example:** P swears the light was green; D's three witnesses say it was red. Genuine dispute: credibility is for the jury, so summary judgment is denied.
> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: PRC procedure has no summary judgment; the court decides after hearing. In the US, SJ exists because the jury, not the judge, finds disputed facts (approx.).

### R-CIV-086 — Summary judgment: burdens and procedure
**Rule (write this):** A movant that will not bear the burden of proof at trial may carry its initial burden either by producing evidence that negates an essential element or by showing that the non-movant lacks evidence to prove one; the non-movant must then go beyond the pleadings and cite particular materials in the record—depositions, documents, declarations, admissions, interrogatory answers—showing a genuine dispute. Supporting and opposing declarations must be made on personal knowledge and set out facts that would be admissible in evidence. Unless a local rule or order provides otherwise, a motion may be filed any time until 30 days after the close of all discovery, and if the non-movant shows by declaration that it cannot yet present facts essential to its opposition, the court may defer or deny the motion or allow time for discovery.

**Elements:** Initial burden; shifting burden; admissible-form evidence; timing; Rule 56(d) relief.
**Exceptions / defenses:** Summary judgment may be sought on part of a claim or defense; the court may grant it on grounds not raised after notice (56(f)).
**Authority:** FRCP 56(a)–(g); Celotex Corp. v. Catrett, 477 U.S. 317 (1986).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** California requires a separate statement of undisputed facts, 81 days' notice and summary adjudication only of whole causes of action (R-CIV-113).
**Example:** After discovery, an asbestos plaintiff has no evidence of exposure to D's product; D moves, pointing to P's interrogatory answers. P must cite admissible evidence of exposure or lose (Celotex).

### R-CIV-087 — Judgment as a matter of law (directed verdict)
**Rule (write this):** If a party has been fully heard on an issue during a jury trial and the court finds that a reasonable jury would not have a legally sufficient evidentiary basis to find for that party on the issue, the court may resolve the issue against it and grant judgment as a matter of law on any claim or defense that depends on it. The motion may be made at any time before the case is submitted to the jury and must specify the judgment sought and the law and facts entitling the movant to it; the court considers all the evidence in the light most favorable to the non-movant and may not weigh evidence or make credibility determinations.

**Elements:** 1. Party fully heard. 2. No legally sufficient evidentiary basis. 3. Timing and specificity.
**Exceptions / defenses:** JMOL does not violate the Seventh Amendment (Galloway).
**Authority:** FRCP 50(a)(1)–(2); Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Products, Inc., 530 U.S. 133 (2000); Galloway v. United States, 319 U.S. 372 (1943).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** California uses nonsuit (CCP § 581c) and directed verdict (CCP § 630) (R-CIV-117).
**Example:** In a seat-defect case, P's expert testifies that the seat failed below industry standards and D's experts disagree. A reasonable jury could find for P, so denying D's directed-verdict motion is correct.

### R-CIV-088 — Renewed motion for judgment as a matter of law (JNOV)
**Rule (write this):** If the court does not grant a Rule 50(a) motion, the movant may file a renewed motion no later than 28 days after entry of judgment, limited to the grounds raised in the pre-verdict motion, and may join an alternative request for a new trial. The court may allow judgment on the verdict, order a new trial or direct entry of judgment as a matter of law, and if it grants the renewed motion it must also rule conditionally on any new-trial motion. Without a proper pre-verdict motion and a timely renewed motion, an appellate court may not review the sufficiency of the evidence or direct judgment.

**Elements:** 1. Pre-verdict 50(a) motion. 2. Renewal within 28 days. 3. Same grounds. 4. Same standard as 50(a).
**Exceptions / defenses:** The 28-day period cannot be extended (Rule 6(b)(2)). A purely legal issue rejected at summary judgment is preserved for appeal without a Rule 50 motion (Dupree v. Younger (2023)) [VERIFY V-161].
**Authority:** FRCP 50(b), 50(c), 50(e), 6(b)(2); Unitherm Food Systems, Inc. v. Swift-Eckrich, Inc., 546 U.S. 394 (2006); Weisgram v. Marley Co., 528 U.S. 440 (2000).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** California JNOV does not require a prior directed-verdict motion (CCP § 629; R-CIV-117).
**Example:** D never moved for JMOL before the case went to the jury. After a verdict for P, D's "renewed" motion must be denied—there is nothing to renew.

### R-CIV-089 — New trial, remittitur and motions to alter or amend
**Rule (write this):** After a jury trial, the court may grant a new trial on all or some issues for any reason for which a new trial has heretofore been granted in an action at law in federal court—most commonly a verdict against the clear weight of the evidence, excessive or inadequate damages, prejudicial legal error, misconduct by jurors, parties or counsel, or newly discovered evidence; unlike on JMOL, the judge may weigh the evidence and assess credibility, but may not simply substitute its own view. A motion for a new trial, or to alter or amend the judgment, must be filed within 28 days after entry of judgment, and the court may order a new trial on its own within that time, stating its reasons. For an excessive verdict the court may offer remittitur—a new trial unless the plaintiff accepts a reduced award—but additur is not permitted in federal court.

**Elements:** Ground; timing (28 days, no extension); remittitur option.
**Exceptions / defenses:** Errors that did not affect substantial rights are harmless (Rule 61). In diversity cases the state's excessiveness standard may apply (Gasperini).
**Authority:** FRCP 59(a)–(e), 6(b)(2), 61; Dimick v. Schiedt, 293 U.S. 474 (1935); Gasperini v. Center for Humanities, Inc., 518 U.S. 415 (1996).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** California permits both additur and remittitur (CCP § 662.5) and requires written reasons (CCP § 657; R-CIV-117).
**Example:** A jury awards $5 million for a broken arm that fully healed. The court may order a new trial on damages unless P accepts $500,000; it could not raise a $5,000 award.

### R-CIV-090 — Relief from a final judgment (Rule 60)
**Rule (write this):** The court may correct a clerical mistake or one arising from oversight or omission at any time. On motion and just terms, it may relieve a party from a final judgment for (1) mistake, inadvertence, surprise or excusable neglect; (2) newly discovered evidence that, with reasonable diligence, could not have been discovered in time to move for a new trial; (3) fraud, misrepresentation or misconduct by an opposing party; (4) a void judgment; (5) a judgment that has been satisfied, released or discharged, rests on an earlier judgment that has been reversed or vacated, or whose prospective application is no longer equitable; or (6) any other reason that justifies relief. The motion must be made within a reasonable time—and for reasons (1) to (3) no more than a year after entry of the judgment—and the reasonable-time limit applies even to a motion attacking a judgment as void.

**Elements:** Ground; timing; just terms.
**Exceptions / defenses:** "Mistake" includes a judge's legal error (Kemp). Clause (6) requires extraordinary circumstances and is unavailable if another clause applies. A motion does not suspend the judgment. An independent action and relief for fraud on the court are not limited by Rule 60(c) (60(d)).
**Authority:** FRCP 60(a)–(d); Ackermann v. United States, 340 U.S. 193 (1950); Pioneer Investment Services Co. v. Brunswick Associates, 507 U.S. 380 (1993); Kemp v. United States, 596 U.S. 528 (2022); Coney Island Auto Parts Unlimited, Inc. v. Burton (2026) [S-252] [VERIFY V-034].
**MBE vs. CA essay:** California relief from default and mistake: CCP § 473(b) (six months; R-CIV-107).
**Example:** Ten months after a defense verdict, P learns that D concealed internal test reports P had requested in discovery. A Rule 60(b)(3) motion is timely (within a year); most courts require P to prove the misconduct by clear and convincing evidence and show it prevented a full and fair presentation of P's case.

## VI. Verdicts and judgments

### R-CIV-091 — Verdict forms and inconsistent verdicts
**Rule (write this):** The court may require a special verdict, consisting of written findings on each issue of fact, or a general verdict accompanied by answers to written questions. If the answers are consistent with each other but one or more is inconsistent with the general verdict, the court may enter judgment on the answers notwithstanding the general verdict, direct the jury to consider further, or order a new trial; if the answers are inconsistent with each other and one or more is also inconsistent with the general verdict, the court must not enter judgment and must direct further consideration or order a new trial. A party waives jury trial on an issue omitted from a special verdict unless it demands submission before the jury retires, and the court may then make the finding itself.

**Elements:** Identify form; classify inconsistency; choose the permitted response.
**Exceptions / defenses:** Most courts require inconsistency objections before the jury is discharged. Judgment must be set out in a separate document (Rule 58).
**Authority:** FRCP 49(a)–(b), 48(c), 58.
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same in federal court.
**Example:** The general verdict is for P, but the jury's answers find P 60% at fault in a modified comparative-fault State. The answers are consistent with each other but not with the verdict: the court may enter judgment for D on the answers, resubmit, or order a new trial.

### R-CIV-092 — Juror nondisclosure and juror testimony about the verdict
**Rule (write this):** To obtain a new trial because a juror failed to disclose information during voir dire, a party must show that the juror failed to answer honestly a material question and that a correct response would have provided a valid basis for a challenge for cause. In an inquiry into a verdict's validity, a juror may not testify about statements made during deliberations, the effect of anything on any juror's vote, or jurors' mental processes, but may testify about whether extraneous prejudicial information was improperly brought to the jury's attention, whether an outside influence was improperly brought to bear, or whether there was a mistake in entering the verdict on the form.

**Elements:** McDonough test (dishonest answer + valid for-cause basis); FRE 606(b) limits on proof.
**Exceptions / defenses:** Juror testimony about deliberations cannot be used to show that a juror lied during voir dire (Warger v. Shauers, 574 U.S. 40 (2014)).
**Authority:** McDonough Power Equipment, Inc. v. Greenwood, 464 U.S. 548 (1984); FRE 606(b).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** California admits evidence of statements, conduct, conditions or events likely to have influenced the verdict improperly but not jurors' mental processes (Evid. Code § 1150); juror concealment is jury misconduct and a new-trial ground (CCP § 657).
**Example:** After the verdict, P learns that a juror falsely answered "no" when asked whether she had ever sued D's company. If a truthful answer would have supported a for-cause challenge, a new trial is warranted.

### R-CIV-093 — Findings and conclusions in bench trials
**Rule (write this):** In an action tried on the facts without a jury or with an advisory jury, the court must find the facts specially and state its conclusions of law separately, on the record or in a written opinion; findings are also required when granting or refusing an interlocutory injunction, but not when ruling on Rule 12 or 56 motions. Findings of fact, whether based on oral or other evidence, must not be set aside unless clearly erroneous, and the reviewing court must give due regard to the trial court's opportunity to judge witnesses' credibility. A party may move to amend the findings within 28 days after judgment, and the court may enter judgment on partial findings once a party has been fully heard on an issue.

**Elements:** Special findings; separate conclusions; clear-error review; 52(b) and 52(c).
**Exceptions / defenses:** A party may challenge the sufficiency of the evidence supporting findings on appeal without having objected below (52(a)(5)).
**Authority:** FRCP 52(a)(1)–(6), 52(b), 52(c); Anderson v. City of Bessemer City, 470 U.S. 564 (1985).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** California's analogue is the statement of decision on request (CCP § 632).
**Example:** After a bench trial the judge simply announces "judgment for D." The court of appeals will vacate and remand for findings under Rule 52(a)(1).

### R-CIV-094 — Claim preclusion (res judicata)
**Rule (write this):** A valid final judgment on the merits bars the same parties, or those in privity with them, from relitigating the same claim, including every theory and form of relief arising out of the same transaction or series of connected transactions that was or could have been raised. A judgment for the plaintiff merges the claim into the judgment; a judgment for the defendant bars it.

**Elements:** 1. Same claim (transactional test). 2. Same parties or privies, in the same alignment. 3. Valid, final judgment on the merits.
**Exceptions / defenses:** Dismissals for lack of jurisdiction, improper venue or nonjoinder are not on the merits; claims the first court lacked jurisdiction to hear, claims the parties agreed to split, and claims the court expressly reserved are not barred. A defense the defendant failed to raise is not barred as a defense in a later suit about different conduct (Lucky Brand). Default judgments and 12(b)(6) dismissals are on the merits. Under the federal (majority) rule a judgment is final for preclusion when entered, even if an appeal is pending.
**Authority:** Restatement (Second) of Judgments §§ 17–19, 24, 26; Federated Department Stores, Inc. v. Moitie, 452 U.S. 394 (1981); Lucky Brand Dungarees, Inc. v. Marcel Fashions Group, Inc., 590 U.S. 405 (2020).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Federal/MBE: transactional test. California: primary-rights theory and finality only after appeal (R-CIV-114, R-CIV-115).
**Example:** P sues a moving company for breach of contract after its crew dropped P's piano, and loses on the merits. P's second suit for negligence over the same piano is barred—same transaction, new theory.
> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: Roughly the PRC idea of 既判力 / 一事不再理, but the US transactional test also bars theories that were never raised (approx.).

### R-CIV-095 — Issue preclusion (collateral estoppel)
**Rule (write this):** When an issue of fact or law is actually litigated and determined by a valid and final judgment, and the determination is essential to the judgment, the determination is conclusive in a later action, whether on the same or a different claim, against a party (or privy) who had a full and fair opportunity to litigate it.

**Elements:** 1. Same issue. 2. Actually litigated and determined. 3. Valid, final judgment. 4. Essential to the judgment. 5. Asserted against a party or privy who had a full and fair opportunity. 6. Mutuality, or a permitted non-mutual use (R-CIV-096).
**Exceptions / defenses:** Issues resolved by default, admission or stipulation are not "actually litigated." Independent alternative grounds are not preclusive under the Restatement unless affirmed on appeal. No preclusion where the second action has a lower burden of proof (e.g., a civil claim after a criminal acquittal), the party lacked incentive or procedural opportunities, or the law has changed.
**Authority:** Restatement (Second) of Judgments §§ 27–29; Montana v. United States, 440 U.S. 147 (1979); B&B Hardware, Inc. v. Hargis Industries, Inc., 575 U.S. 138 (2015).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** California: Lucido elements plus a public-policy check (R-CIV-115).
**Example:** In Suit 1 for March rent, the court rules the lease valid and enforces it. In Suit 2 for April rent—a different claim—the tenant cannot relitigate the lease's validity.

### R-CIV-096 — Non-mutual issue preclusion
**Rule (write this):** Most courts no longer require mutuality. Defensive non-mutual issue preclusion—a new defendant using a prior finding against a plaintiff who lost it—is generally permitted. Offensive non-mutual issue preclusion—a new plaintiff using a prior finding against a defendant who lost it—rests in the trial court's discretion and should be denied where the plaintiff could easily have joined the earlier action, the defendant had little incentive to litigate the first action vigorously, the judgment is inconsistent with earlier judgments, or the second action offers procedural opportunities unavailable in the first.

**Elements:** Who is using preclusion, against whom; fairness factors for offensive use.
**Exceptions / defenses:** Offensive non-mutual estoppel is not available against the federal government (United States v. Mendoza, 464 U.S. 154 (1984)). Preclusion can never be used against a non-party who did not have a full and fair opportunity.
**Authority:** Blonder-Tongue Laboratories, Inc. v. University of Illinois Foundation, 402 U.S. 313 (1971); Parklane Hosiery Co. v. Shore, 439 U.S. 322 (1979).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** California abandoned mutuality in Bernhard v. Bank of America (1942) 19 Cal.2d 807 and applies similar fairness limits.
**Example:** The SEC obtains a finding that D's proxy statement was misleading. Shareholders suing later may use it offensively unless a Parklane factor cuts against it—for example, if they deliberately waited out the first case.

### R-CIV-097 — Who is bound: non-party preclusion
**Rule (write this):** A judgment generally binds only the parties. A non-party is bound only if it (1) agreed to be bound; (2) had a pre-existing substantive legal relationship with a party, such as successive owners of property, bailor and bailee, or assignor and assignee; (3) was adequately represented by a party with the same interests in a properly conducted class action or a suit by a trustee, guardian or other fiduciary; (4) assumed control over the prior litigation; (5) is relitigating through a proxy; or (6) is bound under a special statutory scheme, such as bankruptcy or probate. "Virtual representation" is not a basis for non-party preclusion.

**Elements:** Fit the non-party into one of the six categories or it is not bound.
**Exceptions / defenses:** Due process forbids binding a non-party without adequate representation (Hansberry).
**Authority:** Taylor v. Sturgell, 553 U.S. 880 (2008); Hansberry v. Lee, 311 U.S. 32 (1940); Martin v. Wilks, 490 U.S. 755 (1989).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same principle; California uses "privity," judged by whether the non-party's interests were represented so that binding it is fair.
**Example:** Friend A loses a records-access suit. Friend B, using the same lawyer, files an identical suit. B is not bound unless one of the six categories applies, such as B acting as A's agent (Taylor).

### R-CIV-098 — Preclusion across court systems
**Rule (write this):** Federal courts must give a state-court judgment the same preclusive effect it would receive in the courts of the State that rendered it. The preclusive effect of a federal-question judgment is governed by federal law, and the claim-preclusive effect of a federal diversity judgment is governed by federal common law, which ordinarily adopts the preclusion law of the State in which the rendering federal court sat unless that law is incompatible with federal interests.

**Elements:** Identify the rendering court; apply its preclusion law (via § 1738 or Semtek).
**Exceptions / defenses:** Congress may create exceptions by statute.
**Authority:** U.S. Const. art. IV, § 1; 28 U.S.C. § 1738; Kremer v. Chemical Construction Corp., 456 U.S. 461 (1982); Marrese v. American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons, 470 U.S. 373 (1985); Semtek Int'l Inc. v. Lockheed Martin Corp., 531 U.S. 497 (2001).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** If the first judgment was a California state judgment, apply California's primary-rights law even in federal court; if it was a federal diversity judgment rendered in California, Semtek usually points to California law too—discuss both tests.
**Example:** A federal court in California dismisses P's diversity claim as time-barred "on the merits." P refiles in Maryland under a longer limitations period. Whether the dismissal bars P depends on how California law treats limitations dismissals (Semtek).

## VII. Appealability and review

### R-CIV-099 — Final judgment rule, Rule 54(b) and appeal deadlines
**Rule (write this):** Courts of appeals have jurisdiction of appeals from all final decisions of the district courts—decisions that end the litigation on the merits and leave nothing for the court to do but execute the judgment. In an action with multiple claims or parties, the court may direct entry of a final judgment on one or more, but fewer than all, claims or parties only if it expressly determines that there is no just reason for delay. A notice of appeal must be filed within 30 days after entry of the judgment (60 days if the United States is a party), and a timely motion under Rule 50(b), 52(b) or 59, or under Rule 60 if filed within 28 days, postpones the time until the motion is decided.

**Elements:** Finality; 54(b) certification; FRAP 4 timing and tolling.
**Exceptions / defenses:** R-CIV-100 and R-CIV-101. The statutory appeal deadline is jurisdictional (Bowles). Consolidated cases keep their separate identities for finality (Hall v. Hall).
**Authority:** 28 U.S.C. § 1291; FRCP 54(b), 58; FRAP 4(a)(1), 4(a)(4); Catlin v. United States, 324 U.S. 229 (1945); Bowles v. Russell, 551 U.S. 205 (2007); Hall v. Hall, 584 U.S. 59 (2018).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** California's one-final-judgment rule is similar, but it lists appealable orders by statute and allows 60 days after notice of entry (R-CIV-118).
**Example:** The court grants D summary judgment on one of P's three claims and sets the rest for trial. No appeal lies unless the court enters a Rule 54(b) judgment with an express no-just-reason-for-delay determination.
> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: The PRC two-instance system (两审终审) allows appeal of right from first-instance judgments and certain rulings; US federal appeals generally wait for a final judgment (approx.).

### R-CIV-100 — Interlocutory appeals by statute and rule
**Rule (write this):** A party may immediately appeal interlocutory orders granting, continuing, modifying, refusing or dissolving injunctions, or refusing to dissolve or modify them, and orders appointing receivers. A district judge may certify an order for immediate appeal if it involves a controlling question of law as to which there is substantial ground for difference of opinion and an immediate appeal may materially advance the ultimate termination of the litigation, and the court of appeals may then permit the appeal in its discretion if application is made within 10 days. Orders granting or denying class certification may be appealed by permission under Rule 23(f).

**Elements:** § 1292(a)(1) injunction orders; § 1292(b) double discretion; Rule 23(f).
**Exceptions / defenses:** TROs are generally not appealable (Sampson v. Murray). An order that has the practical effect of refusing an injunction and threatens serious, perhaps irreparable, consequences is appealable (Carson). A § 1292(b) application does not stay the case unless ordered. Orders refusing to compel arbitration are appealable (9 U.S.C. § 16).
**Authority:** 28 U.S.C. § 1292(a)(1)–(2), (b); FRCP 23(f); Carson v. American Brands, Inc., 450 U.S. 79 (1981); 9 U.S.C. § 16.
**MBE vs. CA essay:** California injunction orders are also appealable (CCP § 904.1).
**Example:** After removal, the district court preliminarily bars a franchisor from terminating a franchise. The franchisor may appeal at once under § 1292(a)(1), but must file its notice within 30 days of the order.

### R-CIV-101 — Collateral order doctrine and mandamus
**Rule (write this):** A small class of non-final orders is treated as final under § 1291 if the order (1) conclusively determines the disputed question, (2) resolves an important issue completely separate from the merits, and (3) would be effectively unreviewable on appeal from a final judgment. The class is narrow: it covers denials of qualified immunity that turn on issues of law (not fact-based sufficiency rulings: Johnson v. Jones, 515 U.S. 304 (1995)), Eleventh Amendment immunity and double-jeopardy claims, but not orders compelling disclosure of privileged material, disqualifying counsel, refusing to enforce a forum-selection clause, denying class certification, or denying a federal contractor's derivative sovereign immunity. Mandamus is available only to correct a clear abuse of discretion or usurpation of power, where the petitioner has no other adequate means of relief and its right to the writ is clear and indisputable.

**Elements:** Cohen's three requirements, applied to the category of order, not the individual case.
**Exceptions / defenses:** Appeal after final judgment generally suffices to review a disclosure order; a party may also seek § 1292(b) certification or mandamus, or defy the order and appeal a contempt sanction (Mohawk).
**Authority:** Cohen v. Beneficial Industrial Loan Corp., 337 U.S. 541 (1949); Coopers & Lybrand v. Livesay, 437 U.S. 463 (1978); Mitchell v. Forsyth, 472 U.S. 511 (1985); Richardson-Merrell Inc. v. Koller, 472 U.S. 424 (1985); Lauro Lines s.r.l. v. Chasser, 490 U.S. 495 (1989); Will v. Hallock, 546 U.S. 345 (2006); Mohawk Industries, Inc. v. Carpenter, 558 U.S. 100 (2009); GEO Group, Inc. v. Menocal (2026) [S-253] [VERIFY V-150]; 28 U.S.C. § 1651; Cheney v. U.S. District Court, 542 U.S. 367 (2004).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** California uses writ petitions for such rulings (R-CIV-118).
**Example:** The trial court orders D to produce memos D claims are privileged. No collateral-order appeal (Mohawk); D may seek § 1292(b) certification or mandamus.

### R-CIV-102 — Standards of review
**Rule (write this):** Appellate courts review conclusions of law de novo; a judge's findings of fact for clear error, with due regard for credibility determinations; discretionary rulings on evidence, discovery, sanctions, new-trial motions and case management for abuse of discretion; and a jury's verdict only for whether a reasonable jury could have reached it on the evidence, because the Seventh Amendment forbids re-examining facts tried by a jury except according to the rules of the common law. Errors that do not affect substantial rights are harmless, and errors not preserved below receive only plain-error review.

**Elements:** Classify the ruling; apply its standard; then harmless-error analysis.
**Exceptions / defenses:** Mixed questions are reviewed according to whether they are primarily legal or factual.
**Authority:** U.S. Const. amend. VII (Reexamination Clause); FRCP 52(a)(6), 61; 28 U.S.C. § 2111; Anderson v. City of Bessemer City, 470 U.S. 564 (1985); Cooter & Gell v. Hartmarx Corp., 496 U.S. 384 (1990); Gasperini v. Center for Humanities, Inc., 518 U.S. 415 (1996).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** California uses similar standards (de novo, substantial evidence, abuse of discretion).
**Example:** On appeal from a bench trial, the appellant argues that the judge believed the wrong witness. Reversal is unlikely: credibility findings get special deference under clear-error review.

## California Code of Civil Procedure rules (essays)

> Use these when the essay places the case in a California superior court or asks you to compare the FRCP with the CCP [S-084]. Side-by-side tables are in `ca-distinctions.md`.

### R-CIV-103 — California subject-matter jurisdiction: limited and unlimited civil cases
**Rule (write this):** California superior courts are trial courts of general jurisdiction and may hear any civil case, including federal claims within concurrent jurisdiction, but not claims within exclusive federal jurisdiction such as patent and copyright claims. Civil cases are classified as limited—an amount in controversy of $35,000 or less, exclusive of attorney's fees, interest and costs—or unlimited; limited cases follow economic-litigation procedures with restricted discovery, and their appeals go to the superior court's appellate division.

**Elements:** 1. General jurisdiction of the superior court. 2. Classification by amount (and type of relief). 3. Consequences for procedure and appeal.
**Exceptions / defenses:** A case may be reclassified if it is wrongly classified (CCP § 403.040). Small claims are heard in the small-claims division: up to $12,500 for natural persons and $6,250 for other plaintiffs.
**Authority:** Cal. Const. art. VI, § 10; CCP §§ 85, 88 [S-147]; CCP §§ 90–100, including § 94 discovery limits [VERIFY V-162]; CCP §§ 403.040, 904.2; CCP §§ 116.220, 116.221 [S-270]; 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Not tested on the MBE. On an essay, contrast California's general jurisdiction with federal limited jurisdiction (R-CIV-001).
**Example:** P sues in Los Angeles Superior Court for $30,000 on a contract and adds a federal civil-rights claim. The court may hear both (concurrent jurisdiction); the case is a limited civil case.
> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: California's limited/unlimited split resembles PRC small-claims and simplified procedure (小额诉讼、简易程序) chosen by amount and complexity, not a jurisdictional limit (approx.).

### R-CIV-104 — California personal jurisdiction, service of process and the motion to quash
**Rule (write this):** A California court may exercise jurisdiction on any basis not inconsistent with the Constitution of California or of the United States, so the analysis is the federal due-process test. Service may be made by personal delivery; by substituted service after reasonable diligence, leaving the papers at the defendant's dwelling with a competent household member at least 18 years old or at the office with the person apparently in charge, and then mailing a copy; by mail with a notice and acknowledgment of receipt; outside California by first-class mail with return receipt; or by publication on court order when no other method works with reasonable diligence. The defendant ordinarily has 30 days after service to respond and may challenge jurisdiction or service by a motion to quash filed on or before the last day to plead, without making a general appearance.

**Elements:** 1. Long-arm (§ 410.10) → due process. 2. Method of service. 3. Timing. 4. Motion to quash.
**Exceptions / defenses:** Substituted service is complete 10 days after mailing. A defendant who appears generally without moving to quash waives the objection, but a motion to quash may be joined with a demurrer or motion to strike without waiver (§ 418.10(e)). An order granting a motion to quash is appealable (§ 904.1).
**Authority:** CCP §§ 410.10, 412.20(a)(3), 415.10, 415.20, 415.30, 415.40, 415.50, 416.10, 418.10, 583.210 (service within 3 years).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Due process is identical; methods and timing differ (federal: 21 days to answer; Rule 4 methods).
**Example:** After three failed attempts at D's home, the server leaves the papers with D's adult roommate and mails a copy. Valid substituted service, complete 10 days after mailing.

### R-CIV-105 — California venue and forum non conveniens
**Rule (write this):** California venue concerns the proper county. Generally the proper court is the county where any defendant resides when the action is commenced; for personal injury, wrongful death or injury to personal property, also the county where the injury occurred; for a contract claim, also the county where the contract was entered into or was to be performed; and for a corporation, also the county where the contract was made or performed, the obligation or liability arose, the breach occurred, or the corporation has its principal place of business. Actions affecting real property are tried where the property is located. The court may transfer when the county is not proper, when an impartial trial cannot be had, or when the convenience of witnesses and the ends of justice would be promoted; and under CCP § 410.30 it may stay or dismiss when, in the interest of substantial justice, the action should be heard outside California.

**Elements:** Venue: residence / injury / contract / corporation / real property. FNC: suitable alternative forum, then private and public interests.
**Exceptions / defenses:** A California resident's action is ordinarily stayed, not dismissed, on FNC grounds (Stangvik). An order granting a motion to stay or dismiss on FNC grounds is appealable (§ 904.1). California courts cannot transfer a case to another State.
**Authority:** CCP §§ 392, 395(a), 395.5, 396b, 397, 410.30; Stangvik v. Shiley Inc. (1991) 54 Cal.3d 744.
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Federal venue is by district (§ 1391) with §§ 1404/1406 transfer between federal districts.
**Example:** D, an Orange County resident, injures P in a crash in San Diego County; P lives in Los Angeles. Venue is proper in Orange or San Diego County, not Los Angeles.

### R-CIV-106 — California fact pleading and demurrers
**Rule (write this):** A California complaint must contain a statement of the facts constituting the cause of action in ordinary and concise language and a demand for judgment—fact pleading, which requires the ultimate facts supporting each element. A defendant may challenge defects appearing on the face of the pleading or in judicially noticeable matter by demurrer, filed within 30 days after service and after meeting and conferring. A general demurrer—failure to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action, or lack of subject-matter jurisdiction—is never waived; special demurrers, such as uncertainty (ambiguous or unintelligible), lack of capacity, another action pending, defect or misjoinder of parties, or, in a contract action, failure to show whether the contract is written, oral or implied by conduct, are waived if not raised. Leave to amend is liberally granted where there is a reasonable possibility the defect can be cured.

**Elements:** Fact pleading; demurrer grounds; timing; meet and confer; leave to amend.
**Exceptions / defenses:** Uncertainty demurrers are disfavored and sustained only when the defendant cannot reasonably respond. In personal-injury and wrongful-death actions the complaint does not state the amount of damages (§ 425.10(b)), and a punitive-damages amount is never pleaded (Civ. Code § 3295(e)). Fraud is the main exception to liberal construction: it must be pleaded with particularity — how, when, where, to whom and by what means the misrepresentations were made (*Lazar v. Superior Court*).
**Authority:** CCP §§ 425.10, 430.10 [S-256] [VERIFY V-152], 430.30, 430.40, 430.41, 430.80; Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311. *Lazar v. Superior Court* (1996) 12 Cal.4th 631.
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Federal: notice pleading tested by 12(b)(6) plausibility; federal 12(e) is the counterpart of the special demurrer for uncertainty.
**Example:** P alleges only that "D negligently caused P's injuries." D's general demurrer should be sustained with leave to amend to plead what D did, when, and how it caused the harm.

### R-CIV-107 — California motion to strike, answers, amendment, Doe defendants and relief from default
**Rule (write this):** A motion to strike may target irrelevant, false or improper matter, including an improper demand for damages, or a pleading not drawn in conformity with law. An answer to an unverified complaint may deny generally, but an answer to a verified complaint must be verified and deny specifically. A party may amend once without leave before an answer, demurrer or motion to strike is filed, or after a demurrer or motion to strike is filed but before it is heard if the amendment is filed by the opposition deadline; otherwise amendment requires leave in furtherance of justice. A plaintiff genuinely ignorant of a defendant's identity may sue a fictitiously named "Doe" and later substitute the true name with relation back, and the court may relieve a party from a judgment, order or default taken through mistake, inadvertence, surprise or excusable neglect on application made within a reasonable time not exceeding six months—and must do so when the attorney swears the default was the attorney's fault.

**Elements:** Motion to strike; answer form; amendment; Doe practice; § 473(b) relief.
**Exceptions / defenses:** Doe substitution requires genuine ignorance of the defendant's identity or of the facts giving rise to liability when the complaint was filed. Mandatory § 473(b) relief requires a timely application with the attorney's sworn affidavit of fault.
**Authority:** CCP §§ 431.30, 435, 436, 438, 472, 473(a), 473(b), 474.
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Federal: Rule 12(f) (narrower), Rule 15 amendment, Rule 15(c) relation back with notice within 90 days, Rule 55(c)/60(b) relief.
**Example:** P sues "Hospital and Does 1–20"; 18 months later P learns the anesthesiologist's name and substitutes her for Doe 1. The amendment relates back if P was truly ignorant of her identity or role when filing.

### R-CIV-108 — Anti-SLAPP special motion to strike
**Rule (write this):** A cause of action against a person arising from any act in furtherance of the person's right of petition or free speech under the United States or California Constitution in connection with a public issue is subject to a special motion to strike, unless the plaintiff establishes a probability of prevailing on the claim. First, the defendant must show the claim arises from protected activity—statements before, or in connection with an issue under consideration by, a legislative, executive or judicial body; statements in a public forum on an issue of public interest; or other conduct furthering those rights in connection with a public issue. Second, the plaintiff must show the claim is legally sufficient and supported by a prima facie showing of facts that would sustain a favorable judgment.

**Elements:** 1. Arising from (the protected activity supplies elements of the claim). 2. Protected category under § 425.16(e). 3. Probability of prevailing (minimal merit, admissible evidence). 4. Filed within 60 days of service; discovery stayed; prevailing defendant recovers fees and costs; order appealable.
**Exceptions / defenses:** Claims that merely mention protected activity as background do not "arise from" it (Park). Public-interest and commercial-speech exemptions (§ 425.17). A plaintiff recovers fees if the motion was frivolous or solely intended to delay. In federal court, the Ninth Circuit applies § 425.16 to state-law claims (Newsham), testing legal challenges under Rule 12(b)(6) and factual ones under Rule 56 (Planned Parenthood v. Center for Medical Progress), and its en banc court held in 2025 that denials are not immediately appealable in federal court (Gopher Media LLC v. Melone) [S-255]; other circuits do not apply anti-SLAPP statutes, and Berk v. Choy's reasoning may undercut Newsham [VERIFY V-151].
**Authority:** CCP § 425.16(b)(1), (c)(1), (e), (f), (g), (i) [S-259]; CCP §§ 425.17, 904.1(a)(13) [S-258]; Equilon Enterprises v. Consumer Cause, Inc. (2002) 29 Cal.4th 53; Navellier v. Sletten (2002) 29 Cal.4th 82; Baral v. Schnitt (2016) 1 Cal.5th 376; Park v. Board of Trustees of California State University (2017) 2 Cal.5th 1057; FilmOn.com Inc. v. DoubleVerify Inc. (2019) 7 Cal.5th 133; U.S. ex rel. Newsham v. Lockheed Missiles & Space Co., 190 F.3d 963 (9th Cir. 1999); Planned Parenthood Fed'n of Am. v. Center for Medical Progress, 890 F.3d 828 (9th Cir. 2018).
**MBE vs. CA essay:** No MBE equivalent. On a California essay set in federal court, flag the Erie question.
**Example:** A developer sues a neighbor for defamation over her testimony at a city-council hearing on its permit. Prong one is met (statement in an official proceeding); the developer must offer admissible evidence of a false statement of fact made with the required fault, or the claim is stricken and the developer pays the neighbor's fees.

### R-CIV-109 — California cross-complaints and the compulsory cross-complaint rule
**Rule (write this):** In California every claim by a defendant—against the plaintiff, a co-defendant or a new party—is pleaded as a cross-complaint, which may be brought against any party who has sued the cross-complainant and against any person, party or not, on a claim arising out of the same transaction, occurrence or series as the claim against the cross-complainant or asserting an interest in the property or controversy at issue. A defendant who files an answer must plead in a cross-complaint any related cause of action—one arising out of the same transaction, occurrence or series as the plaintiff's claim—that it has against the plaintiff when it serves the answer, or it may not assert that claim against the plaintiff in any later action. Cross-complaints against co-parties and third persons are permissive.

**Elements:** 1. Defendant answered. 2. Related cause of action against the plaintiff. 3. Existing when the answer was served. 4. Not pleaded → barred.
**Exceptions / defenses:** The bar does not apply if the court lacked personal jurisdiction or the defendant did not answer (§ 426.30(b)), or to the claims excepted in § 426.40; a party who omitted a related claim may seek leave to file it while the action is pending, and leave must be granted for a party acting in good faith (§ 426.50). The bar must be pleaded as an affirmative defense in the later action.
**Authority:** CCP §§ 426.10(c), 426.30 [S-257], 426.40, 426.50, 428.10, 428.50; joinder CCP §§ 378, 379, 382, 386, 387, 389.
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Federal Rule 13(a) compulsory counterclaim ≈ § 426.30; federal Rule 13(g) crossclaim and Rule 14 impleader ≈ permissive cross-complaints.
**Example:** P sues D for an unpaid invoice; D answers but does not cross-complain for P's defective work on the same job. D's later suit for the defective work is barred unless D obtained leave under § 426.50 while the first case was pending.

### R-CIV-110 — California discovery: scope and key devices
**Rule (write this):** Under the Civil Discovery Act a party may obtain discovery of any non-privileged matter that is relevant to the subject matter of the pending action, if the matter is itself admissible or appears reasonably calculated to lead to the discovery of admissible evidence—a broader standard than the federal claim-or-defense and proportionality test. Specially prepared interrogatories and requests for admission (other than those about the genuineness of documents) are each limited to 35 absent a declaration of necessity; inspection demands have no numerical limit; and in a personal-injury case a defendant may demand one physical examination of the plaintiff without a court order if it involves no painful, protracted or intrusive procedure and takes place within 75 miles of the plaintiff's residence, while a mental examination requires leave of court for good cause. A motion to compel further responses must be made within 45 days after the response and be accompanied by a meet-and-confer declaration.

**Elements:** Scope; device limits; examination rules; motion-to-compel deadline and declaration.
**Exceptions / defenses:** If a personal-injury plaintiff stipulates that no claim is made for mental or emotional distress beyond that usually associated with the physical injuries, a mental examination may be ordered only on a showing of exceptional circumstances. Depositions are generally limited to 7 hours of examination by parties other than the deponent's counsel, with exceptions [VERIFY V-157]. A responding party withholding documents on privilege grounds must provide enough information to evaluate the claim, including a privilege log if necessary.
**Authority:** CCP §§ 2016.040, 2017.010, 2025.290, 2030.030, 2030.300, 2031.010, 2031.240, 2031.310, 2032.220 [S-265], 2032.310, 2032.320 [S-266], 2033.030, 2034.210.
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Federal: R-CIV-064 (proportionality), R-CIV-069 (25 interrogatories), R-CIV-070 (Rule 35 needs an order for every exam).
**Example:** A shopper sues a store in superior court for a back injury. The store may demand one physical examination near her home without a court order but needs leave for a psychological examination—and will not get it, absent exceptional circumstances, if she stipulates to claim only the distress that usually accompanies her physical injury.

### R-CIV-111 — California initial disclosures on demand and discovery sanctions
**Rule (write this):** In California, initial disclosures are made only on demand: within 60 days after any party demands them, each party that has appeared must disclose the persons likely to have discoverable information that it may use to support its claims or defenses (with the subjects of that information), copies or descriptions of the documents and ESI it may use, and applicable insurance agreements, unless all parties stipulate otherwise; unlawful-detainer, small-claims and Family Code proceedings are among the exempt actions. The court must impose a $1,000 sanction on a party, its attorney or both for specified misconduct, such as failing to respond in good faith to a document request or failing to confer in good faith, and may impose monetary, issue, evidence, terminating and contempt sanctions for misuse of the discovery process.

**Elements:** Demand; 60 days; content; exemptions; graduated sanctions.
**Exceptions / defenses:** The sunset on the initial-disclosure statute was removed in 2025, so it remains in force for July 2027 [S-112] [VERIFY V-029].
**Authority:** CCP § 2016.090 [S-111] [S-113] [S-269] [VERIFY V-159]; CCP §§ 2023.010, 2023.030, 2023.050 [S-111].
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Federal disclosures are automatic within 14 days after the 26(f) conference (R-CIV-062).
**Example:** P demands initial disclosures 90 days after D answers. D must disclose within 60 days of the demand; if D later refuses document requests in bad faith, the $1,000 sanction is mandatory.

### R-CIV-112 — California attorney work product
**Rule (write this):** A writing that reflects an attorney's impressions, conclusions, opinions, or legal research or theories is not discoverable under any circumstances. Other attorney work product is not discoverable unless the court determines that denying discovery will unfairly prejudice the party seeking it in preparing its claim or defense or will result in an injustice. The protection belongs to the attorney, and witness statements obtained through an attorney's efforts receive at least qualified protection, and absolute protection where disclosure would reveal the attorney's tactics, impressions or evaluation of the case.

**Elements:** 1. Attorney work product. 2. Absolute (impressions) or qualified (other). 3. For qualified: unfair prejudice or injustice.
**Exceptions / defenses:** The client may not waive the attorney's protection alone; protection can be waived by disclosure inconsistent with confidentiality.
**Authority:** CCP §§ 2018.020, 2018.030; Coito v. Superior Court (2012) 54 Cal.4th 480.
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Federal Rule 26(b)(3) protects materials prepared by a party or any representative, using a substantial-need-and-undue-hardship test (R-CIV-066).
**Example:** P's lawyer interviews and records eyewitnesses; D seeks the recordings. Under Coito they have at least qualified protection—D must show unfair prejudice, such as a witness's later unavailability—and the lawyer's choice of whom to interview may be absolutely protected.

### R-CIV-113 — California summary judgment and summary adjudication
**Rule (write this):** Summary judgment shall be granted if all the papers submitted show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. The motion must be supported by a separate statement of undisputed material facts and served at least 81 days before the hearing, with the opposition due 20 days and the reply 11 days before the hearing (the reply may not add new evidence), and the hearing must be held at least 30 days before trial. A defendant meets its initial burden by showing that one or more elements of the cause of action cannot be established or that there is a complete defense—including by showing that the plaintiff does not possess and cannot reasonably obtain needed evidence.

**Elements:** Standard; separate statement; 81/20/11-day timing; defendant's burden; summary adjudication limits.
**Exceptions / defenses:** Summary adjudication is available only if it completely disposes of a cause of action, an affirmative defense, a claim for punitive damages or an issue of duty. An order denying summary judgment is reviewable before trial only by petition for writ filed within 20 days after service of notice of entry.
**Authority:** CCP § 437c(a), (b), (c), (f)(1), (m)(1), (p)(2) [S-114] [S-263]; Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826.
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Federal Rule 56: no separate-statement requirement in the rule itself, filing any time until 30 days after discovery closes, and partial summary judgment on any part of a claim (R-CIV-085, R-CIV-086).
**Example:** D moves for summary adjudication that "P's damages cannot exceed $50,000." Improper in California because it disposes of no entire cause of action, defense, punitive-damages claim or duty issue.

### R-CIV-114 — California claim preclusion: the primary-rights theory
**Rule (write this):** California defines a cause of action by the primary right invaded, not by the legal theory or the transaction: one injury to one primary right gives rise to one cause of action, however many theories or remedies are available. A final judgment on the merits bars a later suit between the same parties or their privies on the same primary right, including theories never raised, but a later suit on a different primary right—even one arising from the same transaction—is not barred.

**Elements:** 1. Same primary right (same harm). 2. Same parties or privies. 3. Final judgment on the merits.
**Exceptions / defenses:** Personal injury and property damage from one accident may be separate primary rights [VERIFY V-155]; a judgment is not final while an appeal is pending (R-CIV-115).
**Authority:** Slater v. Blackwood (1975) 15 Cal.3d 791 [S-272]; Crowley v. Katleman (1994) 8 Cal.4th 666; Mycogen Corp. v. Monsanto Co. (2002) 28 Cal.4th 888; Boeken v. Philip Morris USA, Inc. (2010) 48 Cal.4th 788 [S-273]; DKN Holdings LLC v. Faerber (2015) 61 Cal.4th 813; Holmes v. David H. Bricker, Inc. (1969) 70 Cal.2d 786 [S-274].
**MBE vs. CA essay:** The federal and MBE transactional test bars every claim from the same transaction; the primary-rights test can allow a second suit. When the first judgment came from a federal diversity court in California, Semtek usually points to California law (R-CIV-098).
**Example:** P wins a judgment ordering D to perform a contract, then sues D for damages for the same breach. Barred: one primary right, so all remedies had to be sought in the first suit (Mycogen).

### R-CIV-115 — California issue preclusion and finality pending appeal
**Rule (write this):** Issue preclusion applies in California when (1) the issue is identical to one decided in a prior proceeding, (2) it was actually litigated, (3) it was necessarily decided, (4) the prior decision is final and on the merits, and (5) the party against whom preclusion is sought was a party, or in privity with a party, to the prior proceeding—and applying it serves the doctrine's public-policy purposes. Mutuality is not required, so a non-party may invoke it defensively and, subject to fairness, offensively. Unlike the federal rule, a California trial-court judgment is not final for claim-preclusion purposes while an appeal is pending or until the time to appeal has expired.

**Elements:** The five Lucido elements plus public policy; finality rule.
**Exceptions / defenses:** Issue preclusion may be asserted only against a party to the prior action or one in privity (DKN). Courts may stay a second action while the first is on appeal.
**Authority:** Bernhard v. Bank of America (1942) 19 Cal.2d 807; Lucido v. Superior Court (1990) 51 Cal.3d 335; DKN Holdings LLC v. Faerber (2015) 61 Cal.4th 813; Samara v. Matar (2018) 5 Cal.5th 322 [S-267]; CCP § 1049; Sandoval v. Superior Court (1983) 140 Cal.App.3d 932 [S-268].
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Federal majority rule: a judgment is final for preclusion when entered, despite a pending appeal.
**Example:** P loses Suit 1 in superior court and appeals. While the appeal is pending, P files Suit 2 on the same primary right. D's res judicata defense is premature because Suit 1's judgment is not yet final; the court may stay Suit 2.

### R-CIV-116 — California civil jury trial
**Rule (write this):** Trial by jury is an inviolate right in California, but in a civil cause three-fourths of the jury may render a verdict, and a civil jury consists of 12 persons unless the parties agree in open court to fewer. The right extends to actions that were triable by jury at common law when the California Constitution was adopted in 1850; whether an action is legal or equitable depends on its gist, and a pre-dispute contractual jury waiver is unenforceable. A party waives a jury by failing to appear, failing to demand a jury, or failing to deposit advance jury fees on time, among other statutory methods.

**Elements:** Right (1850 test); size; three-fourths verdict; waiver.
**Exceptions / defenses:** The Legislature may provide eight-person juries in certain civil causes, and limited civil cases use eight jurors [VERIFY V-153]. Promissory estoppel is equitable and carries no jury right (C & K Engineering).
**Authority:** Cal. Const. art. I, § 16 [S-260]; CCP §§ 231(c) [S-271], 631; C & K Engineering Contractors v. Amber Steel Co. (1978) 23 Cal.3d 1; Grafton Partners L.P. v. Superior Court (2005) 36 Cal.4th 944.
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Federal: 1791 test, 6–12 jurors, unanimity unless stipulated, Beacon Theatres sequencing (R-CIV-078 to R-CIV-081).
**Example:** A 12-person California civil jury votes 9–3 for P. That is a valid verdict; in federal court, absent a stipulation, it would be a hung jury.

### R-CIV-117 — California post-trial motions: new trial, JNOV, nonsuit, directed verdict
**Rule (write this):** A California court may grant a new trial for irregularity in the proceedings, jury misconduct, accident or surprise, newly discovered material evidence that could not with reasonable diligence have been produced at trial, excessive or inadequate damages, insufficiency of the evidence or a decision against law, or an error in law excepted to at trial; it must specify the grounds and reasons in writing, and may grant a new trial for insufficient evidence or excessive or inadequate damages only if, after weighing the evidence, it is convinced the jury clearly should have reached a different verdict. A notice of intention to move for a new trial must be filed within 15 days after service of notice of entry of judgment, or 180 days after entry, whichever is earliest, and the court's power to rule expires 75 days later, after which the motion is denied by operation of law. JNOV must be granted whenever a directed verdict should have been granted had a motion been made—no prior motion is required—and nonsuit (after the plaintiff's opening statement or evidence) and directed verdict (after all evidence) are the pre-verdict analogues of federal JMOL.

**Elements:** New-trial grounds and reasons; timing; JNOV standard; nonsuit and directed verdict.
**Exceptions / defenses:** The court may condition a new-trial order on the plaintiff's acceptance of a remittitur or the defendant's acceptance of an additur (§ 662.5). An order granting a new trial or denying JNOV is appealable (§ 904.1(a)(4)). The 15/180/75-day limits are jurisdictional and cannot be extended.
**Authority:** CCP §§ 581c, 629, 630, 657, 659 [S-261], 660 [S-262], 662.5; Evid. Code § 1150.
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Federal: Rule 50 (renewed JMOL requires a pre-verdict motion), Rule 59 (28 days; no additur).
**Example:** After a defense verdict, P learns that a juror concealed a lawsuit against P's employer. P files a notice of intention 10 days after service of notice of entry; the court must rule within 75 days or the motion is denied by operation of law.

### R-CIV-118 — California appeals and writs
**Rule (write this):** California follows a one-final-judgment rule: an appeal lies from a final judgment and from orders the Legislature has made appealable, including orders granting a motion to quash service or to stay or dismiss for forum non conveniens, granting a new trial or denying JNOV, granting, dissolving or refusing an injunction, and granting or denying an anti-SLAPP motion, as well as orders made after an appealable judgment. A notice of appeal is generally due 60 days after service of notice of entry of judgment, or 180 days after entry if no notice is served. Non-appealable interlocutory rulings, such as the denial of summary judgment or discovery orders on privilege, may be reviewed by petition for an extraordinary writ of mandate or prohibition when there is no adequate remedy at law.

**Elements:** Appealable judgment or order; timing; writ review.
**Exceptions / defenses:** An order denying a petition to compel arbitration is appealable (§ 1294(a)). An order denying class certification is appealable under the "death knell" doctrine (Daar). Limited civil case appeals go to the appellate division (§ 904.2).
**Authority:** CCP §§ 904.1 [S-258], 904.2, 1085, 1102, 1294(a), 437c(m)(1); Cal. Rules of Court, rule 8.104; Daar v. Yellow Cab Co. (1967) 67 Cal.2d 695.
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Federal: § 1291 finality, § 1292 interlocutory appeals, collateral-order doctrine, 30-day deadline (R-CIV-099 to R-CIV-101).
**Example:** A superior court grants D's anti-SLAPP motion in part. Either side may appeal immediately (§ 904.1(a)(13)), whereas in federal court the Ninth Circuit no longer permits an immediate appeal from a denial (Gopher Media) [VERIFY V-151].

### R-CIV-119 — California sanctions for frivolous filings (CCP § 128.7)
**Rule (write this):** California's counterpart to Rule 11 provides that by presenting a pleading, motion or similar paper, an attorney or unrepresented party certifies that, after a reasonable inquiry, it is not presented for an improper purpose, its legal contentions are warranted by existing law or a nonfrivolous argument for changing it, and its factual contentions and denials have, or are likely to have, evidentiary support. A sanctions motion must be served first and may be filed only if the challenged paper is not withdrawn or corrected within 21 days, and sanctions are limited to what suffices to deter repetition.

**Elements:** Certification; two-step serve-then-file procedure; 21-day safe harbor; deterrence limit.
**Exceptions / defenses:** The served motion must be a formal noticed motion identical to the one later filed. Bad-faith tactics are separately sanctionable under CCP § 128.5.
**Authority:** CCP §§ 128.5, 128.7 [S-264].
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same structure as Rule 11 (R-CIV-050, R-CIV-051).
**Example:** D serves a § 128.7 motion attacking P's frivolous fraud count, and P dismisses that count on day 15. D cannot file the motion: the safe harbor worked.

### R-CIV-120 — Waiver of the right to compel arbitration
**Rule (write this):** A party waives its contractual right to compel arbitration when it knowingly acts inconsistently with that right—for example, by litigating the merits, pursuing discovery or delaying for a long period—under ordinary waiver principles, and the party opposing arbitration need not show that it was prejudiced. This is now the rule under both the Federal Arbitration Act and California law.

**Elements:** 1. Knowledge of the right. 2. Conduct inconsistent with it. 3. No prejudice requirement.
**Exceptions / defenses:** Pre-2024 California decisions requiring prejudice are abrogated. Orders denying arbitration are immediately appealable (9 U.S.C. § 16; CCP § 1294(a)).
**Authority:** Morgan v. Sundance, Inc. (2022); Quach v. California Commerce Club, Inc. (2024) [S-131]; CCP §§ 1281.2, 1294(a); 9 U.S.C. §§ 3, 4, 16.
**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule; on a California essay, note that Quach abrogated the former prejudice requirement.
**Example:** An employer answers, serves discovery and litigates for 13 months before moving to compel arbitration. It has waived the right; the employee need not show prejudice (Quach).
