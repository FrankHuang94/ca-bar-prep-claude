# Wills and Succession — primer

Read this before the [outline](outline.md). It gives you the map, the vocabulary and the eight ideas that decide most California Wills essays.

## 1. What the subject is about

When a Californian dies, the law must answer one question for every asset: **who gets it?** The answer comes from four sources, in this order:

1. **What the decedent did not own.** If the decedent was married, the surviving spouse already owns half of the community property (CP) and half of the decedent's quasi-community property (QCP). That half never enters the estate. This is why Wills essays so often begin with Community Property.
2. **Nonprobate transfers.** Joint tenancy, life insurance, pay-on-death (POD) accounts, transfer-on-death deeds and revocable trusts pass by their own terms, outside the will.
3. **The will.** If there is a valid will, it controls the rest (the "probate estate"), subject to statutory protections for spouses and children the will forgot.
4. **Intestacy.** Whatever the will does not validly give away passes to the heirs named by statute (Prob. Code §§ 6400–6414).

The State Bar's outline for the subject follows this logic: general provisions (CP at death, contracts about death, simultaneous death), intestacy, validity, execution, component parts, revocation, and the omitted spouse and omitted child statutes [S-094].

## 3. How the pieces connect: the essay flow

Almost every California Wills essay can be organized with this sequence (it mirrors [`issue-checklist.md`](issue-checklist.md)):

1. **Characterize** each asset if the decedent was married (CP, QCP, SP) and pull out nonprobate assets.
2. **Is there a valid will?** Execution (attested, harmless error, holographic, foreign execution), then capacity, undue influence, fraud, duress.
3. **What does the will include?** Integration, incorporation by reference, the § 6132 list, acts of independent significance, codicils and republication, pour-overs.
4. **Was it revoked?** Later instrument, physical act, divorce, lost-will presumption; then revival and dependent relative revocation.
5. **Who takes what?** Interpretation and ambiguity; lapse and anti-lapse; class gifts; ademption; abatement.
6. **Statutory protections.** Omitted spouse; omitted child; disqualified donees; no-contest clauses.
7. **Intestacy** for anything left over.

## 4. The eight ideas that matter most

1. **Characterize before you distribute.** A married decedent can give away only SP, half the CP and half of his or her QCP (§§ 100, 101, 6101). If the will gives "my house" and the house is CP, the beneficiary gets the decedent's half only. California has **no elective share**; community property does that job.
2. **Execution is the first issue.** California accepts three routes: an **attested** will (two witnesses present at the same time, § 6110), the **harmless-error** rule for witnessing defects proven by clear and convincing evidence (§ 6110(c)(2)), and the **holographic** will (signature and material provisions in the testator's hand, § 6111). A will valid where it was signed is valid here (§ 6113).
3. **Handwritten changes are a three-step problem.** Is the new text a valid holographic codicil? If not, was the crossing-out a partial revocation by physical act? If so, does **dependent relative revocation** restore the old gift because the testator would rather have it than nothing?
4. **Revocation needs act plus intent.** Burning, tearing, cancelling, obliterating or destroying, by the testator or another **in the testator's presence and at his direction** (§ 6120). A missing will that was last in the testator's possession is presumed revoked (§ 6124). Divorce revokes gifts to the former spouse (§ 6122).
5. **Anti-lapse saves gifts to relatives, not friends or spouses.** If a beneficiary who is **kindred** of the testator (or of the testator's spouse or former spouse) dies first, the beneficiary's issue take, unless the will requires survival (§ 21110).
6. **The omitted spouse and omitted child statutes are the heart of many essays.** A spouse married after **all** testamentary instruments gets the decedent's half of CP and QCP plus up to half of SP (§ 21610); a child born after them gets an intestate share (§ 21620); each has a short list of exceptions (§§ 21611, 21621). A later codicil or trust amendment republishes the plan and defeats the claim.
7. **Undue influence has three routes.** The common-law presumption (confidential relationship, active procurement, undue benefit — *Rice v. Clark*); the statutory definition (§ 86 and Welf. & Inst. Code § 15610.70); and the statutory presumption against drafters, fiduciary-transcribers and care custodians (§ 21380).
8. **Intestacy is a fixed ladder.** Spouse first for CP and QCP; SP split between the spouse and issue, or parents, or siblings (§ 6401); then issue, parents, siblings' lines, grandparents' lines, stepchildren's line, next of kin (§ 6402). Shares are divided by representation at the first generation with a living member (§ 240).

## 5. How this differs from what you know

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: in PRC succession law the spouse, children and parents share the first order equally and a will can be set aside only for formal defects, incapacity, coercion or fraud; California instead gives the spouse a property-based share (CP), ranks parents behind children, and adds a thick layer of salvage doctrines (harmless error, DRR, anti-lapse, reformation) meant to carry out what the testator probably wanted (approx.).

Three habits to build:

- **Cite the section.** California graders expect Probate Code section numbers for execution (§§ 6110–6113), revocation (§§ 6120–6124), omitted heirs (§§ 21610–21623) and anti-lapse (§ 21110).
- **Argue both sides on intent questions.** Undue influence, testamentary intent, DRR and "substantially all to the other parent" are fact-heavy; use every fact and conclude with "likely."
- **Show the arithmetic.** When the call asks what each person takes, give fractions or dollar amounts.
