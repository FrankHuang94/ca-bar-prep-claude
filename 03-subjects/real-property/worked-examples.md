# Real Property — worked examples

Each example is original. Format: facts → rule (with ID) → application → conclusion. "CA" lines show the one-sentence California addition.

### 1. The library grant
**Facts.** In 2000, O deeded land "to the Town so long as it is used for a public library." In 2026 the Town converts the building to offices. O died in 2015, leaving everything to her daughter D.
**Rule.** Durational words create a fee simple determinable; the grantor's possibility of reverter is devisable and takes effect automatically (R-PRP-002, R-PRP-018).
**Application.** "So long as" is durational; the use ended in 2026. O's possibility of reverter passed to D by will.
**Conclusion.** At common law, title reverted to D automatically in 2026. CA: the interest is a power of termination; because the deed was recorded in 2000, the 30-year period runs to 2030, so the power is still enforceable in 2026, but forfeiture is not automatic and D must act to terminate (R-PRP-005).

### 2. The gift over to descendants
**Facts.** O conveys Lot B "to Barry, but if Lot B is ever used other than as a personal residence, to Zach's descendants." Barry opens a bakery there.
**Rule.** A gift over to a third party on a condition is a shifting executory interest, subject to the RAP (R-PRP-004, R-PRP-014).
**Application.** The bakery use could have occurred centuries later, so at common law the executory interest might vest too late and is void; striking it leaves Barry with a fee simple absolute (conditional language "but if").
**Conclusion.** Barry keeps Lot B at common law. CA: under USRAP the interest is valid if the triggering use happens within 90 years of the grant, so Zach's descendants may take (R-PRP-016).

### 3. Remainder to grandchildren
**Facts.** T's will: "to my son S for life, then to S's children who reach 25." At T's death S has one child, age 3.
**Rule.** Contingent remainder to a class; RAP all-or-nothing for class gifts (R-PRP-010, R-PRP-015).
**Application.** S could have another child after T's death who reaches 25 more than 21 years after S and every other life in being die.
**Conclusion.** Void at common law; T's heirs hold the reversion after S's life estate. CA: valid if every child actually reaches 25 (or the class closes) within 90 years, otherwise reformed (R-PRP-016).

### 4. Three joint tenants
**Facts.** A, B and C hold as joint tenants. A deeds her interest to D. Then B dies, leaving a will giving everything to E.
**Rule.** A conveyance severs as to the conveyed share; a will cannot sever (R-PRP-021).
**Application.** D holds one-third as a tenant in common. B and C remained joint tenants of two-thirds; B's will is ineffective, and C takes B's share by survivorship.
**Conclusion.** C owns two-thirds and D one-third, as tenants in common; E takes nothing.

### 5. One joint tenant's deed of trust
**Facts.** H and his brother J own a house as joint tenants. H alone borrows $100,000 from Bank secured by a deed of trust on "his interest," then dies.
**Rule.** In a lien-theory state, a mortgage by one joint tenant does not sever, and the lien ends at the mortgagor's death (R-PRP-022).
**Application.** California is a lien-theory state. H's interest ended at death; Bank's lien attached only to that interest.
**Conclusion.** J owns the house free of Bank's lien (*People v. Nogarr*); Bank has an unsecured claim against H's estate.

### 6. Cotenant in possession
**Facts.** Siblings P and L inherit a farm as tenants in common. P farms it alone for ten years, pays all property taxes, and builds a barn. L now wants half the farm profits and rent; P wants half the taxes and barn cost.
**Rule.** No rent owed absent ouster; contribution for taxes offset by the value of exclusive use; improvements credited only in partition or accounting (R-PRP-024, R-PRP-025).
**Application.** L never demanded possession and was not excluded, so no ouster. P's own farming profits need not be shared (minority would charge rental value). P's tax claim is reduced by the rental value of P's exclusive occupancy; the barn is credited only on partition, to the extent it added value.
**Conclusion.** L likely recovers nothing for rent; P likely recovers little or nothing for taxes after offset; barn value is an equity on partition.

### 7. Holdover prior tenant
**Facts.** L leases an apartment to T for a year from June 1. The prior tenant R stays until June 15. T stays in a hotel and moves in on June 16, then refuses to pay June rent.
**Rule.** English rule (majority): landlord must deliver actual possession; tenant may affirm with abatement and damages (R-PRP-030).
**Application.** L failed to deliver possession for 15 days. T affirmed by moving in.
**Conclusion.** Under the English rule T owes rent only from June 16 and may recover reasonable hotel costs; under the American rule T owes full rent and must sue R. CA: likely English rule, given Civ. Code § 1927's duty to secure quiet possession.

### 8. No heat, tenant stays
**Facts.** T's apartment has no heat from November to January; L ignores three written requests. T pays a contractor $1,200 (rent $2,000) and deducts it; L serves a notice to quit a week later.
**Rule.** Warranty of habitability; repair and deduct; retaliation (R-PRP-036 to R-PRP-038).
**Application.** No heat in winter is a substantial health and safety violation; notice and more than a reasonable time passed. $1,200 is under one month's rent. L's notice follows protected activity closely.
**Conclusion.** T's deduction is valid; L's notice is presumptively retaliatory (CA: within 180 days, Civ. Code § 1942.5).

### 9. Commercial flooding
**Facts.** A florist's shop floods each heavy rain because of a broken storm drain the landlord controls. After repeated complaints, the florist moves out two months later and stops paying.
**Rule.** Constructive eviction (R-PRP-035).
**Application.** Repeated flooding substantially interferes; landlord's duty and control; notice; vacated within a reasonable time. Habitability does not apply to a commercial lease.
**Conclusion.** Constructive eviction; no further rent; damages possible.

### 10. Assignment chain
**Facts.** T leases for five years, then assigns to A, who promises to "assume and perform all lease obligations." A assigns to B, who makes no promise. B stops paying.
**Rule.** Original tenant liable on contract; assuming assignee liable on contract; non-assuming assignee liable on privity of estate while it holds the leasehold (R-PRP-039, R-PRP-040).
**Conclusion.** L may sue T, A and B for rent accruing while B holds the leasehold; once B reassigns, B's liability for later rent ends (merely moving out does not end it), but T's and A's continue.

### 11. Tenant leaves early
**Facts.** T abandons a two-year residential lease after six months. L refuses to show the unit to two qualified applicants because L "wants T to pay," then sues for 18 months' rent.
**Rule.** Duty to mitigate (R-PRP-033).
**Application.** L rejected suitable replacements; damages are limited to what could not reasonably have been avoided.
**Conclusion.** L recovers only rent until a reasonable reletting date plus costs. CA: under § 1951.2 T must prove what could have been avoided, which the rejected applicants show.

### 12. Consent to assign
**Facts.** A commercial lease requires L's written consent to assignment, with no standard. L refuses to approve a financially stronger assignee unless rent increases 15%.
**Rule.** Traditional rule vs. reasonableness; *Kendall*; § 1995.260 (R-PRP-042).
**Conclusion.** Majority-traditional: refusal valid. CA: refusal is not commercially reasonable; T may assign and recover damages.

### 13. Subdivision restriction
**Facts.** A developer sells 15 lots, each deed reciting "for residential purposes only," and records no separate declaration. Owner X runs a car-detailing business from her driveway; neighbor N sues for an injunction. Three other owners have run small businesses for over ten years without complaint.
**Rule.** Equitable servitude; scope; acquiescence and abandonment (R-PRP-052, R-PRP-055, R-PRP-056).
**Application.** Writing in X's deed, intent shown by uniform restrictions, touch and concern, record notice. A customer-facing business likely violates "residential purposes." Three tolerated businesses out of 15 may not show abandonment of the whole plan, but if N tolerated similar uses, acquiescence may bar N.
**Conclusion.** Likely enforceable unless N acquiesced in comparable uses.

### 14. The unrestricted lot
**Facts.** Same subdivision, but the deed to Lot 15 omitted the restriction. The Lot 15 buyer saw the uniform houses and recorded deeds of neighbors before buying.
**Rule.** Common scheme / implied reciprocal servitude (R-PRP-053).
**Conclusion.** Majority (*Sanborn*): Lot 15 is bound because of the plan and inquiry notice. CA: no implied servitude without a recorded instrument covering Lot 15 (*Riley*); here none exists, so Lot 15 is likely unrestricted.

### 15. Subdividing the dominant estate
**Facts.** A written easement grants F "a right of way over the lane for ingress and egress to the farm." F subdivides the farm into 30 home lots and plans to bury power lines under the lane.
**Rule.** Scope of an express easement (R-PRP-066).
**Application.** Increased traffic from foreseeable development is a change in degree; utility lines are a different kind of use not mentioned in an ingress-and-egress grant.
**Conclusion.** The lot owners may likely use the lane for access (unless the burden becomes unreasonable), but the servient owner can likely enjoin the power lines.

### 16. Landlocked lot
**Facts.** O owns a 40-acre tract fronting a highway. She sells the back 20 acres, which have no access except across the front 20. Ten years later a county road reaches the back parcel.
**Rule.** Easement by necessity (R-PRP-063).
**Conclusion.** The buyer had an easement by necessity across the front parcel at severance; it ended when the county road provided access.

### 17. Neighbor's path
**Facts.** For six years N walked across O's field daily to the beach, openly; O never spoke to N. In year seven O fences the path. O had posted signs reading "Right to pass by permission, and subject to control, of owner" at the entrances throughout.
**Rule.** Prescriptive easement; California § 1008 (R-PRP-064).
**Conclusion.** Majority: N likely has a prescriptive easement if the period is five years or less. CA: the statutory signs prevent any prescriptive easement; N has none.

### 18. Fire before closing
**Facts.** S contracts to sell a house to B; before closing and before B moves in, lightning burns the house. S has insurance proceeds of $300,000. The price is $800,000.
**Rule.** Equitable conversion; UVPRA (R-PRP-082).
**Conclusion.** Majority: B must pay $800,000 with a $300,000 credit. CA: the UVPRA puts the risk on S; B may rescind and recover the deposit.

### 19. Zoning violation found
**Facts.** Before closing, B discovers the seller's garage was built in violation of the setback ordinance.
**Rule.** Marketable title (R-PRP-080).
**Conclusion.** An existing zoning violation makes title unmarketable; B may refuse to close and recover the deposit unless S cures before closing.

### 20. The race-notice priority
**Facts.** On March 1, O mortgages Blackacre to Lender, which records on March 20. On March 10, O sells Blackacre to Barry for value; Barry has no notice of the mortgage and records on March 15. Barry later sells to Carlos by general warranty deed; Lender forecloses.
**Rule.** Race-notice act; covenants of title (R-PRP-116, R-PRP-111).
**Application.** Barry was a purchaser for value without notice on March 10 and recorded first (March 15 before March 20).
**Conclusion.** Barry took free of Lender's mortgage under a race-notice statute, and Carlos takes Barry's protected title (shelter). If instead Lender had recorded first, Carlos would take subject to the mortgage and could sue Barry for breach of the covenant against encumbrances (at delivery) and, on foreclosure, the future covenants.

### 21. Gift and record
**Facts.** O sells to A for value; A does not record. O then gives the land to his nephew B, who records.
**Rule.** Recording acts protect only purchasers for value (R-PRP-117).
**Conclusion.** A prevails under every type of statute; B paid no value.

### 22. Wild deed
**Facts.** O conveys to A (unrecorded). A conveys to B, who records. O then sells to C, who searches the grantor index under O, finds nothing, and records.
**Rule.** Chain of title; wild deeds (R-PRP-118).
**Conclusion.** A–B is a wild deed; C had no record notice and, in a notice or race-notice state, prevails over B.

### 23. Assumption vs. subject to
**Facts.** M mortgages to Bank, then sells to A "subject to" the mortgage. A sells to B, who "assumes and agrees to pay" it. Bank forecloses; a $40,000 deficiency remains.
**Rule.** Personal liability after transfers (R-PRP-094).
**Conclusion.** M is personally liable. A is not (subject to). B's liability to Bank is doubtful in many states because A, to whom B promised, owed Bank nothing. CA: no deficiency at all after a trustee's sale or if the loan was purchase money for an owner-occupied home (R-PRP-104).

### 24. Foreclosure waterfall
**Facts.** Blackacre has a first mortgage (Bank 1, $200,000), a second (Bank 2, $100,000) and a third (Bank 3, $50,000). Bank 2 forecloses judicially, joining Bank 3. The sale nets $120,000.
**Rule.** Foreclosure effects and distribution (R-PRP-101, R-PRP-102).
**Conclusion.** Buyer takes subject to Bank 1. Bank 2 receives $100,000; Bank 3 receives $20,000; its lien is extinguished and its $30,000 remaining claim is unsecured. Owner receives nothing.

### 25. Boundary strip in California
**Facts.** For seven years D has fenced and gardened a 10-foot strip that the record shows belongs to neighbor P. P has paid the taxes on her whole parcel, including the strip.
**Rule.** California adverse possession requires payment of taxes (R-PRP-106); agreed boundary (R-PRP-108).
**Conclusion.** D cannot acquire title by adverse possession (no taxes paid). D may argue a prescriptive easement (disfavored if it excludes P entirely) or an agreed boundary (only if both were uncertain of the line and agreed on the fence).

### 26. The deed in the drawer
**Facts.** G signs a deed to her son S and keeps it in her desk, telling S "the house will be yours when I die." After G's death, S finds the deed.
**Rule.** Delivery (R-PRP-110).
**Conclusion.** No delivery: G kept control and showed no intent that the deed operate during her life. The house passes under G's will or by intestacy.
