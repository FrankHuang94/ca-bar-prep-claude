# Real Property — primer

## The big picture
US property law treats ownership of land as a **bundle of rights** (to possess, use, exclude and transfer) that can be **divided** in three ways:

1. **Over time:** one person owns now, another later (present estates and future interests).
2. **Among people at the same time:** co-owners share possession (tenancy in common, joint tenancy, community property).
3. **Between owner and non-owners:** a tenant holds possession for a term (landlord-tenant); a neighbor holds a right to cross (easement) or to stop a use (covenant); a lender holds security (mortgage).

The exam then asks what happens when these divided rights are **transferred** (deeds, wills, assignments) and when transfers **collide** (recording acts, adverse possession, foreclosure priorities). Almost every Real Property question is one of two kinds: "**who owns what**, now and later?" or "**who wins** between two people claiming the same land?"

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: PRC law starts from state or collective land ownership with private land-use rights registered in a public system; US law starts from private fee ownership, with priority among private claimants decided by recording acts (approx.).

## Key vocabulary
| Term | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| Fee simple | Full ownership, forever |
| Defeasible fee | Ownership that can be cut short if something happens |
| Life estate | Ownership for someone's lifetime |
| Future interest | A present right to possess later (reversion, remainder, executory interest, possibility of reverter, right of entry) |
| Vest | Become certain: the taker is known and no condition remains except the end of the earlier estate |
| Rule against perpetuities (RAP) | Limit on how long a contingent future interest may remain uncertain |
| Cotenancy | Shared ownership; a joint tenancy adds survivorship |
| Privity of estate / contract | The two legal links that let a landlord sue someone for rent |
| Easement | A right to use someone else's land (often to cross it) |
| Real covenant / equitable servitude | A promise about land use that binds later owners (damages / injunction) |
| Recording act | Statute deciding priority between competing buyers based on recording and notice |
| BFP | Bona fide purchaser: paid value, had no notice |
| Mortgage / deed of trust | Security for a loan; foreclosure lets the lender sell the land |
| Lien theory | The lender holds only a lien, not title (majority; California) |
| Adverse possession | Gaining title by possessing another's land long enough |

## How the pieces connect
- **Estates feed everything.** You must know what A holds before asking whether A could lease it, mortgage it, or convey it with warranties. A life tenant's lease ends at her death; a joint tenant's mortgage dies with her in most lien-theory states, including California.
- **Running with the land** is the same idea in three places: lease covenants (R-PRP-041), real covenants and servitudes (R-PRP-050 to R-PRP-053), and easements (R-PRP-067). In each, ask: did the original parties intend it to bind successors, does it touch and concern the land, and does the successor have the required privity or notice?
- **Notice is the hinge** of transfers: equitable servitudes bind with notice; recording acts protect only purchasers without notice; possession gives inquiry notice of leases, easements and adverse claims.
- **Mortgages are transfers too.** A mortgage is an interest in land, so recording acts decide priority between mortgages and deeds (Feb 2025 Q3 [VERIFY V-071]).
- **Remedies cross into other subjects.** Specific performance and ejectment are in Remedies; nuisance and premises liability in Torts; takings in Constitutional Law; spousal title in Community Property.

## The ideas that matter most
1. **Classify first, by words.** "So long as" = automatic end (determinable); "but if … may re-enter" = election (condition subsequent); "but if …, to B" = executory interest. Get the words right and the rest follows (R-PRP-002 to R-PRP-005).
2. **The RAP is about what might happen, not what is likely.** If any possible chain of events vests the interest more than 21 years after all lives in being die, it fails at common law. California saves most gifts with a 90-year wait-and-see rule (R-PRP-014 to R-PRP-016).
3. **Joint tenancy survives only as long as the four unities do.** A conveyance severs; a will does not; a mortgage depends on the mortgage theory (R-PRP-020 to R-PRP-022).
4. **Landlord-tenant is contract plus property.** The tenant pays rent because of privity; the landlord must deliver possession, keep quiet enjoyment and (residential) habitability; the tenant who leaves early is liable, but the landlord must mitigate (R-PRP-030 to R-PRP-040).
5. **Covenants vs. servitudes is a remedy choice.** Damages require the full real-covenant checklist including horizontal privity; an injunction needs only writing, intent, touch and concern and notice (R-PRP-050 to R-PRP-056).
6. **Easements are created five ways and end many ways.** Express, implied from prior use, necessity, prescription, estoppel; scope follows the method of creation (R-PRP-060 to R-PRP-068).
7. **Recording acts protect the later innocent purchaser.** Identify the statute type, then ask: purchaser for value? without notice (actual, record, inquiry)? recorded first (race-notice)? (R-PRP-116 to R-PRP-119).
8. **Foreclosure flows downhill.** It wipes out the foreclosed mortgage and interests junior to it, never seniors; proceeds pay the foreclosing debt, then juniors in order, then the owner (R-PRP-100 to R-PRP-102).

## How Real Property is tested
- **MBE:** precise black-letter distinctions: classification of future interests, RAP traps, cotenant severance, who is liable for rent after assignments, recording-act outcomes, foreclosure distributions. The question often quotes the statute (e.g., the recording act); read it carefully. See [`mbe-rules.md`](mbe-rules.md).
- **California essays:** general principles applied to rich facts. Recurring structures: a title timeline ("What interests do A, B, C hold?"), a landlord-tenant dispute (claims and counterclaims), or an enforceability analysis (covenant, easement). Add California points in one sentence each. See [`essay-templates.md`](essay-templates.md) and [`ca-distinctions.md`](ca-distinctions.md).
