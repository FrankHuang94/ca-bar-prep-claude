# Real Property — rule statements

## I.A Present estates and future interests

### R-PRP-001 — Fee simple absolute
**Rule (write this):** A fee simple absolute is the largest estate in land: it is of potentially infinite duration, freely alienable, devisable and descendible, and is not cut short by any condition. At common law it was created by "to A and his heirs" ("to A" = words of purchase; "and his heirs" = words of limitation, giving the heirs nothing); today a grant "to A" presumptively passes a fee simple.

**Elements:** 1. Grant with no durational or conditional limit. 2. No words of inheritance needed (modern rule). 3. The heirs of a living person have only an expectancy, not an interest.

**Exceptions / defenses:** The fee tail ("to A and the heirs of his body") is abolished in most states; in California a would-be fee tail is a fee simple (Civ. Code § 763) [S-774]. Precatory words ("hoping that she will keep it in the family") do not cut down a fee.

**Authority:** Common law; Civ. Code §§ 762, 1072 (words of inheritance unnecessary), 1105 (fee simple presumed to pass by a grant unless a lesser estate appears).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** O deeds Blackacre "to A." A has a fee simple absolute; A's son has nothing while A lives and takes only if A dies intestate still owning the land.

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: there is no private fee ownership of land in the PRC; the nearest analogue to a fee is a long-term construction-land use right (建设用地使用权), which has a fixed term (approx.).

### R-PRP-002 — Fee simple determinable and possibility of reverter
**Rule (write this):** A fee simple determinable is a fee that ends automatically when a stated event occurs, created by durational words such as "so long as," "while," "during" or "until." The grantor keeps a possibility of reverter, and when the event happens title returns to the grantor (or successors) automatically, without any action.

**Elements:** 1. Durational language. 2. Stated event or use. 3. Future interest in grantor = possibility of reverter (often implied; need not be expressly reserved). 4. Automatic forfeiture.

**Exceptions / defenses:** The possibility of reverter is not subject to the RAP. If a third party (not the grantor) holds the future interest, it is an executory interest and the estate is a fee simple subject to executory limitation (R-PRP-004). The statute of limitations for recovering possession runs from the triggering event. California has abolished the fee simple determinable; see R-PRP-005.

**Authority:** Common law.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: apply the common-law classification. CA essay: classify under the common law, then add that California treats the interest as a fee simple subject to a condition subsequent and a power of termination (R-PRP-005).

**Example:** O conveys "to the School District so long as the land is used for school purposes." The District stops using it for a school in 2026. At common law, title reverts to O at once.

### R-PRP-003 — Fee simple subject to condition subsequent and right of entry
**Rule (write this):** A fee simple subject to a condition subsequent is a fee that the grantor may cut short if a stated condition occurs, created by conditional words such as "but if," "provided that" or "on condition that," together with an express right to re-enter. The estate does not end automatically; it continues until the holder of the right of entry (power of termination) elects to terminate it within a reasonable time.

**Elements:** 1. Conditional language. 2. Right of entry reserved to the grantor (courts usually require it expressly). 3. Breach of condition. 4. Affirmative election to terminate (notice or suit).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Waiver or estoppel if the holder unreasonably delays or accepts the breach. The right of entry is not subject to the common-law RAP. When language is ambiguous, courts prefer a condition subsequent (no automatic forfeiture), and they prefer a covenant or mere statement of purpose over either.

**Authority:** Common law; Civ. Code §§ 885.010–885.070 (California calls it a "power of termination") [S-758].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same classification; California adds the 30-year expiry of recorded powers of termination (R-PRP-005).

**Example:** O conveys "to A, but if liquor is ever sold on the premises, O may re-enter and retake." A sells liquor. A keeps title until O acts; if O waits years while knowing of the sales, A can argue waiver.

### R-PRP-004 — Fee simple subject to executory limitation
**Rule (write this):** A fee simple subject to an executory limitation is a fee that ends automatically when a stated event occurs, with title then passing to a third party, not back to the grantor. The third party's interest is a shifting executory interest, which is subject to the rule against perpetuities.

**Elements:** 1. Fee simple. 2. Divesting event. 3. Future interest in a transferee (not the grantor). 4. RAP check on the executory interest.

**Exceptions / defenses:** If the executory interest violates the RAP, strike it; the grantee's estate becomes a fee simple determinable (if durational words were used, with a possibility of reverter in the grantor) or a fee simple absolute (if conditional words were used).

**Authority:** Common law (Statute of Uses 1536 made executory interests possible).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule. In California, the RAP check uses USRAP (R-PRP-016).

**Example:** O conveys Lot B "to Barry, but if the lot is ever used other than as a personal residence, to Zach's descendants." Barry has a fee simple subject to executory limitation; Zach's descendants have a shifting executory interest. Because the use could change more than 21 years after any life in being, the gift over fails at common law; under USRAP, wait 90 years.

### R-PRP-005 — Construing defeasible language; California's statutory treatment
**Rule (write this):** Courts construe grants to avoid forfeiture: words of motive or purpose ("for the purpose of a park") create a fee simple absolute, ambiguous words are read as a condition subsequent rather than a determinable fee, and a restriction may also be read as a covenant enforceable by damages or injunction. In California, fees simple determinable and possibilities of reverter are abolished; a would-be determinable fee is a fee simple subject to a restriction in the form of a condition subsequent, and the grantor holds a power of termination that expires 30 years after recording unless a notice of intent to preserve is recorded.

**Elements:** 1. Classify the language. 2. Check whether a future interest was created and in whom. 3. California: convert FSD to condition subsequent; check the 30-year limit and whether the restriction has become obsolete.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A power of termination that is obsolete (the restriction no longer benefits anyone substantially) is unenforceable in California (Civ. Code § 885.040) [S-758]. Charitable or public grantees may also invoke changed conditions.

**Authority:** Civ. Code §§ 885.010, 885.020, 885.030, 885.040 [S-758]; *Walton v. City of Red Bluff* (1991) 2 Cal.App.4th 117 [S-759].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: common-law FSD and possibility of reverter still exist. CA essay: give the common-law answer first (graders expect it), then "In California, under Civ. Code § 885.020, the possibility of reverter is treated as a power of termination, so forfeiture is not automatic."

**Example:** O conveys in 1990 "to the City so long as used as a library." In 2026 the City closes the library. Common law: automatic reverter to O. California: O holds a power of termination, but it expired in 2020 unless O recorded a notice of intent to preserve.

### R-PRP-006 — Life estates
**Rule (write this):** A life estate is a possessory estate measured by the life of the grantee ("to A for life") or of another person ("to A for the life of B," a life estate pur autre vie). The life tenant may possess, use, lease and transfer the estate, but any transferee takes only for the measuring life, and the estate ends at the measuring life's death.

**Elements:** 1. Words limiting duration to a life. 2. Future interest following it (reversion in grantor or remainder in a transferee). 3. Alienable but only for the measuring life.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A lease by a life tenant ends at the life tenant's death. A life estate pur autre vie passes by will or intestacy if the life tenant dies before the measuring life. A life estate can be defeasible ("to W for life or until she remarries"). A legal life estate cannot be sold in fee without all future interest holders unless a court orders sale for waste or necessity.

**Authority:** Common law; *Baker v. Weedon*, 262 So.2d 641 (Miss. 1972) (judicial sale only to protect all interests).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** O deeds "to A for life, then to B." A leases to T for ten years and dies in year three. T's lease ends; B takes possession.

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: the PRC Civil Code's right of habitation (居住权) is a personal lifetime use right somewhat like a life estate, but it is generally non-transferable (approx.).

### R-PRP-007 — Life tenant's duties and waste
**Rule (write this):** A life tenant must not commit waste, that is, conduct that unreasonably injures the interests of the future interest holders. Affirmative (voluntary) waste is harmful conduct such as exploiting natural resources; permissive waste is failure to make ordinary repairs, pay property taxes or pay interest on a mortgage, up to the income or rental value of the land; ameliorative waste is a change that increases value but alters the property's character, allowed today only if the future interest holders would not reasonably object or the neighborhood has changed.

**Elements:** 1. Life tenant (or lessee, cotenant, mortgagor) in possession. 2. Act or omission. 3. Type of waste. 4. Remedies: damages, injunction, and sometimes forfeiture or treble damages by statute.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Open-mines doctrine: a life tenant may continue mines already open when the estate began, or take resources for repairs and reasonable fuel. No duty to insure the remainder. The life tenant must pay interest but not principal on a mortgage; principal is the remainderman's burden.

**Authority:** Common law; *Melms v. Pabst Brewing Co.*, 104 Wis. 7, 79 N.W. 738 (1899) (ameliorative waste allowed where conditions changed); CCP § 732 (waste action; treble damages) [VERIFY V-663].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule. California follows the common-law categories.

**Example:** Life tenant L lets the roof leak for years and stops paying property taxes although the house could be rented for $3,000 a month. Remainderman R may sue for permissive waste and recover the cost of repairs and the taxes, up to the rental value.

### R-PRP-008 — Reversions
**Rule (write this):** A reversion is the future interest left in a grantor who transfers a lesser estate than he holds and does not provide who takes when it ends. Reversions are always vested, are freely transferable, devisable and descendible, and are not subject to the rule against perpetuities.

**Elements:** 1. Grantor conveys less than the full estate (life estate, term of years). 2. No transferee named to take afterward, or a named remainder might fail.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A grantor who creates a contingent remainder keeps a reversion that may never become possessory. A reversion differs from a possibility of reverter (after a determinable fee) and a right of entry (after a condition subsequent).

**Authority:** Common law.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** O conveys "to A for life, then to B if B reaches 25." B is 10. O has a reversion that becomes possessory if A dies before B turns 25. Under modern law B can still take when B turns 25 (B's interest then divests O as an executory interest); at common law, destructibility would have ended B's remainder (R-PRP-010, R-PRP-012).

### R-PRP-009 — Vested remainders
**Rule (write this):** A remainder is a future interest in a transferee that can become possessory on the natural end of the prior estate. It is vested if it is given to an ascertained person and is not subject to any condition precedent other than the end of the prior estate. A vested remainder may be indefeasibly vested, vested subject to open (a class that can still gain members), or vested subject to complete divestment (a condition subsequent can take it away).

**Elements:** 1. Future interest in a transferee. 2. Follows a life estate or term of years (never a fee). 3. Ascertained taker. 4. No condition precedent. 5. Classify the subtype.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Read the conditional words in order: "to A for life, then to B, but if B dies before A, to C" gives B a vested remainder subject to complete divestment (the condition comes after the gift), while "to A for life, then to B if B survives A" gives B a contingent remainder. Vested remainders subject to open are subject to the RAP; indefeasibly vested remainders are not.

**Authority:** Common law; Civ. Code §§ 694 (vested future interest defined), 695 (contingent future interest defined) [S-1606].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** O conveys "to A for life, then to the children of B." B has one child, C. C has a vested remainder subject to open; later-born children of B join the class until it closes (R-PRP-013).

### R-PRP-010 — Contingent remainders
**Rule (write this):** A remainder is contingent if it is given to an unborn or unascertained person, or is subject to a condition precedent. When a contingent remainder is created, the grantor keeps a reversion. Contingent remainders are subject to the rule against perpetuities and, at modern law, are freely transferable.

**Elements:** 1. Remainder (follows a life estate or term). 2. Taker unascertained (e.g., "heirs of B" while B lives) or condition precedent ("if B marries"). 3. Alternative contingent remainders where both gifts are conditioned ("to B if she reaches 30, and if not, to C").

**Exceptions / defenses:** A contingent remainder that is still contingent when the prior estate ends was destroyed at common law (destructibility); see R-PRP-012. Today, if the remainder is still contingent, the grantor's reversion becomes possessory and the remainder can later spring into possession as an executory interest.

**Authority:** Common law; Civ. Code § 695 (contingent while the person or the event remains uncertain) [S-1606].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** O conveys "to A for life, then to the heirs of B." B is alive. B's heirs are unascertained, so the remainder is contingent, and O has a reversion.

### R-PRP-011 — Executory interests
**Rule (write this):** An executory interest is a future interest in a transferee that can become possessory only by cutting short a prior estate or by springing from the grantor at a future time. A shifting executory interest divests a prior transferee; a springing executory interest divests the grantor. Executory interests are subject to the rule against perpetuities.

**Elements:** 1. Future interest in a transferee. 2. Cannot take on the natural end of the prior estate (it divests, or there is a gap). 3. Classify as shifting or springing. 4. RAP check.

**Exceptions / defenses:** If an interest could qualify as a remainder, it is a remainder, not an executory interest (the rule of preference for remainders).

**Authority:** Common law; Statute of Uses (1536).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule. California tests the RAP under USRAP (R-PRP-016).

**Example:** O conveys "to A when she marries." A is single. O keeps a fee simple subject to A's springing executory interest.

### R-PRP-012 — Destructibility, Rule in Shelley's Case, Doctrine of Worthier Title
**Rule (write this):** Three old rules may alter the grantor's stated plan, but each has been abolished in most states. (1) Destructibility: a legal contingent remainder that had not vested when the prior estate ended was destroyed. (2) Rule in Shelley's Case: "to A for life, then to A's heirs" gave A a fee simple. (3) Doctrine of Worthier Title: "to A for life, then to O's heirs" left a reversion in O, not a remainder in O's heirs (now at most a rebuttable rule of construction).

**Elements:** For each: identify the pattern, state the common-law result, then state the modern rule (abolished or rule of construction).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Merger: when a life estate and the next vested estate come into the same hands, they merge, which at common law could destroy an intervening contingent remainder.

**Authority:** Common law. California: fee tail abolished (Civ. Code § 763); the remainder to a life tenant's heirs takes as a remainder, not by merger (Civ. Code § 779), which rejects the Shelley result; worthier title is not part of California law (Prob. Code § 21108) [S-774]; destructibility of contingent remainders abolished (Civ. Code § 741) [VERIFY V-652].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: assume these rules are abolished unless the question says the common law applies unchanged; know both results. CA essay: mention the common-law rule in one sentence and state that California has abolished it.

**Example:** O conveys "to A for life, then to A's heirs." Under Shelley, A has a fee simple and can sell it; under modern law, A has a life estate and A's heirs a contingent remainder.

### R-PRP-013 — Class gifts and the rule of convenience
**Rule (write this):** A class gift is a gift to a group described by a common characteristic ("children of B"). Absent contrary intent, the class closes under the rule of convenience when any member is entitled to demand possession of a share; persons born afterward are excluded, but those conceived before closing (in gestation) are included. A class also closes physiologically when the parent dies.

**Elements:** 1. Gift to a class. 2. Identify when a member can demand distribution (at the grantor's death for an outright gift; at the end of a life estate for a remainder). 3. Close the class then. 4. Members already in the class keep shares even if they die before distribution (absent a survival condition).

**Exceptions / defenses:** If no member exists when the gift takes effect, the class stays open until the parent's death. Adopted children are generally included. For anti-lapse and wills-based class gifts, see R-WIL-066.

**Authority:** Common law rule of convenience.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** O devises "to A for life, then to B's grandchildren." At A's death, B has two grandchildren; a third is born a year later. The class closed at A's death, so the third grandchild takes nothing.

### R-PRP-014 — Common-law rule against perpetuities
**Rule (write this):** Under the common-law rule against perpetuities, no interest is good unless it must vest, if at all, not later than 21 years after the death of some life in being at the creation of the interest. The rule applies to contingent remainders, executory interests, vested remainders subject to open (class gifts) and options and rights of first refusal not attached to a lease; an interest that might vest too late is void from the start.

**Elements:** 1. Identify each interest subject to the rule. 2. Find when it is created (deed: delivery; will: testator's death; revocable trust: when it becomes irrevocable). 3. State what must happen for it to vest. 4. Find a measuring life who will reveal, within 21 years after death, whether the interest vests. 5. If any possible scenario vests it later, strike the interest and reread the grant without it.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Not subject to the rule: interests in the grantor (reversion, possibility of reverter, right of entry), indefeasibly vested remainders, vested remainders subject to complete divestment, and gifts from one charity to another charity.

**Authority:** Common law; J. C. Gray's classic statement.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: apply the common-law rule unless the question supplies a wait-and-see or USRAP statute. CA essay: apply the common-law rule, then state that California applies USRAP's 90-year wait-and-see alternative (R-PRP-016).

**Example:** O conveys "to A for life, then to the first of A's children to reach 25." A could have a child after O's conveyance who reaches 25 more than 21 years after A's death. The remainder is void at common law; O has a reversion after A's life estate.

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: the PRC has no rule against perpetuities because long-chain private future interests in land do not exist there; think of it as a US limit on "dead-hand" control (approx.).

### R-PRP-015 — RAP applications and classic traps
**Rule (write this):** The common-law rule voids interests based on what might happen, not what is likely to happen. A class gift is all-or-nothing: if the interest of any potential class member might vest too late, the whole class gift fails. Classic traps are the fertile octogenarian (anyone is presumed able to have children), the unborn widow (a spouse not yet identified), and the administrative contingency (an event that might occur after the period, such as "when the estate is probated").

**Elements:** 1. Class gift all-or-nothing. 2. Fertility presumed regardless of age. 3. Spouses described generally might be born after the grant. 4. Contingencies tied to open-ended events fail. 5. Commercial interests: options in gross and (majority) rights of first refusal are subject to the rule; an option held by a tenant to buy the leased premises is not.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Gifts to a class closed at creation (e.g., "children of A" where A is dead) are valid. A gift "to A's children for life, then to A's grandchildren" in a will where A is alive fails as to the grandchildren; if A is dead at the testator's death, it is valid.

**Authority:** Common law; *Jee v. Audley* (1787) (fertile octogenarian).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE tests these traps directly. CA essay: identify the trap, then note USRAP's wait-and-see and reformation would likely save the gift (R-PRP-016).

**Example:** T's will leaves "to my grandchildren who reach 21." T's children are alive at T's death and could have more children. The gift fails at common law, because a grandchild born later might turn 21 more than 21 years after all lives in being die. Under USRAP the gift is valid if it actually vests within 90 years.

### R-PRP-016 — RAP reforms and California's USRAP
**Rule (write this):** Most states have reformed the RAP. Under the Uniform Statutory Rule Against Perpetuities, adopted in California, a nonvested interest is valid if it either satisfies the common-law rule or actually vests or terminates within 90 years after its creation (wait-and-see). If it does neither, a court reforms the disposition to approximate the transferor's plan within the 90-year period.

**Elements:** 1. Test under the common-law rule; if valid, done. 2. If not, wait 90 years. 3. If it has not vested within 90 years (or is certain not to), reform. 4. Check exclusions, including most nondonative (commercial) transfers.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Other reforms: "wait-and-see" for the common-law period, cy pres reformation, and statutory fixes such as reducing age contingencies to 21 and presuming fertility ends at a stated age. USRAP does not apply to most commercial transactions (options, leases, rights of first refusal arising from a nondonative transfer) (Prob. Code § 21225) [S-766].

**Authority:** Prob. Code §§ 21200 et seq., 21205, 21220, 21225 [S-766]. See also R-TRS-034 (trusts).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: use the common law unless the facts give the statute. CA essay: "Under the common law the interest is void because …; however, California has adopted USRAP (Prob. Code § 21205), so the interest is valid if it vests within 90 years, and otherwise the court may reform it (§ 21220)."

**Example:** A 2026 deed gives an executory interest "to Z's descendants if the land is ever used commercially." It fails at common law. Under California's USRAP, it is valid if the commercial use (if any) occurs by 2116.

### R-PRP-017 — Restraints on alienation
**Rule (write this):** A disabling restraint (the grantee cannot transfer) on a fee simple is void; a forfeiture restraint (the estate is forfeited on transfer) and a promissory restraint (the grantee promises not to transfer) on a fee simple are also generally void. Reasonable restraints that serve a legitimate purpose and are limited in time or scope, such as rights of first refusal, lease anti-assignment clauses and condominium restrictions, are valid.

**Elements:** 1. Identify the restraint type. 2. Identify the estate restrained (fee vs. life estate or leasehold). 3. Test reasonableness (purpose, duration, scope).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Forfeiture and promissory restraints on life estates and leaseholds are usually valid. Spendthrift trusts are valid (see R-TRS-041). Due-on-sale clauses are enforceable under federal law (R-PRP-096).

**Authority:** Common law; Civ. Code § 711 ("Conditions restraining alienation, when repugnant to the interest created, are void").

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** O deeds Blackacre "to A in fee simple, but A shall never sell it." The restraint is void; A owns Blackacre free of it.

### R-PRP-018 — Alienability, descendibility and devisability of interests
**Rule (write this):** Present estates and vested future interests are freely transferable during life, devisable by will and descendible by intestacy. At modern law, contingent remainders and executory interests are also transferable inter vivos and, unless survival is a condition of the interest, devisable and descendible; possibilities of reverter and rights of entry are descendible and devisable and, in most states, transferable inter vivos.

**Elements:** 1. Identify the interest. 2. Ask whether it is conditioned on survival (if so, it ends with the holder's death). 3. Ask whether the jurisdiction limits inter vivos transfer of rights of entry.

**Exceptions / defenses:** At common law, a right of entry could not be transferred inter vivos, and a purported transfer could destroy it in some states. California makes future interests transferable (Civ. Code § 699) and enforces a power of termination for the successor of the original holder subject to the 30-year rule (§ 885.030) [S-758].

**Authority:** Common law; Civ. Code §§ 699, 885.010–885.070 [S-758].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule. Tested on MBE as "who holds the interest after X died intestate?"

**Example:** O conveys "to A for life, then to B." B dies before A, leaving a will giving everything to C. B's vested remainder passes to C, who takes on A's death.

## I.B Cotenancy

### R-PRP-019 — Tenancy in common
**Rule (write this):** A tenancy in common is concurrent ownership in which each cotenant holds a separate, undivided share with an equal right to possess the whole. There is no right of survivorship: each share is transferable, devisable and descendible. A conveyance to two or more persons is presumed to create a tenancy in common unless a joint tenancy is clearly expressed.

**Elements:** 1. Two or more owners. 2. Only the unity of possession is required. 3. Shares may be unequal.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A conveyance to spouses may create community property (California; R-CMP-012, R-CMP-013) or a tenancy by the entirety (other states). Heirs who inherit together take as tenants in common.

**Authority:** Common law; Civ. Code §§ 685, 686 (interest in common; presumption).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** O devises Greenacre "to A and B." A and B are tenants in common; when A dies, A's half passes under A's will, not to B.

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: a tenancy in common resembles 按份共有 (co-ownership by shares) under the PRC Civil Code, with the US difference that each cotenant may possess the whole (approx.).

### R-PRP-020 — Joint tenancy: creation
**Rule (write this):** A joint tenancy is concurrent ownership with a right of survivorship: when one joint tenant dies, the decedent's interest ends and the survivors own the whole. At common law it requires the four unities of time, title, interest and possession, plus clear language of survivorship ("as joint tenants with right of survivorship").

**Elements:** 1. Time: interests acquired at the same time. 2. Title: by the same instrument. 3. Interest: equal, identical shares. 4. Possession: equal right to possess the whole. 5. Express intent.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Most states, including California, let an owner create a joint tenancy by conveying to herself and another, without a straw person. California requires that the joint tenancy be expressly declared in the transfer (Civ. Code § 683). Unequal contributions do not prevent a joint tenancy but can matter for contribution claims.

**Authority:** Common law; Civ. Code § 683.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** O conveys "to A and B as joint tenants with right of survivorship." A dies with a will leaving everything to C. B owns all of the land; C takes nothing, because the will operates only at death, when A's interest ends.

### R-PRP-021 — Severance of a joint tenancy by conveyance
**Rule (write this):** A joint tenant may sever unilaterally by conveying her interest during life, without the other's consent or knowledge; the conveyance turns the transferred share into a tenancy in common, while any remaining joint tenants keep a joint tenancy among themselves. In California, and in a growing number of states (the modern trend), a joint tenant may also sever unilaterally by a deed to herself or by a written declaration; in California the instrument must be recorded in time to cut off the other joint tenants' survivorship.

**Elements:** 1. Inter vivos transfer of a joint tenant's interest (to a third party or, in California, to herself). 2. Effect: the transferee is a tenant in common with the others. 3. California: to defeat survivorship, the severing instrument must be recorded before death, or executed and acknowledged no earlier than three days before death and recorded within seven days after death.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A will cannot sever (it takes effect only at death). In California, a severing instrument that misses the § 683.2(c) recording deadlines does not terminate the other joint tenants' right of survivorship in the severing tenant's share [S-760]. Spousal joint tenancy may also raise community property issues (R-CMP-014).

**Authority:** Civ. Code § 683.2 [S-760]; *Riddle v. Harmon* (1980) 102 Cal.App.3d 524 (self-conveyance severs); *Estate of England* (1991) 233 Cal.App.3d 1 [S-761].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: a conveyance to a third party severs; the traditional rule required a straw person for self-conveyance (modern trend: a self-deed severs). CA essay: unilateral self-conveyance severs (*Riddle*), subject to the § 683.2 recording rule.

**Example:** A, B and C are joint tenants. A deeds her share to D. D is a tenant in common with a one-third share; B and C remain joint tenants of two-thirds. If B dies, C owns two-thirds and D one-third.

### R-PRP-022 — Severance by mortgage, lease, contract or killing
**Rule (write this):** Whether a mortgage by one joint tenant severs depends on the mortgage theory: in a title-theory state the mortgage transfers title and severs; in most lien-theory states (lien theory is the majority, and California's rule) it does not sever, and if the mortgagor dies first the survivor takes free of the lien, although lien-theory courts are not uniform on this point. A lease by one joint tenant does not sever in California and ends when the lessor dies; a specifically enforceable contract to sell severs in most states; a joint tenant who feloniously kills another forfeits survivorship.

**Elements:** 1. Identify the act. 2. Apply the state's mortgage theory. 3. Lease: majority, no severance (some courts treat it as a temporary severance). 4. Contract of sale: equitable conversion severs. 5. Killing: severance and no benefit to the killer.

**Exceptions / defenses:** If both joint tenants sign the mortgage, it survives either one's death. If the mortgagor survives, the lien attaches to the whole. A few lien-theory courts reach a different result, so on the MBE follow the theory and any rule the question states.

**Authority:** *People v. Nogarr* (1958) 164 Cal.App.2d 591 and *Hamel v. Gootkin* (1962) 202 Cal.App.2d 27 (mortgage/deed of trust by one joint tenant does not sever; survivor takes free) [S-765]; *Tenhet v. Boswell* (1976) 18 Cal.3d 150 (lease does not sever; ends at lessor's death) [S-765]; *Harms v. Sprague*, 105 Ill. 2d 215, 473 N.E.2d 930 (1984); Prob. Code § 251 (killing) [VERIFY V-664].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: lien theory is the majority, and most lien-theory courts find no severance; know both theories. CA essay: California is a lien-theory state, so the survivor takes free of a co-owner's sole mortgage (*Nogarr*).

**Example:** A and B are joint tenants. A alone gives Bank a deed of trust and dies. In California, B owns the land free of Bank's lien; Bank is left with an unsecured claim against A's estate.

### R-PRP-023 — Tenancy by the entirety; California's spousal forms
**Rule (write this):** In about half the states, a tenancy by the entirety is a joint tenancy between spouses with the four unities plus marriage; neither spouse can sever, transfer or encumber it alone, and creditors of one spouse cannot reach it. Divorce converts it into a tenancy in common. California does not recognize tenancy by the entirety; spouses may hold as community property, community property with right of survivorship, joint tenants or tenants in common.

**Elements:** 1. Married couple. 2. Five unities. 3. No unilateral severance. 4. Ends by death, divorce or joint conveyance.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Federal tax liens can reach entireties property in some circumstances.

**Authority:** Common law; Civ. Code §§ 682, 682.1 (community property with right of survivorship). Cross-reference R-CMP-012 to R-CMP-014, R-CMP-098.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: tenancy by the entirety may appear and is tested on creditor and severance questions. CA essay: say California does not recognize it and analyze the spouses' title under community property law.

**Example:** H and W own a house as tenants by the entirety. H's creditor obtains a judgment against H alone. In an entirety state, the creditor cannot levy on the house while both live.

### R-PRP-024 — Cotenants: possession, ouster and rents
**Rule (write this):** Each cotenant may possess and use the entire property, so a cotenant in sole possession owes no rent to the others unless there has been an ouster (a refusal to admit a cotenant, or an express claim of exclusive ownership) or an agreement. A cotenant who collects rent from third parties, or profits from exploiting the land's resources, must account to the others for their shares of the net amounts.

**Elements:** 1. Possession by one cotenant. 2. Ouster? (demand to share refused, lockout, or claim of sole title communicated). 3. Third-party rents or extraction profits must be shared. 4. Adverse possession against a cotenant requires ouster plus the statutory period.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A minority of states charge an occupying cotenant fair rental value. A cotenant's sole possession is presumed permissive, so it is not adverse until ouster.

**Authority:** Common law; *Spiller v. Mackereth*, 334 So. 2d 859 (Ala. 1976) (ouster requires refusal of a demand to share possession).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** A and B inherit a cabin. A lives there alone for five years; B never asks to use it. B cannot recover rent from A. If A had rented it to a tenant for $1,000 a month, B would get half of the net rent.

### R-PRP-025 — Cotenants: contribution, improvements, accounting and waste
**Rule (write this):** A cotenant who pays more than her share of property taxes or mortgage payments may obtain contribution from the others, but a cotenant in sole possession can claim contribution only to the extent the payments exceed the rental value of her occupancy. There is no right to contribution for repairs or improvements made without agreement, but in a partition or accounting the improving cotenant gets credit for the value added (and bears any loss).

**Elements:** 1. Carrying costs (taxes, mortgage interest, insurance): contribution. 2. Necessary repairs: no affirmative action in most states; credit in accounting. 3. Improvements: credit limited to value added, in partition or accounting. 4. Waste by a cotenant: liability to the others.

**Exceptions / defenses:** An occupying cotenant's credit for payments is offset by the value of exclusive use when she seeks contribution.

**Authority:** Common law; CCP § 872.140 (partition court may adjust equities among cotenants) [VERIFY V-672].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule. Past CA essays ask for "an accounting and contribution."

**Example:** Paul lives alone on jointly owned land and pays all taxes ($6,000). Lenny cannot force Paul to share a new barn's cost, but Lenny may owe $3,000 toward taxes, reduced by any rental value Paul enjoyed beyond his share.

### R-PRP-026 — Partition
**Rule (write this):** Any cotenant (TIC or JT, but not a tenant by the entirety) may sue for partition. Courts prefer partition in kind (physical division); they order a partition by sale and division of the proceeds when physical division is impracticable or would be unfair or greatly reduce value. In the partition, the court adjusts the parties' equities (taxes, improvements, rents).

**Elements:** 1. Concurrent ownership. 2. No valid agreement waiving partition. 3. In kind vs. sale. 4. Accounting of contributions and credits.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A reasonable agreement not to partition for a limited time is enforceable. California's Partition of Real Property Act (CCP § 874.311 et seq.) applies to tenancies in common without a governing written agreement, in actions filed on or after January 1, 2023: it gives cotenants a right to buy out the plaintiff's share at appraised value, prefers partition in kind, and requires open-market sale through a broker rather than auction [S-772].

**Authority:** Common law; CCP § 872.010 et seq.; CCP § 874.311 et seq. [S-772].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: the preference for partition in kind. CA essay: state the general rule and mention the 2023 Act's buyout right if the property is a tenancy in common.

**Example:** Siblings own a 400-acre ranch with one house. A court can divide the acreage in kind with a payment (owelty) to equalize values, rather than ordering a forced sale.

## I.C Landlord-tenant law

### R-PRP-027 — Types of tenancies
**Rule (write this):** There are four leasehold estates. A tenancy for years lasts a fixed period and ends automatically without notice; a periodic tenancy runs from period to period (month to month, year to year) until either party gives proper notice; a tenancy at will has no fixed duration and ends when either party chooses (or at either's death); a tenancy at sufferance arises when a tenant wrongfully holds over after the lease ends.

**Elements:** 1. Fixed end date → term of years (a lease over one year must satisfy the statute of frauds). 2. Rent by period with no end date, or an oral lease void under the statute of frauds followed by possession and periodic rent → periodic. 3. No term and no periodic rent → at will. 4. Holdover → at sufferance until the landlord elects (R-PRP-029).

**Exceptions / defenses:** A lease "for as long as both parties wish" is at will; one terminable only by the tenant is usually construed as a determinable life estate or a periodic tenancy, not at will (split). In California, a hiring of real property with no fixed term is presumed to be for the period by which rent is paid, and month to month if not otherwise designated (Civ. Code §§ 1943, 1944); a tenancy at will requires at least 30 days' written notice to terminate (Civ. Code § 789).

**Authority:** Common law; Civ. Code §§ 789, 1091, 1624(a)(3), 1943, 1944.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** L and T agree orally on a two-year lease; T moves in and pays $2,000 monthly. The two-year term is unenforceable under the statute of frauds, but T is a month-to-month periodic tenant.

### R-PRP-028 — Terminating a periodic tenancy; notice
**Rule (write this):** A periodic tenancy continues until either party gives notice. At common law, notice must equal one full period (but six months for a year-to-year tenancy) and must end the tenancy at the end of a period. In California, notice must be at least as long as the rental period but need not exceed 30 days; for a residential tenancy, a landlord must give 60 days' notice if the tenant has occupied for a year or more, otherwise 30 days.

**Elements:** 1. Classify the tenancy. 2. Proper length of notice. 3. Timed to end with a period (common law). 4. California overlay: just-cause rules under the Tenant Protection Act after 12 months (R-PRP-046) and local ordinances.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Defective notice is generally effective for the next proper date (majority). Retaliatory or discriminatory termination is barred (R-PRP-038, R-PRP-048).

**Authority:** Common law; Civ. Code §§ 1946, 1946.1.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: common-law notice. CA essay: give the common-law rule, then the California 30/60-day rule and the just-cause requirement.

**Example:** On March 10, L tells month-to-month tenant T to leave "by March 31." At common law, the notice is too short; it takes effect at the end of April.

### R-PRP-029 — Holdover tenants
**Rule (write this):** When a tenant for years stays after the lease ends, the landlord may elect either to evict the tenant (and recover damages for the holdover period) or to bind the tenant to a new periodic tenancy. Most courts make the new tenancy year to year if the original lease was for a year or more (month to month for residential leases in many states), on the same terms so far as applicable.

**Elements:** 1. Tenant remains after the term. 2. Landlord's election (express, or by accepting rent). 3. Duration and terms of the new tenancy.

**Exceptions / defenses:** No holdover if the delay is brief and caused by circumstances beyond the tenant's control (e.g., serious illness), or if a few belongings are left behind. If the landlord announced a rent increase before the term ended, the tenant who holds over is bound by it. In California, accepting rent after the term ends presumes a renewal on the same terms for the rent period, not exceeding one month when rent is payable monthly and never more than one year (Civ. Code § 1945).

**Authority:** Common law; Civ. Code § 1945; CCP § 1161(1) (unlawful detainer for holdover).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: the landlord's election and the year-to-year default. CA essay: add § 1945.

**Example:** T's two-year commercial lease ends December 31; T stays and L accepts the January rent. L has elected a new periodic tenancy (year to year under the majority rule; month to month under § 1945 if rent is monthly).

### R-PRP-030 — Landlord's duty to deliver possession
**Rule (write this):** Under the majority (English) rule, the landlord must deliver actual possession at the start of the term, so if a prior tenant or trespasser is holding over, the landlord is in breach: the new tenant may terminate and recover damages, or affirm the lease, withhold rent for the period of exclusion and recover damages. Under the minority (American) rule, the landlord need deliver only the legal right to possession, and the tenant's remedy is against the wrongful occupant.

**Elements:** 1. Lease term begins. 2. Third party in possession without right (or with paramount title). 3. Which rule? 4. Tenant's election: terminate or affirm with abatement and damages; damages for reasonable costs (alternative premises, storage).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Under either rule the landlord is liable if the person in possession holds paramount title. The tenant must act within a reasonable time; continuing to wait and then demanding full damages raises mitigation issues. California has no clear holding; the statutory duty to "secure to the hirer the quiet possession" (Civ. Code § 1927) supports the English rule [S-778] [VERIFY V-651].

**Authority:** English rule: majority and Restatement (Second) of Property (Landlord and Tenant) position [VERIFY V-651]; American rule: *Hannan v. Dusch*, 154 Va. 356, 153 S.E. 824 (1930); Civ. Code § 1927 [S-778].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: if the jurisdiction is not stated, use the majority English rule. CA essay: state both rules, apply both if the facts allow, and conclude under the English rule. Tested Feb 2019 Q3 and Feb 2023 Q3 [VERIFY V-071].

**Example:** L leases an apartment to T from June 1, but prior tenant R does not leave until June 15. Under the English rule, T owes no rent for June 1–15 and may recover hotel costs; under the American rule, T must pay rent and sue R.

### R-PRP-031 — Rent; security deposits
**Rule (write this):** The tenant must pay the agreed rent; if the tenant fails to pay, the landlord may sue for rent as it comes due and, under most leases and statutes, terminate and evict. A security deposit secures the tenant's obligations; the landlord must return it, less itemized deductions for unpaid rent, damage beyond ordinary wear and tear and cleaning, within the statutory period. In California, a residential landlord must return the deposit within 21 days after the tenant vacates, and the deposit is capped at one month's rent (two months for a small landlord) for tenancies covered by the 2024 amendment.

**Elements:** 1. Rent covenant (independent of landlord's covenants at common law; dependent under modern residential law). 2. Deposit limits. 3. Return and itemization. 4. Bad-faith retention penalty.

**Exceptions / defenses:** The tenant's duty to pay rent is suspended by actual or constructive eviction (R-PRP-034, R-PRP-035) and reduced by a habitability breach (R-PRP-036). Rent-acceleration clauses are enforceable if they are not penalties. California's small-landlord exception allows two months' rent for natural persons (or LLCs of natural persons) owning no more than two residential rental properties with no more than four units in total, but not for a service-member tenant [S-750] [S-751].

**Authority:** Common law; Civ. Code § 1950.5 (as amended by AB 12 (2023), effective July 1, 2024) [S-750] [S-751].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: common-law rent duty. CA essay: mention § 1950.5 only if the facts involve a deposit.

**Example:** In 2026 L, a corporation, demands a $6,000 deposit plus first month's rent of $3,000 for an unfurnished apartment. Under § 1950.5 as amended, the deposit may not exceed $3,000.

### R-PRP-032 — Tenant's duties: waste, repairs, lawful use
**Rule (write this):** A tenant must not commit waste, must make ordinary repairs needed to keep the premises in the condition received (reasonable wear and tear excepted) unless the lease or statute puts that duty on the landlord, and must not use the premises for an illegal purpose. The landlord may terminate the lease if the tenant uses the premises illegally, and may recover damages for waste.

**Elements:** 1. Waste categories as for life tenants (R-PRP-007). 2. Repair: ordinary maintenance, not major structural repair. 3. Illegal use: landlord's remedies. 4. Fixtures: the tenant may remove trade, domestic and ornamental fixtures before the lease ends if removal causes no substantial damage (R-PRP-069).

**Exceptions / defenses:** A tenant who covenants to "repair" was at common law liable even for rebuilding after a fire; modern courts and leases usually exclude casualty. California residential tenants have statutory duties (keep the unit clean and sanitary, dispose of garbage, use fixtures properly, not damage the premises) (Civ. Code § 1941.2).

**Authority:** Common law; Civ. Code §§ 1019, 1941.2.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** A commercial tenant knocks down an interior wall to enlarge a showroom without consent. Even if the value increases, the landlord may recover restoration costs as waste unless the lease permits alterations.

### R-PRP-033 — Abandonment by tenant; duty to mitigate
**Rule (write this):** When a tenant abandons the premises before the term ends without justification, the landlord may (1) accept the surrender and terminate the lease, ending future rent liability; (2) relet the premises on the tenant's account and hold the tenant liable for any deficiency; or (3) under the traditional rule, leave the premises vacant and sue for rent as it comes due. The modern majority rule requires the landlord to make reasonable efforts to mitigate by reletting, at least for residential leases.

**Elements:** 1. Abandonment (vacating with intent not to return and stopping rent). 2. Landlord's response (surrender by operation of law if the landlord retakes for its own use). 3. Mitigation: reasonable efforts, treating the unit like other vacant units. 4. Damages: unpaid rent plus the difference between the lease rent and the reasonably obtainable rent, plus reletting costs.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Burden of proof on mitigation: majority places it on the landlord to show reasonable efforts; California places on the tenant the burden of proving the amount that could reasonably have been avoided (Civ. Code § 1951.2). California also lets the parties agree to a "lock-in" remedy: if the lease so provides and allows reasonable subletting or assignment, the landlord may keep the lease in force and recover rent as it comes due (Civ. Code § 1951.4). A landlord may not refuse a suitable replacement tenant and then sue for full rent. A landlord with other vacant units satisfies the duty by treating the abandoned unit like the rest of its vacant stock (*Sommer*); it need not fill the abandoned unit first, and it may argue that a new tenant who would have rented one of its other vacant units anyway does not reduce its loss (a "lost volume" argument), so check whether the replacement rented the abandoned unit or a unit that was already available.

**Authority:** *Sommer v. Kridel*, 74 N.J. 446, 378 A.2d 767 (1977); Civ. Code §§ 1951.2, 1951.3, 1951.4 [VERIFY V-662].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: majority duty to mitigate. CA essay: duty to mitigate under § 1951.2 (residential and commercial), unless a valid § 1951.4 clause applies. Tested Feb 2019 Q3, Feb 2023 Q3 [VERIFY V-071].

**Example:** T abandons a one-year lease after four months. L does not advertise for six months, then relets at the same rent. L recovers only the rent for a reasonable reletting period, not all six months.

### R-PRP-034 — Covenant of quiet enjoyment; actual eviction
**Rule (write this):** Every lease contains an implied covenant of quiet enjoyment: the landlord promises that neither the landlord nor anyone with paramount title will disturb the tenant's possession. Actual eviction occurs when the landlord or a paramount title holder physically excludes the tenant from all or part of the premises; total eviction ends the tenant's rent obligation, and partial eviction by the landlord suspends all rent (majority common law) while partial eviction by a paramount title holder reduces rent proportionately.

**Elements:** 1. Lease (express or implied covenant). 2. Interference by the landlord, someone acting for the landlord, or a paramount title holder. 3. Physical exclusion (actual) or substantial interference (constructive, R-PRP-035). 4. Remedy: rent relief and damages.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Interference by a stranger (trespasser) is not a breach of the covenant; the tenant's remedy is against the trespasser. Modern authority may limit the partial-eviction rule to abatement.

**Authority:** Common law; Civ. Code § 1927 [S-778].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** L locks T out of one of three rented warehouse bays. At common law, T may stay in the other two bays and pay no rent until the eviction ends.

### R-PRP-035 — Constructive eviction
**Rule (write this):** A constructive eviction occurs when the landlord's act or failure to perform a legal duty substantially interferes with the tenant's use and enjoyment of the premises (more than a temporary inconvenience), the tenant gives notice and the landlord fails to cure within a reasonable time, and the tenant vacates within a reasonable time. The tenant is then excused from further rent and may recover damages.

**Elements:** 1. Substantial interference (e.g., no heat in winter, repeated flooding). 2. Caused by the landlord's act or breach of a duty (including failure to control other tenants' nuisance in areas the landlord controls). 3. Notice and failure to cure. 4. Tenant vacates within a reasonable time.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A tenant who stays too long waives the claim (but may still sue for damages or rely on habitability). Interference by another tenant usually does not count unless the landlord had the duty and power to stop it (common areas, lease covenants). In California, a tenant may also terminate under Civ. Code § 1932 when the landlord fails, after request, to secure quiet possession or make required repairs.

**Authority:** *Reste Realty Corp. v. Cooper*, 53 N.J. 444, 251 A.2d 268 (1969); Civ. Code § 1932.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule. On an essay, contrast constructive eviction (commercial or residential; must vacate) with the warranty of habitability (residential; may stay).

**Example:** A basement office floods every heavy rain because of a defective drain the landlord controls. After repeated complaints and no repair, T moves out within a month. T is constructively evicted and owes no further rent.

### R-PRP-036 — Implied warranty of habitability
**Rule (write this):** In residential leases, the landlord impliedly warrants that the premises are fit for human habitation at the start of the lease and will be maintained so throughout the term, measured by local housing codes and basic health and safety needs. The warranty cannot be waived, and a substantial breach entitles the tenant to remedies without moving out.

**Elements:** 1. Residential lease. 2. Condition that substantially affects health or safety (no heat, hot water, plumbing, weatherproofing, pest infestation; code violations). 3. Notice to the landlord and reasonable time to repair. 4. Condition not caused by the tenant.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Generally not implied in commercial leases (the tenant ordinarily relies on constructive eviction or an express covenant). A minority of jurisdictions imply a covenant in a commercial lease that the premises are suitable for their intended commercial purpose, where a latent defect in facilities vital to that use exists at the start of the lease (*Davidow v. Inwood North Professional Group—Phase I*, 747 S.W.2d 373 (Tex. 1988)). Minor or cosmetic defects are not a breach. California's statutory list of untenantable conditions includes lack of effective waterproofing, plumbing, gas, hot and cold water, heating, electrical lighting, clean and sanitary premises, garbage receptacles and floors and stairways in good repair (Civ. Code § 1941.1); a lease waiver is void (Civ. Code § 1942.1).

**Authority:** *Javins v. First National Realty Corp.*, 428 F.2d 1071 (D.C. Cir. 1970); *Green v. Superior Court* (1974) 10 Cal.3d 616; Civ. Code §§ 1941, 1941.1, 1942.1.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule; cite *Green* on a CA essay.

**Example:** T's apartment has no hot water for six weeks in winter despite repeated requests. L has breached the warranty; T may stay and withhold or reduce rent, repair and deduct, or sue for damages (R-PRP-037).

### R-PRP-037 — Remedies for breach of habitability
**Rule (write this):** A tenant whose landlord breaches the warranty of habitability may (1) terminate the lease and move out, (2) repair the defect and deduct the reasonable cost from rent, (3) reduce or withhold rent (often by paying it into court or escrow) until the defect is repaired, or (4) remain and sue for damages measured by the reduced rental value. The breach is also a defense to the landlord's eviction action for nonpayment.

**Elements:** 1. Breach (R-PRP-036). 2. Notice and reasonable time. 3. Chosen remedy and its limits. 4. Damages: the difference between the agreed rent and the fair rental value in the defective condition (or a percentage reduction).

**Exceptions / defenses:** California's repair-and-deduct remedy is limited to one month's rent and to twice in any 12-month period, after notice and a reasonable time (30 days is presumed reasonable) (Civ. Code § 1942). In a California unlawful detainer for nonpayment, the court determines whether there is a substantial breach of habitability and may reduce the rent owed (CCP § 1174.2) [VERIFY V-662]. A landlord may not demand or collect rent, raise the rent or serve a three-day notice to pay or quit when a dwelling that substantially lacks the § 1941.1 standards has been the subject of a public officer's written notice to abate and the conditions remain unabated, without good cause, 35 days after service of that notice (Civ. Code § 1942.4) [S-1607].

**Authority:** Common law; *Green v. Superior Court* (1974) 10 Cal.3d 616; Civ. Code §§ 1942, 1942.4; CCP § 1174.2.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule; add the California limits on an essay.

**Example:** A leaking water heater floods T's kitchen; L ignores notice for 30 days. T pays a plumber $900 (rent $2,500) and deducts it from next month's rent. Permitted under § 1942.

### R-PRP-038 — Retaliatory eviction
**Rule (write this):** A landlord may not terminate a tenancy, raise rent, reduce services or threaten eviction in retaliation for the tenant's lawful exercise of rights, such as reporting code violations, complaining to the landlord about habitability, or organizing tenants. Many statutes presume retaliation if the landlord acts within a set period after the protected act; in California the period is 180 days.

**Elements:** 1. Tenant's protected act. 2. Landlord's adverse act. 3. Causal link (presumed within the statutory period). 4. Landlord's rebuttal: a legitimate, non-retaliatory reason.

**Exceptions / defenses:** The California statutory presumption is unavailable to a tenant in default in rent, and the landlord may still evict for a good-faith valid reason stated in the notice.

**Authority:** Common law (trend); Civ. Code § 1942.5.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** Two weeks after T reports mold to the city, L serves a 30-day notice ending T's month-to-month tenancy. Retaliation is presumed; L must show a legitimate reason.

### R-PRP-039 — Assignment versus sublease; privity
**Rule (write this):** An assignment transfers the tenant's entire remaining interest in the leasehold; a sublease transfers less than the entire remaining term, so the original tenant keeps a reversion. An assignee is in privity of estate with the landlord and is directly liable for rent and for covenants that run with the land while it holds the leasehold (until it reassigns); a sublessee is in privity with neither estate nor contract with the landlord and is not directly liable to the landlord unless it assumes the lease.

**Elements:** 1. Did the tenant transfer the whole remaining term (assignment) or keep a reversion (sublease)? 2. Majority: objective test by duration; minority looks to the parties' intent or retained right of re-entry. 3. Privity map: landlord–original tenant (contract and, until assignment, estate); landlord–assignee (estate; contract only if assumed); landlord–sublessee (none).

**Exceptions / defenses:** A transfer of the whole term with a reserved right of re-entry is treated as a sublease by some courts. The landlord can still terminate the master lease for the original tenant's default and evict the sublessee.

**Authority:** Common law; *Ernst v. Conditt*, 54 Tenn. App. 328, 390 S.W.2d 703 (1964) (intent-based approach).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule. Tested Feb 2012 Q6 and Feb 2015 Q2 [VERIFY V-071].

**Example:** T, with three years left, transfers "all my rights for the balance of the term" to A. That is an assignment; L may sue A directly for unpaid rent accruing while A is in possession.

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: a PRC sublease (转租) leaves the original lease intact, much like a US sublease; a US assignment is closer to a transfer of the whole contract position (合同权利义务一并转让) (approx.).

### R-PRP-040 — Liability after assignment or sublease
**Rule (write this):** After an assignment, the original tenant remains liable to the landlord for rent and lease covenants by privity of contract, as a surety, unless the landlord releases the tenant (novation). An assignee who does not assume the lease is liable only for obligations that accrue while it holds the estate and may end that liability by reassigning; an assignee who expressly assumes the lease is liable for the whole remaining term, even after reassignment.

**Elements:** 1. Identify each party's privity. 2. Original tenant: contract liability for the entire term. 3. Non-assuming assignee: privity of estate only, while it holds the leasehold (ends on reassignment, not on vacating). 4. Assuming assignee: privity of contract (landlord is a third-party beneficiary of the assumption). 5. Sublessee: liable only to the sublessor.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Landlord's acceptance of rent from an assignee does not by itself release the original tenant. A material modification of the lease between the landlord and assignee without the original tenant's consent may release the original tenant as surety to that extent.

**Authority:** Common law.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule. A favorite MBE pattern: T assigns to A, A assigns to B, B defaults; L may sue T (contract) and B (estate), but not A unless A assumed.

**Example:** T assigns to A, who assumes the lease; A then assigns to B, who does not assume. B stops paying. L can recover from T, from A (assumption) and from B (privity of estate).

### R-PRP-041 — Covenants running with the leasehold
**Rule (write this):** A lease covenant binds or benefits assignees of the landlord or tenant if the original parties intended it to run, it touches and concerns the land, and the assignee is in privity of estate. Covenants to pay rent, to repair, to pay taxes, to use the premises only for a stated purpose and options to renew or purchase usually touch and concern the land; purely personal covenants do not.

**Elements:** 1. Intent (express words "assigns," or inferred). 2. Touch and concern: the covenant affects the use, value or enjoyment of the land itself. 3. Privity of estate between the party suing and the party sued.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A covenant to insure the premises usually runs when the proceeds are to be used to restore or benefit the premises; a covenant only to pay money to the landlord with no connection to the land may be held personal. A sublessee is not bound because it lacks privity of estate with the landlord.

**Authority:** Common law; *Spencer's Case* (1583) 5 Co. Rep. 16a.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule. Tested on a "covenant to insure" call (Feb 2015 Q2) [VERIFY V-071].

**Example:** T covenants to keep the building insured and to use proceeds to rebuild. T assigns to A. The covenant touches and concerns the land, so L may enforce it against A while A holds the estate.

### R-PRP-042 — Restrictions on assignment and subletting; consent
**Rule (write this):** Absent a lease restriction, a tenant may freely assign or sublet. Restrictions are strictly construed: a clause barring assignment does not bar subletting, and vice versa. Under the traditional rule, a landlord whose consent is required may withhold it for any reason, but the modern trend (and California for commercial leases: *Kendall*; Civ. Code § 1995.260) requires that consent not be unreasonably withheld unless the lease clearly states otherwise; whether *Kendall* extends to residential leases is unsettled.

**Elements:** 1. Is there a restriction, and does it cover this transfer? 2. Standard for consent (express standard; none stated → reasonableness in California). 3. Reasonable objections: the transferee's financial responsibility and the suitability of its use; unreasonable: a demand for higher rent or a personal dislike. 4. Waiver: knowingly accepting rent from the transferee waives the breach.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Rule in *Dumpor's Case*: once the landlord consents to one assignment, the restriction is waived for later ones unless the landlord reserves it; many states have rejected the rule. A California lease may absolutely prohibit transfer if it says so expressly, and may give an express standard for consent (Civ. Code §§ 1995.230, 1995.250) [S-762]. Where a lease restricts transfer by requiring consent but states no standard, consent may not be unreasonably withheld (Civ. Code § 1995.260, restrictions executed on or after September 23, 1983) [S-762]. **Scope:** *Kendall* decided a commercial lease, and Civ. Code §§ 1995.010–1995.340 apply only to leases of real property "for other than residential purposes" (§§ 1995.010, 1995.020(b)) [S-1600]. No statute or California Supreme Court decision settles the standard for a residential consent clause, so on an essay argue *Kendall*'s reasoning by analogy.

**Authority:** *Kendall v. Ernest Pestana, Inc.* (1985) 40 Cal.3d 488 [S-762]; Civ. Code §§ 1995.010–1995.340 [S-762]; *Dumpor's Case* (1603) 4 Co. Rep. 119b.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: the traditional rule is still tested, with the modern trend as the alternative; read the call. CA essay: cite *Kendall* and § 1995.260 for a commercially reasonable objection.

**Example:** A lease says "no assignment without Landlord's consent." L refuses consent to a creditworthy assignee in the same business unless T pays 20% more rent. In California, the refusal is unreasonable; T may assign and recover damages.

### R-PRP-043 — Transfers by the landlord
**Rule (write this):** A landlord may transfer the reversion without the tenant's consent. The transferee takes subject to the existing lease (the tenant's possession gives notice), is entitled to rent and to the benefit of the tenant's covenants that run with the land, and is bound by the landlord's covenants that touch and concern the land, such as a covenant to repair or a tenant's option to renew.

**Elements:** 1. Conveyance of the reversion. 2. Tenant's possession or recorded lease = notice. 3. Covenants that run bind and benefit the new landlord. 4. The tenant may pay the original landlord until notified of the transfer.

**Exceptions / defenses:** The original landlord remains liable on its own express promises by privity of contract. Purely personal covenants of the landlord do not bind the grantee.

**Authority:** Common law.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** L sells the building to B mid-term. B may collect T's rent and must honor T's option to renew contained in the lease.

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: compare 买卖不破租赁 ("sale does not break the lease") under the PRC Civil Code, which reaches a similar result (approx.).

### R-PRP-044 — Landlord's remedies: self-help and eviction
**Rule (write this):** When a tenant defaults or holds over, the landlord may terminate the lease (if the lease or statute allows) and recover possession through the summary eviction proceeding (unlawful detainer). Most states, including California, prohibit self-help eviction such as changing locks, removing doors or cutting off utilities, even against a defaulting tenant.

**Elements:** 1. Ground for termination (nonpayment, breach of a material covenant, holdover). 2. Required notice (California: three-day notice to pay rent or quit, or to perform a curable covenant). 3. Unlawful detainer action. 4. Damages for rent and holdover.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Common law allowed peaceable self-help in some states; never against a tenant still present who objects in a manner threatening a breach of the peace. California imposes statutory damages for utility shutoffs, lockouts and removal of the tenant's property (Civ. Code § 789.3) [VERIFY V-662]. Tenant defenses: habitability, retaliation, defective notice, waiver by acceptance of rent.

**Authority:** CCP §§ 1159–1179a (unlawful detainer), § 1161; Civ. Code § 789.3.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** T is two months behind. L changes the locks while T is at work. Even though T is in default, L is liable for wrongful eviction and statutory damages in California.

### R-PRP-045 — Landlord's tort liability
**Rule (write this):** At common law a landlord owed no duty to the tenant or the tenant's guests for conditions on the leased premises, subject to exceptions: (1) latent defects the landlord knew or should have known of and did not disclose, (2) common areas under the landlord's control, (3) repairs the landlord undertook and performed negligently, (4) premises leased for a public purpose with a dangerous condition at the start of the lease, and (5) short-term furnished dwellings. The modern trend, and California, applies ordinary negligence: a landlord must use reasonable care in light of what it knew or should have known and its ability to control the danger.

**Elements:** 1. Duty (exception or general negligence). 2. Knowledge of the condition (actual or constructive) and the right and ability to remedy it. 3. Breach, causation, damages. 4. Criminal acts of third parties: duty to take reasonable security measures when the crime is foreseeable, with a higher degree of foreseeability required for costly measures.

**Exceptions / defenses:** California landlords are not strictly liable for latent defects in rental units (*Peterson v. Superior Court* (1995) 10 Cal.4th 1185, overruling *Becker v. IRM Corp.* (1985) 38 Cal.3d 454). A landlord is liable for a tenant's dangerous dog only if it had actual knowledge and the right to remove the animal (*Uccello v. Laudenslayer* (1975) 44 Cal.App.3d 504).

**Authority:** *Rowland v. Christian* (1968) 69 Cal.2d 108; *Ann M. v. Pacific Plaza Shopping Center* (1993) 6 Cal.4th 666; *Uccello*; *Peterson*. Cross-reference the Torts subject files (premises liability; negligence duty).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: common-law exceptions unless the question indicates general negligence. CA essay: state the common-law exceptions and then the California general-negligence approach.

**Example:** L knows the stair railing in the common hallway is loose and does nothing; a tenant's guest falls. L is liable under the common-area exception and under ordinary negligence.

### R-PRP-046 — California Tenant Protection Act (just cause and rent cap)
**Rule (write this):** Under California's Tenant Protection Act, once a tenant has continuously and lawfully occupied covered residential property for 12 months, the owner may terminate the tenancy only for just cause stated in the written notice, either at-fault (e.g., nonpayment, material breach, nuisance, criminal activity) or no-fault (e.g., owner or relative move-in, withdrawal from the rental market, substantial remodel), and a no-fault termination requires relocation assistance. Annual rent increases on covered units are capped at the lesser of 5% plus the change in the cost of living or 10% over any 12 months.

**Elements:** 1. Covered unit (not exempt). 2. Tenancy of 12 months or more. 3. Just cause stated. 4. No-fault: relocation assistance or rent waiver, and SB 567's stricter conditions (owner move-in within 90 days and occupancy as a primary residence for at least 12 months; substantial remodel requires permits and work that cannot be done safely with the tenant in place, displacing for at least 30 days). 5. Rent-increase cap.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Exemptions include housing first occupied within the prior 15 years, many single-family homes and condominiums owned by natural persons where the required notice was given, owner-occupied duplexes and housing under stricter local rent control [VERIFY V-650]. Violations expose the owner to actual damages, attorney's fees and up to treble damages for willful violations [S-753]. The just-cause and rent-cap provisions are scheduled to be repealed on January 1, 2030; a 2025 bill to remove the sunset died in January 2026 [S-755].

**Authority:** Civ. Code §§ 1946.2, 1947.12 (AB 1482 (2019), amended by SB 567 (2023)) [S-752] [S-753] [S-754] [S-755].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Not tested on the MBE. CA essay: mention in one or two sentences only if a residential tenancy over 12 months is being ended or rent raised, and the call allows California law.

**Example:** L wants to end a two-year tenant's month-to-month tenancy to rent to a friend at a higher rent. That is not just cause under § 1946.2, so the termination is invalid if the unit is covered.

### R-PRP-047 — Other ways a lease ends: surrender, destruction, condemnation, illegality, merger
**Rule (write this):** A lease ends by surrender when the landlord accepts the tenant's offer to give up the premises, expressly (in writing if the remaining term exceeds one year) or by operation of law through conduct inconsistent with the continuing lease, such as reletting on the landlord's own account. Modern law lets the tenant terminate if the premises are destroyed without the tenant's fault; a total condemnation ends the lease and the tenant shares in the award for the value of the leasehold; a lease for an illegal purpose is void; and a lease ends by merger when the tenant acquires the reversion.

**Elements:** 1. Surrender: offer, acceptance (express or implied). 2. Destruction: common-law rule (tenant still owes rent) vs. modern rule and statutes. 3. Condemnation: total vs. partial (partial: rent continues, tenant gets share of award). 4. Illegality at signing vs. later; frustration of purpose for commercial leases.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Merely accepting keys or reletting with notice to the tenant that it is for the tenant's account is not a surrender. In California, the hiring terminates on the destruction of the thing hired or the hirer's acquisition of superior title (Civ. Code § 1933), and the tenant may terminate if the greater part of the premises perishes without the tenant's fault (Civ. Code § 1932(2)).

**Authority:** Common law; Civ. Code §§ 1932, 1933.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** T mails L the keys with a note: "I'm leaving; lease over." L moves its own storage into the space. L's use of the space for itself is acceptance; surrender by operation of law ends T's future rent liability.

## I.D Special problems

### R-PRP-048 — Fair housing
**Rule (write this):** The federal Fair Housing Act prohibits discrimination in the sale or rental of dwellings, and in their terms, advertising, financing and brokerage, because of race, color, religion, sex, disability, familial status or national origin; it also requires reasonable accommodations in rules and reasonable modifications for persons with disabilities. Liability may rest on intentional discrimination or on disparate impact. Discriminatory advertising is barred even for owners otherwise exempt.

**Elements:** 1. Dwelling transaction. 2. Protected class. 3. Discriminatory act (refusal, terms, steering, advertising, failure to accommodate). 4. Exemptions: single-family house sold or rented by an owner owning no more than three such houses without a broker or discriminatory advertising; owner-occupied dwelling of four or fewer units ("Mrs. Murphy"); religious organizations and private clubs; qualifying housing for older persons.

**Exceptions / defenses:** 42 U.S.C. § 1982 bars racial discrimination in all property transactions with no exemptions (*Jones v. Alfred H. Mayer Co.*, 392 U.S. 409 (1968)). Racially restrictive covenants are judicially unenforceable (*Shelley v. Kraemer*, 334 U.S. 1 (1948)). California's Fair Employment and Housing Act adds protected classes, including marital status, sexual orientation, gender identity, ancestry and source of income (which includes housing vouchers), and has a narrower owner-occupancy exemption (one roomer or boarder in an owner-occupied single-family home) [S-776].

**Authority:** 42 U.S.C. §§ 3603(b), 3604, 3607; *Texas Dep't of Housing & Community Affairs v. Inclusive Communities Project, Inc.*, 576 U.S. 519 (2015) (disparate impact); Gov. Code §§ 12927, 12955 [S-776]; Civ. Code § 51 (Unruh Act).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: FHA classes, exemptions and the advertising rule. CA essay: rarely tested; add FEHA's wider classes if relevant.

**Example:** An owner of a four-unit building who lives in one unit refuses to rent to a family with children. The Mrs. Murphy exemption covers the refusal under the FHA, but an ad saying "no children" violates § 3604(c), and FEHA's narrower exemption does not protect the owner in California.

### R-PRP-049 — Conflict of laws: the situs rule
**Rule (write this):** Questions about title to and interests in land, including the validity, formalities and effect of a conveyance, the estates created, recording priority, liens, adverse possession, and succession to land by will or intestacy, are governed by the law of the state where the land is located (the situs). Only a court of the situs can directly transfer title, but a court with personal jurisdiction over a party can order that party to convey land located elsewhere.

**Elements:** 1. Is the issue one of title or interest in land (situs law) or a contract or personal obligation about land (law chosen by the parties or the most significant relationship)? 2. Apply situs law, often including its choice-of-law rules. 3. Enforcement: situs court for in rem relief; any court with personal jurisdiction for an in personam order.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Marital property characterization of land may be affected by the domicile's law (in California, quasi-community property and out-of-state land at dissolution: R-CMP-003, R-CMP-083). A foreign decree purporting to transfer title directly need not be given effect by the situs (*Fall v. Eastin*, 215 U.S. 1 (1909)).

**Authority:** Restatement (Second) of Conflict of Laws §§ 223, 236, 239 [VERIFY V-659]; *Fall v. Eastin*, 215 U.S. 1 (1909).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: the situs rule. CA essay: arises mainly in Community Property and Wills crossovers.

**Example:** A domiciliary of Nevada dies with a will that is valid in Nevada but not executed in the form required where her Arizona ranch is located. Arizona law (the situs) decides whether the will passes the ranch.

## II.A Restrictive covenants

### R-PRP-050 — Real covenant: running of the burden
**Rule (write this):** A real covenant is a promise concerning the use of land that is enforceable at law, by damages, between successors of the original parties. The burden runs to a successor of the promisor if (1) the promise is in a writing satisfying the statute of frauds, (2) the original parties intended it to bind successors, (3) it touches and concerns the land, (4) there was horizontal privity between the original parties, (5) there is vertical privity between the promisor and the successor, and (6) the successor had notice.

**Elements:** 1. Writing (usually the deed). 2. Intent ("heirs and assigns"; or inferred). 3. Touch and concern (R-PRP-054). 4. Horizontal privity: the promise was made in a conveyance of an interest in land between the original parties (grantor–grantee, landlord–tenant); neighbors who simply sign an agreement lack it in most states. 5. Vertical privity (strict for the burden): the successor took the promisor's entire estate. 6. Notice: actual, inquiry or record.

**Exceptions / defenses:** An adverse possessor lacks vertical privity. If horizontal privity is missing, treat the promise as an equitable servitude (R-PRP-052) and seek an injunction. The Restatement (Third) of Property: Servitudes drops the horizontal privity requirement and merges covenants and servitudes. California's statute requires that the instrument describe both parcels, state that successors are bound, relate to the use, repair, maintenance or similar matters of the land, and be recorded (Civ. Code § 1468) [S-769].

**Authority:** Common law; Civ. Code §§ 1460–1471, especially § 1468 [S-769].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: the six elements, with horizontal privity. CA essay: analyze both real covenant (damages) and equitable servitude (injunction) whenever a promise about land use is enforced against a successor. Tested Feb 2021 Q5 and Feb 2026 Q1 [VERIFY V-071].

**Example:** O sells half her land to A; the deed has A promise "for herself, her heirs and assigns" to maintain a shared fence. A sells to B. O can recover damages from B for failure to maintain: writing, intent, touch and concern, horizontal privity (O–A deed), vertical privity (A–B) and record notice.

### R-PRP-051 — Real covenant: running of the benefit
**Rule (write this):** The benefit of a real covenant runs to a successor of the promisee if the promise is in writing, the original parties intended the benefit to run, the promise touches and concerns the benefited land, and there is vertical privity. Horizontal privity is not required for the benefit to run, and vertical privity is relaxed: the successor need only hold some possessory estate derived from the promisee.

**Elements:** 1. Writing. 2. Intent. 3. Touch and concern (benefit side). 4. Relaxed vertical privity.

**Exceptions / defenses:** When the benefit is in gross (not tied to any land of the promisee), older cases refuse to let the burden run; modern law generally allows it.

**Authority:** Common law.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** A promises neighbor N (by agreement, not by deed) not to build above one story. N sells to M. M may enforce the benefit even though A–N lacked horizontal privity; whether the burden runs to A's buyer is a separate question.

### R-PRP-052 — Equitable servitudes
**Rule (write this):** An equitable servitude is a promise concerning the use of land that equity enforces by injunction against a successor of the promisor who takes with notice. The burden runs if the promise is in writing (or implied from a common scheme), the parties intended it to bind successors, it touches and concerns the land, and the successor had actual, inquiry or record notice; no privity of estate is required.

**Elements:** 1. Writing or common scheme (R-PRP-053). 2. Intent. 3. Touch and concern. 4. Notice. 5. Remedy: injunction (damages possible).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Equitable defenses: unclean hands, laches, acquiescence, estoppel and changed conditions (R-PRP-056). A purchaser for value without notice takes free.

**Authority:** *Tulk v. Moxhay* (1848) 2 Ph. 774, 41 Eng. Rep. 1143; Civ. Code § 1468 [S-769]; Civ. Code § 5975 (common interest development declarations enforceable as equitable servitudes unless unreasonable) [S-775].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule; equitable servitude is the usual path when the plaintiff wants to stop a use.

**Example:** A buys a lot whose recorded deed says "residential purposes only" and opens a café. Neighbors who benefit from the restriction seek an injunction; A had record notice, so the servitude binds A.

### R-PRP-053 — Common scheme (implied reciprocal servitudes)
**Rule (write this):** When a developer subdivides land under a general plan of restrictions and sells lots with restrictions in most deeds, many courts imply a reciprocal negative servitude that binds every lot in the plan, including lots sold without the restriction in the deed, and lets any lot owner in the subdivision enforce it. The burdened owner must have had notice, which may come from the recorded restrictions in neighboring deeds or from the uniform appearance of the neighborhood.

**Elements:** 1. Common plan at the time sales began (plat, advertising, restrictions in most deeds). 2. The lot in question was part of the plan. 3. Notice (actual, record or inquiry). 4. Enforcement by earlier and later purchasers.

**Exceptions / defenses:** California does not imply a servitude from a common plan alone; the restriction must appear in a recorded instrument. A declaration of covenants, conditions and restrictions (CC&Rs) recorded before the lots are sold, which describes the property and states that it binds all purchasers and successors, is enforceable as an equitable servitude even if a later deed does not mention it.

**Authority:** *Sanborn v. McLean*, 233 Mich. 227, 206 N.W. 496 (1925); *Riley v. Bear Creek Planning Committee* (1976) 17 Cal.3d 500; *Citizens for Covenant Compliance v. Anderson* (1995) 12 Cal.4th 345.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: implied reciprocal servitude (Sanborn) is the majority rule. CA essay: state the majority rule, then "California requires a recorded writing (*Riley*), but a recorded declaration of CC&Rs suffices even if the deed is silent (*Citizens for Covenant Compliance*)." Tested Feb 2026 Q1 [VERIFY V-071].

**Example:** A developer records a plat and sells 14 of 15 lots by deeds restricting use "for residential purposes"; the 15th deed omits the restriction. Under Sanborn, the 15th lot is bound if its buyer had notice. In California, the 15th lot is bound only if a recorded instrument (such as recorded CC&Rs) imposed the restriction on it.

### R-PRP-054 — Touch and concern; affirmative covenants
**Rule (write this):** A covenant touches and concerns land if it affects the owners as landowners, by restricting the use of the burdened land or enhancing the use or value of the benefited land, rather than as members of the public. Negative covenants (use restrictions, building limits) almost always touch and concern; affirmative covenants to pay money, such as homeowners' association assessments, touch and concern when the money is used to maintain or improve the land or common areas.

**Elements:** 1. Burden side: restricts the promisor's use of its land or requires an act on it. 2. Benefit side: increases the value or enjoyment of the promisee's land. 3. Affirmative covenants: courts are more cautious but enforce assessments tied to the property.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Covenants not to compete are often held to touch and concern if they benefit nearby land. The Restatement (Third) of Property: Servitudes replaces touch and concern with a test of whether the servitude is arbitrary, unconstitutional, unconscionable or against public policy.

**Authority:** *Neponsit Property Owners' Ass'n v. Emigrant Industrial Savings Bank*, 278 N.Y. 248, 15 N.E.2d 793 (1938).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** Deeds in a subdivision require each owner to pay $1,200 a year for maintenance of private roads and parks. A successor owner is bound because the payments benefit the land (Neponsit).

### R-PRP-055 — Scope, interpretation and enforcement of servitudes
**Rule (write this):** A servitude is interpreted to carry out the intent of the parties shown by its words and the circumstances, with older cases resolving true ambiguity in favor of free use of land. The original promisee, successors to the benefited land and, under a common scheme, any lot owner in the plan may enforce it; the remedy is damages for a real covenant and an injunction (or damages) for an equitable servitude.

**Elements:** 1. Read the restriction's words ("residential purposes," "single-family dwelling," "no business"). 2. Apply to the use: does it change the residential character (traffic, signs, employees, customers)? 3. Identify who may sue. 4. Identify the remedy sought.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Minor incidental uses (a home office with no customers) usually do not violate "residential purposes." Defenses are covered in R-PRP-056.

**Authority:** Common law; Civ. Code § 1468 [S-769].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** A deed limits lots to "residential purposes." An owner runs a daycare for 12 children with constant drop-offs. Most courts would find a violation because it is a commercial use that changes the residential character.

### R-PRP-056 — Termination and defenses to covenants and servitudes
**Rule (write this):** A covenant or servitude ends, or becomes unenforceable, by its own terms; by a written release from the benefited owners; by merger of the benefited and burdened estates; by abandonment or acquiescence when violations are so widespread that the plan's purpose is defeated; by changed conditions so radical that the restriction can no longer achieve its purpose; by laches, unclean hands or estoppel; by prescription through open, continuous violation for the statutory period; or by condemnation.

**Elements:** 1. Changed conditions: the change must be within the restricted area or so pervasive that the benefit is gone; changes only outside the subdivision, or a mere drop in value, are not enough. 2. Acquiescence: a plaintiff who tolerated similar violations by others may be barred (as to that plaintiff), and widespread tolerated violations can show abandonment of the plan. 3. Laches: unreasonable delay plus prejudice. 4. Unclean hands: the plaintiff violates the same restriction.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A few minor violations do not show abandonment. Border lots facing a changed street may still be bound if interior lots still benefit.

**Authority:** Common law; Restatement (Third) of Property: Servitudes § 7.10 (changed conditions) [VERIFY V-653].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule. Tested Feb 2026 Q1 (changed conditions, acquiescence, prescription) [VERIFY V-071].

**Example:** Several owners in a 15-lot residential subdivision have run home businesses openly for over ten years without objection. A neighbor sues one of them. Defendant argues abandonment, acquiescence and laches; if the plaintiff herself tolerated similar uses, acquiescence likely bars her claim against comparable businesses.

### R-PRP-057 — Unenforceable covenants
**Rule (write this):** Courts will not enforce a covenant that is illegal, unconstitutional or against public policy, including racially restrictive covenants and covenants that violate fair housing laws or unreasonably restrain alienation. Statutes may also override private restrictions on particular uses.

**Elements:** 1. Identify the restriction. 2. Test against the Constitution (state action through judicial enforcement), the Fair Housing Act and state statutes. 3. Strike only the offending restriction.

**Exceptions / defenses:** California provides a recorded "Restrictive Covenant Modification" procedure to remove unlawful discriminatory language from recorded documents (Gov. Code § 12956.2) [S-771], and declares such discriminatory restrictions void (Civ. Code § 782) [VERIFY V-655]. California statutes also limit private restrictions on solar energy systems (Civ. Code § 714) [VERIFY V-655] and on keeping at least one pet in a common interest development (Civ. Code § 4715) [S-775].

**Authority:** *Shelley v. Kraemer*, 334 U.S. 1 (1948); 42 U.S.C. § 3604; Gov. Code § 12956.2 [S-771].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** A 1948 deed restriction bars sale to persons of a particular race. It is unenforceable; a current owner may record a restrictive covenant modification in California.

### R-PRP-058 — Common interest communities and homeowners' associations
**Rule (write this):** Use restrictions in the recorded declaration of a common interest development are presumed reasonable and are enforced unless the challenger proves they are arbitrary, impose burdens on the use of affected land that substantially outweigh their benefits, or violate a fundamental public policy, judged by reference to the development as a whole rather than one owner's circumstances. Rules adopted later by the board, and board decisions, must be reasonable and made in good faith under fair procedures, and courts defer to the board's discretion on ordinary maintenance and management decisions.

**Elements:** 1. Source of the restriction (recorded declaration vs. board rule). 2. Standard: presumption of validity (declaration) or reasonableness (rules). 3. Statutory overrides. 4. Assessments: covenant to pay, enforceable by lien. 5. Directors' fiduciary duties.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Statutes override certain restrictions (e.g., at least one pet, Civ. Code § 4715) [S-775].

**Authority:** *Nahrstedt v. Lakeside Village Condominium Assn.* (1994) 8 Cal.4th 361 [S-775]; *Lamden v. La Jolla Shores Clubdominium Homeowners Assn.* (1999) 21 Cal.4th 249; Civ. Code § 4000 et seq. (Davis-Stirling Common Interest Development Act), § 5975 [S-775].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: II.A.6 tests the association's power to enforce recorded restrictions and collect assessments. CA essay: cite *Nahrstedt* if a recorded CID restriction is challenged.

**Example:** A condominium's recorded declaration bars short-term rentals under 30 days. An owner challenges it as unreasonable for her. Under *Nahrstedt*, the restriction is enforced unless arbitrary or against public policy for the project as a whole.

### R-PRP-059 — Condominiums and cooperatives
**Rule (write this):** In a condominium, each owner holds a fee simple in an individual unit plus an undivided interest, as a tenant in common, in the common areas, and finances and pays taxes on the unit separately; an association governed by a recorded declaration and bylaws manages the common areas and levies assessments. In a cooperative, a corporation owns the building and holds the mortgage; residents own shares and hold long-term proprietary leases, and restrictions on transfer of shares (board approval) are generally upheld because members share financial risk.

**Elements:** 1. Identify the form. 2. Condominium: unit fee + common-area interest; declaration; assessments and liens. 3. Cooperative: shares + proprietary lease; blanket mortgage; board approval of transfers.

**Exceptions / defenses:** In a cooperative, one member's default on carrying charges can endanger all members, which justifies stricter transfer controls than would be valid on a fee.

**Authority:** Common law; Civ. Code § 783 (condominium defined); Civ. Code § 4000 et seq.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** A cooperative board rejects a buyer of shares for insufficient income. The rejection is generally valid as a reasonable restraint, unlike a restraint on a fee simple.

## II.B Easements, profits, and licenses

### R-PRP-060 — Easements, profits and licenses: classification
**Rule (write this):** An easement is a nonpossessory right to use another's land (affirmative) or to prevent the owner from doing something on it (negative). It is appurtenant when it benefits a dominant parcel and passes with it, and in gross when it benefits its holder personally (e.g., a utility line); courts prefer to construe an easement as appurtenant. A profit is a right to enter and remove resources such as timber, minerals or game; a license is revocable permission to enter, not an interest in land.

**Elements:** 1. Affirmative vs. negative (negative easements traditionally limited to light, air, support and stream flow, and created only expressly). 2. Appurtenant (dominant and servient tenements) vs. in gross. 3. Profit (includes an implied easement to access). 4. License: not subject to the statute of frauds; revocable at will; ends on the licensor's death or conveyance.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A license coupled with an interest (e.g., to enter and remove purchased chattels) or one made irrevocable by estoppel cannot be revoked (R-PRP-065).

**Authority:** Common law; Civ. Code §§ 801, 802, 803.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** A grants B "the right to cross my land to reach B's lake lot." The right benefits B's lot, so it is an easement appurtenant; it passes when B sells the lake lot.

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: an easement appurtenant is close to 地役权 (servitude) under the PRC Civil Code, which also attaches to a dominant parcel and passes with it (approx.).

### R-PRP-061 — Express easements (grant and reservation)
**Rule (write this):** An express easement is created by a writing that satisfies the statute of frauds, either by grant (the servient owner conveys an easement) or by reservation (the grantor conveys land and keeps an easement over it). At common law an easement could not be reserved in favor of a third party (a stranger to the deed), but the modern trend and California allow it.

**Elements:** 1. Signed writing describing the easement. 2. Grant or reservation. 3. Identify dominant and servient estates and scope. 4. Recording (to bind a later purchaser without notice).

**Exceptions / defenses:** A failed express easement (e.g., an oral grant) may be saved as an easement by estoppel (R-PRP-065) or by prior use or necessity.

**Authority:** Common law; Civ. Code § 1091; *Willard v. First Church of Christ, Scientist* (1972) 7 Cal.3d 473 (reservation in favor of a third party upheld).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: the traditional no-stranger rule is still tested; the modern view gives effect to the grantor's intent. CA essay: cite *Willard*. Tested Feb 2021 Q5 [VERIFY V-071].

**Example:** O sells Lot 1 to A "reserving to the Church an easement for parking." At common law the reservation is void; in California it is valid (*Willard*).

### R-PRP-062 — Easement implied from prior use (quasi-easement)
**Rule (write this):** An easement is implied from prior use when (1) one owner held both parcels, (2) before severance the owner used one part for the benefit of the other (a quasi-easement), (3) the use was apparent (discoverable on reasonable inspection) and continuous, and (4) the use is reasonably necessary to the enjoyment of the dominant part at the time of severance.

**Elements:** 1. Common ownership and severance. 2. Prior use. 3. Apparent and continuous (underground utilities count if visible on reasonable inspection, such as by hookups). 4. Reasonable necessity (many courts require strict necessity when the easement is implied in favor of the grantor, i.e., by reservation).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Courts are slower to imply a reservation (against the grantor's own deed) than a grant. In California, an implied easement may even exclude the servient owner from most practical uses of the easement area if there is clear evidence that the parties intended such an easement when the parcels were divided (*Romero v. Shih* (2024) 15 Cal.5th 680, distinguishing the prescriptive-easement cases) [S-1601].

**Authority:** Common law; Civ. Code § 1104 (transfer passes easements and creates easements to the extent the property was obviously and permanently used for the benefit of the part transferred); *Romero v. Shih* (2024) 15 Cal.5th 680 [S-1601].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule. Tested Jul 2024 Q2 [VERIFY V-071].

**Example:** O owns two adjoining houses; one has always drained through a visible pipe across the other lot. O sells the drained house to A. A has an implied easement for the drain.

### R-PRP-063 — Easement by necessity
**Rule (write this):** An easement by necessity arises when land held in common ownership is divided and one parcel is left without access (typically landlocked) or another strictly necessary use. The necessity must exist at the time of severance, and the easement ends when the necessity ends.

**Elements:** 1. Unity of title. 2. Severance. 3. Strict necessity at severance. 4. Location: chosen by the servient owner if reasonable.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Inconvenience or the cost of an alternative route is not necessity. Most courts find no necessity where the land is reachable by navigable water or by a public road, however inconvenient. The easement does not arise between parcels that were never commonly owned.

**Authority:** Common law.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule. Tested Jul 2024 Q2 [VERIFY V-071].

**Example:** O sells the back half of her farm to A; it has no road access except across O's front half. A has an easement by necessity across the front half. If a new public road later reaches A's land, the easement ends.

### R-PRP-064 — Prescriptive easements
**Rule (write this):** An easement by prescription is acquired by use of another's land that is open and notorious, continuous and uninterrupted, and adverse (without the owner's permission, under a claim of right) for the statutory period. Unlike adverse possession, exclusivity is not required. In California the period is five years, and payment of taxes is not required unless the easement was separately assessed.

**Elements:** 1. Open and notorious. 2. Continuous for the period (seasonal use consistent with the use's nature counts; tacking allowed among successors in privity). 3. Adverse/hostile: no permission. 4. Statutory period (California five years).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Permission defeats prescription; if use began with permission, adversity starts only on a clear repudiation. An owner may interrupt by blocking the use, or in California prevent prescription by posting statutory signs at entrances or every 200 feet ("Right to pass by permission, and subject to control, of owner") or by recording a notice of consent (Civ. Code §§ 813, 1008) [S-764]. No prescription runs against public entities (Civ. Code § 1007). California courts are reluctant to grant a prescriptive easement that excludes the owner from practical use of the land [VERIFY V-654]; *Romero v. Shih* (2024) 15 Cal.5th 680 distinguished the prescriptive-easement cases and allowed an exclusive *implied* easement on clear evidence of intent (R-PRP-062) [S-1601].

**Authority:** Common law; Civ. Code §§ 813, 1007, 1008, 1009 [S-764]; CCP § 321; *Gilardi v. Hallam* (1981) 30 Cal.3d 317 [S-757].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: use the statutory period given in the question. CA essay: five years; no tax payment. Tested Jul 2024 Q2 [VERIFY V-071].

**Example:** N drives across O's field to reach the highway daily for six years without asking. O never objects. N has a prescriptive easement in California; if O had posted § 1008 signs, N would not.

### R-PRP-065 — Easement by estoppel; irrevocable license
**Rule (write this):** A license that is revocable at will becomes irrevocable, operating as an easement by estoppel, when the licensee, with the licensor's knowledge, reasonably relies on it by making substantial expenditures or improvements, so that revocation would be inequitable. The interest lasts as long as reasonably necessary to protect the licensee's reliance (often until the investment is recouped, sometimes indefinitely).

**Elements:** 1. Permission (oral license or failed express easement). 2. Reasonable, foreseeable reliance by substantial expenditure. 3. Licensor knew or should have known of the reliance. 4. Revocation would be unjust.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Modest expenditures, or reliance the licensor could not foresee, do not suffice.

**Authority:** *Holbrook v. Taylor*, 532 S.W.2d 763 (Ky. 1976).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** O orally lets neighbor B use O's driveway to reach B's new garage; B spends $40,000 building the garage in reliance, with O watching. O later tries to bar B. B has an easement by estoppel.

### R-PRP-066 — Scope of easements
**Rule (write this):** The scope of an express easement is set by its terms, read in light of the parties' intent and the reasonable, foreseeable development of the dominant estate; the scope of an implied easement follows the prior use or the necessity; and a prescriptive easement is limited to the type and intensity of the use during the prescriptive period. The easement holder may not use the easement to benefit land other than the dominant estate, or change its use so as to unreasonably increase the burden on the servient estate.

**Elements:** 1. Source of the easement. 2. Change in degree (normal growth, more traffic: usually allowed) vs. change in kind (new type of use: not allowed unless within the grant). 3. Subdivision of the dominant estate: each part may use the easement if the total burden is not unreasonable. 4. Maintenance: the easement holder maintains the easement and may make reasonable repairs (Civ. Code § 845). 5. Remedy for misuse: injunction against the excess, not termination.

**Exceptions / defenses:** The servient owner may use its land in any way that does not unreasonably interfere with the easement. Traditionally neither party may relocate a fixed easement unilaterally; the Restatement (Third) of Property: Servitudes § 4.8(3) lets the servient owner relocate at its own expense if the easement's utility is not significantly lessened [VERIFY V-653].

**Authority:** Common law; Civ. Code §§ 806, 845; *Brown v. Voss*, 105 Wash. 2d 366, 715 P.2d 514 (1986) (no use to benefit non-dominant land).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule. Tested Feb 2021 Q5 (subdivision of a farm; utility lines along a right-of-way lane) [VERIFY V-071].

**Example:** A written "right-of-way for ingress and egress" serves a farm. The farmer subdivides into 40 lots and wants to run power lines along the lane. More traffic from normal development may be within scope, but utility lines are arguably a different kind of use outside "ingress and egress"; the servient owner can likely enjoin the lines.

### R-PRP-067 — Transfer of easements and profits
**Rule (write this):** The benefit of an easement appurtenant passes automatically with the dominant estate, even if the deed does not mention it, and the burden passes with the servient estate to anyone who takes with notice. An easement in gross for commercial purposes (utilities, pipelines) is transferable, while a personal (noncommercial) easement in gross traditionally is not; profits in gross are transferable.

**Elements:** 1. Appurtenant: runs with dominant land. 2. Burden: binds successors with notice (record, actual, inquiry from visible use). 3. In gross: commercial vs. personal. 4. Divided profits in gross must be exercised as "one stock" so the burden is not increased.

**Exceptions / defenses:** An unrecorded express easement does not bind a bona fide purchaser of the servient estate without notice, but visible use may give inquiry notice.

**Authority:** Common law; *Miller v. Lutheran Conference & Camp Ass'n*, 331 Pa. 241, 200 A. 646 (1938).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule. Tested Jul 2024 Q2 (inquiry notice from a visible right of way) [VERIFY V-071].

**Example:** A has an appurtenant driveway easement over B's lot. A sells to C without mentioning it, and B sells to D, who sees the paved drive. C may use the drive; D took with inquiry notice.

### R-PRP-068 — Termination of easements
**Rule (write this):** An easement ends by (1) its own terms, (2) merger of the dominant and servient estates in one owner, (3) written release, (4) abandonment, shown by nonuse plus conduct showing intent to abandon, (5) estoppel, when the servient owner reasonably relies on the holder's statement or conduct, (6) prescription, when the servient owner blocks the use adversely for the statutory period, (7) end of the necessity, for an easement by necessity, (8) condemnation of the servient estate, or (9) a bona fide purchaser of the servient estate without notice.

**Elements:** 1. Identify the method. 2. Abandonment: mere nonuse is not enough for an express easement (except in California for prescriptive easements: nonuse for five years extinguishes an easement acquired by enjoyment). 3. Merger: the easement does not revive if the parcels are later separated.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Destruction of a servient building ends an easement in it (e.g., a stairway easement) unless caused by the servient owner's wrongful act.

**Authority:** Common law; Civ. Code § 811 (merger; destruction; act incompatible with the servitude; nonuse for the prescriptive period for an easement acquired by enjoyment).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** H holds an express easement but stops using it for 15 years and builds a fence across its entrance on his own land. Nonuse plus the fence shows intent to abandon; the easement ends.

## II.C Fixtures

### R-PRP-069 — Fixtures
**Rule (write this):** A fixture is a chattel so annexed to land that it becomes part of the real property and passes with it. Whether a chattel is a fixture depends on the annexor's objective intent, shown by the degree of physical attachment, the adaptation of the item to the land's use, and the damage removal would cause. Between a landlord and tenant, a tenant may remove trade, domestic and ornamental fixtures it installed before the lease ends if removal does not substantially damage the premises.

**Elements:** 1. Annexation (actual or constructive). 2. Adaptation. 3. Intent (objective). 4. Relationship: vendor–purchaser and mortgagor–mortgagee (fixtures pass with the land); landlord–tenant (tenant may remove trade fixtures, repairing damage); life tenant–remainderman (stricter). 5. Secured creditors: a purchase-money security interest in goods perfected by a fixture filing before the goods become fixtures or within 20 days after has priority over earlier-recorded real estate interests, except a construction mortgage; the secured party who removes the fixture must reimburse for physical injury caused, but not for diminished value.

**Exceptions / defenses:** When someone affixes property to another's land without an agreement allowing removal, it belongs to the landowner (Civ. Code § 1013), but a good-faith improver who mistakenly believed it owned the land may obtain equitable relief (removal or payment) (Civ. Code § 1013.5; CCP §§ 871.1–871.7).

**Authority:** Common law; Civ. Code §§ 660, 1013, 1013.5, 1019; UCC §§ 9-334, 9-604(c)–(d) (Cal. Com. Code §§ 9334, 9604); CCP §§ 871.1–871.7.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule. On an essay, identify the parties' relationship first; the same chattel can be a fixture as between vendor and purchaser but a removable trade fixture as between landlord and tenant.

**Example:** A restaurant tenant bolts ovens and a walk-in freezer to the floor. They are trade fixtures; the tenant may remove them before the lease ends if it repairs the bolt holes.

## II.D Zoning

### R-PRP-070 — Zoning power and constitutional limits
**Rule (write this):** Zoning is an exercise of the police power and is valid if it is rationally related to a legitimate public purpose such as health, safety, morals or general welfare. Zoning is invalid if it is arbitrary or irrational, violates a fundamental right or a protected class (including through the Fair Housing Act), conflicts with the state's enabling law or comprehensive (general) plan, or goes so far that it is a taking.

**Elements:** 1. Authority (state enabling act; in California, the police power of cities and counties under Cal. Const. art. XI, § 7). 2. Rational basis (deferential). 3. Constitutional limits: family-definition limits on related persons (*Moore*, plurality opinion), irrational animus (*Cleburne*), free speech (adult uses, signs), religious uses (RLUIPA, 42 U.S.C. § 2000cc). 4. Consistency with the general plan (in California, Gov. Code § 65860).

**Exceptions / defenses:** A limit on the number of unrelated persons living together is valid (*Belle Terre*). Exclusionary zoning may violate state law (e.g., New Jersey's *Mount Laurel* doctrine).

**Authority:** *Village of Euclid v. Ambler Realty Co.*, 272 U.S. 365 (1926); *Village of Belle Terre v. Boraas*, 416 U.S. 1 (1974); *Moore v. City of East Cleveland*, 431 U.S. 494 (1977) (plurality); *City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Center*, 473 U.S. 432 (1985); *Southern Burlington County NAACP v. Township of Mount Laurel*, 67 N.J. 151, 336 A.2d 713 (1975).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule. Tested Feb 2014 Q4 [VERIFY V-071].

**Example:** A city ordinance bars group homes for persons with intellectual disabilities in a zone that allows boarding houses. Under *Cleburne*, the distinction is irrational and invalid as applied.

### R-PRP-071 — Nonconforming uses and vested rights
**Rule (write this):** A use that was lawful when a zoning restriction took effect may continue as a nonconforming use, but it may not be substantially expanded or changed, and it ends if abandoned or substantially destroyed. Many states, including California, allow the government to require a nonconforming use to end after a reasonable amortization period. A developer gains a vested right to build under the old rules only after obtaining a valid permit and performing substantial work and incurring substantial liabilities in good-faith reliance on it.

**Elements:** 1. Lawful use existing before the change. 2. Scope: no material expansion; repair allowed, rebuilding after substantial destruction often not. 3. Abandonment or discontinuance. 4. Amortization period reasonable in light of the owner's investment. 5. Vested rights test.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Some states treat amortization as a taking requiring compensation. In California, development agreements and vesting tentative maps can lock in rules earlier than the common-law vested-rights test (Gov. Code §§ 65864 et seq., 66498.1).

**Authority:** *City of Los Angeles v. Gage* (1954) 127 Cal.App.2d 442 (amortization upheld); *Avco Community Developers, Inc. v. South Coast Regional Com.* (1976) 17 Cal.3d 785 (vested rights).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule. Tested Feb 2014 Q4 (building permit request; prior-use protection) [VERIFY V-071].

**Example:** D has run a gas station for 20 years when the area is rezoned residential. D may continue but cannot add a car wash. A five-year amortization period is likely reasonable in California.

### R-PRP-072 — Variances, special-use permits and rezoning
**Rule (write this):** A variance is administrative relief from a zoning requirement, granted only if strict application would cause unnecessary hardship because of conditions unique to the property (size, shape, topography), the hardship is not self-created, and the variance will not harm the neighborhood or conflict with the plan. A special-use (conditional-use) permit authorizes a use the ordinance allows on stated conditions. Rezoning is a legislative act that must be consistent with the comprehensive plan; spot zoning that singles out a small parcel for treatment inconsistent with the plan, for private benefit, is invalid.

**Elements:** 1. Area variance (setback, height) vs. use variance (different use; disfavored). 2. Hardship unique to the land. 3. No self-created hardship: the hardship must come from the land, not from the owner's own act (for example, building in violation of the code or dividing a lot into a substandard parcel); a wish to use the land more intensively or profitably is not itself a hardship. 4. Neighborhood impact. 5. Rezoning: plan consistency; spot zoning; contract or conditional zoning (split).

**Exceptions / defenses:** California prohibits use variances: a variance may not authorize a use not otherwise allowed in the zone, and it requires special circumstances applicable to the property that deprive it of privileges enjoyed by nearby property in the same zone (Gov. Code § 65906). The agency must make findings that bridge the evidence and the decision (*Topanga Assn. for a Scenic Community v. County of Los Angeles* (1974) 11 Cal.3d 506).

**Authority:** Common law; Gov. Code § 65906; *Topanga*.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** An owner of an oddly shaped lot seeks a 5-foot reduction of a 25-foot setback so a modest house fits. An area variance is appropriate; a request to build a store in a residential zone would be a use variance, barred in California.

### R-PRP-073 — Takings and land-use exactions (cross-reference)
**Rule (write this):** The Fifth Amendment, applied to the states through the Fourteenth, bars taking private property for public use without just compensation. A permanent physical occupation, or a government-authorized right of access that appropriates the right to exclude, is a per se taking; a regulation that deprives land of all economically beneficial use is a taking unless background principles of property or nuisance law already prohibited the use; other regulations are judged by the Penn Central factors; and a permit condition demanding property or money must have an essential nexus and rough proportionality to the project's impacts.

**Elements:** See R-CON-077 to R-CON-081 for the full analysis. 1. Public use (broad: *Kelo v. City of New London*, 545 U.S. 469 (2005)). 2. Type of taking. 3. Just compensation (fair market value). 4. Exactions: *Nollan*, *Dolan*, *Koontz*, and *Sheetz v. County of El Dorado* (2024) (legislatively imposed conditions also subject to the test) [VERIFY V-035].

**Exceptions / defenses:** A government that keeps surplus equity after selling property for unpaid taxes takes property (*Tyler v. Hennepin County* (2023)) [VERIFY V-035]. California's Constitution also requires compensation when property is "taken or damaged" for public use (Cal. Const. art. I, § 19).

**Authority:** U.S. Const. amends. V, XIV; *Cedar Point Nursery v. Hassid*, 594 U.S. 139 (2021); *Lucas v. South Carolina Coastal Council*, 505 U.S. 1003 (1992); *Penn Central Transp. Co. v. New York City*, 438 U.S. 104 (1978); R-CON-077 to R-CON-081.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Takings appear on both the Constitutional Law and the Real Property MBE. On a California essay, takings has been tested with property (Jul 2015 Q6; Feb 2018 Q3) [VERIFY V-071].

**Example:** A county conditions a home-building permit on a $25,000 traffic fee set by a general ordinance without an individualized finding. After *Sheetz*, the owner may challenge the fee under *Nollan/Dolan*.

## Rights incident to ownership (support, water, light and air)

### R-PRP-074 — Lateral and subjacent support
**Rule (write this):** A landowner has a right to lateral support from adjoining land and to subjacent support from underneath. An adjoining owner who excavates is strictly liable if the excavation causes land in its natural state to subside; if the land is improved with buildings, the excavator is liable for damage to the buildings only if negligent, unless the land would have subsided even without the buildings' weight, in which case many courts impose strict liability for all the damage.

**Elements:** 1. Lateral (adjoining) or subjacent (below; e.g., mining). 2. Withdrawal of support by defendant. 3. Subsidence. 4. Strict liability (natural state) vs. negligence (improvements). 5. Subjacent support: strict liability for land and for structures existing when the subsurface estate was severed.

**Exceptions / defenses:** California requires an excavating owner to give reasonable notice to the adjoining owner and to use ordinary care, with added duties for excavations deeper than nine feet next to a neighbor's building (Civ. Code § 832).

**Authority:** Common law; Civ. Code § 832.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** D digs a deep foundation pit next to P's vacant lot; P's soil slides into the pit. D is strictly liable even if careful.

### R-PRP-075 — Water rights
**Rule (write this):** For watercourses, eastern states follow the riparian doctrine (owners of land bordering the water share a right to reasonable use), while most western states follow prior appropriation (the first to divert water and put it to beneficial use has priority). California uses a dual system recognizing both riparian and appropriative rights, all subject to the constitutional requirement that water use be reasonable. For diffuse surface water, states use the common-enemy, natural-flow or reasonable-use rule; California follows a reasonableness rule.

**Elements:** 1. Type of water (watercourse, groundwater, diffuse surface water). 2. Applicable doctrine. 3. Reasonable use (California: all rights limited by Cal. Const. art. X, § 2). 4. Groundwater: absolute ownership (old), reasonable use, correlative rights (California: overlying owners share), or appropriation.

**Exceptions / defenses:** The public trust doctrine limits even vested appropriative rights in navigable waters (*National Audubon Society v. Superior Court* (1983) 33 Cal.3d 419).

**Authority:** Common law; Cal. Const. art. X, § 2; *Keys v. Romley* (1966) 64 Cal.2d 396 (surface water: reasonableness); *National Audubon Society*.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** Upstream owner U diverts most of a creek to fill an ornamental pond, leaving downstream farmer F's crops dry. Under the riparian reasonable-use rule, and in California, U's use is unreasonable.

### R-PRP-076 — Light, air, view; spite fences; airspace; nuisance and trespass
**Rule (write this):** A landowner has no natural right to light, air or view across a neighbor's land, and in most states such a right can be acquired only by an express easement or covenant, not by prescription or implication. Interference may be actionable as a private nuisance only if it is substantial and unreasonable, and California treats a fence or structure over ten feet high maliciously erected to annoy a neighbor as a private nuisance. An owner owns the airspace above its land to the height it can reasonably use; flights in navigable airspace are not trespass, but low, frequent overflights that destroy the use of the land can be a taking.

**Elements:** 1. Light and air: express easement needed. 2. Nuisance: substantial and unreasonable interference with use and enjoyment (cross-reference Torts). 3. Spite fence: height, malice, annoyance. 4. Airspace and overflight. 5. Encroachments: trespass; courts may deny an injunction to remove a minor good-faith encroachment and award damages instead (relative hardship).

**Exceptions / defenses:** A minority of courts apply nuisance law to blocking a solar collector (*Prah v. Maretti*, 108 Wis. 2d 223, 321 N.W.2d 182 (1982)). California's Solar Shade Control Act limits shading of certain solar collectors by later-planted trees [VERIFY V-670].

**Authority:** *Fontainebleau Hotel Corp. v. Forty-Five Twenty-Five, Inc.*, 114 So. 2d 357 (Fla. Dist. Ct. App. 1959); Civ. Code §§ 841.4, 3479; *United States v. Causby*, 328 U.S. 256 (1946).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule. Nuisance with property tested Feb 2018 Q3 [VERIFY V-071].

**Example:** A hotel builds a 14-story addition that shades a neighboring hotel's pool all afternoon. Absent an express easement, the neighbor has no claim (*Fontainebleau*).

## III Real estate contracts

### R-PRP-077 — Real estate brokerage
**Rule (write this):** A real estate broker is the agent of the party who engages it, usually the seller under a listing agreement, and owes that principal fiduciary duties of loyalty, care and full disclosure. Traditionally the broker earns its commission when it produces a buyer ready, willing and able to buy on the seller's terms, even if the sale never closes; a growing minority makes the commission depend on closing unless the seller wrongfully prevents it. Under the prevailing modern rule, a seller's broker who knows of a material latent defect that the buyer cannot discover by reasonable inspection must disclose it to the buyer.

**Elements:** 1. Listing type: open, exclusive agency, exclusive right to sell. 2. When the commission is earned. 3. Fiduciary duties to the principal. 4. Duties to the other party (no misrepresentation; disclosure of known material latent defects). 5. License and writing requirements.

**Exceptions / defenses:** In California an unlicensed broker cannot sue for a commission (Bus. & Prof. Code §§ 10130, 10136), and an agreement to pay a broker's commission for a sale of real property must be in a signed writing (Civ. Code § 1624(a)(4)). For residential property of one to four units, the seller's broker must conduct a reasonably competent and diligent visual inspection of the accessible areas and disclose what it would reveal (Civ. Code § 2079, codifying *Easton v. Strassburger* (1984) 152 Cal.App.3d 90) [S-768]. Dual agency requires the informed consent of both parties [VERIFY V-669].

**Authority:** Common law of agency; *Ellsworth Dobbs, Inc. v. Johnson*, 50 N.J. 528, 236 A.2d 843 (1967) (commission conditioned on closing); Bus. & Prof. Code §§ 10130, 10136; Civ. Code §§ 1624(a)(4), 2079 [S-768].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: the ready-willing-able rule and fiduciary duties. CA essay: rarely tested alone; mention *Easton* and § 2079 when a broker failed to find a visible defect.

**Example:** S's listing broker shows buyer B a house with obvious cracks in the garage slab and says nothing; the house has a failing foundation. In California, the seller's broker is liable for failing to discover and disclose what a visual inspection would have revealed.

### R-PRP-078 — Statute of frauds and part performance
**Rule (write this):** A contract for the sale of land, or of any interest in land, is unenforceable unless evidenced by a writing signed by the party to be charged that identifies the parties, describes the land and states the price (and other essential terms). Under the part-performance doctrine, equity will specifically enforce an oral land contract when the buyer's acts are unequivocally referable to the contract, usually shown by at least two of: possession, payment of all or part of the price, and substantial improvements.

**Elements:** 1. Land contract within the statute. 2. Writing (may be several documents; electronic records qualify) signed by the defendant. 3. Essential terms. 4. Exceptions: part performance; promissory estoppel (some courts); full performance by the seller (conveyance).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Payment alone is generally insufficient for part performance. An agent's authority to sign a land contract must itself be in writing (equal dignities rule, Civ. Code § 2309). California applies part performance where the buyer's change of position would make it unjust (unjust enrichment or irreparable injury) to deny enforcement.

**Authority:** Common law; Civ. Code §§ 1091, 1624(a)(3), 2309. Cross-reference R-CTR-042, R-CTR-043, R-CTR-046.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** S orally agrees to sell a cabin to B; B pays $10,000, moves in and adds a $30,000 deck with S's knowledge. B's possession, payment and improvements satisfy part performance; B may get specific performance.

### R-PRP-079 — Essential terms; time for performance; tender
**Rule (write this):** A land contract must contain, or allow the court to supply, the essential terms: the parties, a description of the land and the price. Time is not of the essence unless the contract says so, the circumstances show it, or one party makes it so by notice giving a reasonable time to perform; when time is not of the essence, a party who performs within a reasonable time after the closing date can still enforce the contract but is liable for damages caused by the delay. Because the parties' performances are concurrent conditions, a party must tender its own performance to put the other in breach, unless the other has repudiated or tender would be futile.

**Elements:** 1. Essential terms. 2. Time-of-the-essence analysis. 3. Tender at closing (deed vs. price). 4. Anticipatory repudiation excuses tender.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A court will not supply a missing price. Implied terms include the seller's duty to convey marketable title (R-PRP-080).

**Authority:** Common law.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** The contract sets closing for June 1 without saying time is of the essence. B's lender is a week late, and B closes on June 8. S cannot refuse to close but may recover any damages from the delay.

### R-PRP-080 — Marketable title
**Rule (write this):** Every contract for the sale of land contains an implied covenant that the seller will convey marketable title at closing, meaning title reasonably free from doubt that a reasonable, prudent buyer would accept without fear of litigation. Title is unmarketable if there are defects in the chain of title (including title resting on adverse possession not yet established by a quiet-title judgment, in most states), encumbrances such as mortgages, easements, covenants and significant encroachments, or an existing violation of a zoning ordinance; zoning restrictions themselves do not make title unmarketable.

**Elements:** 1. Implied (or express) title covenant. 2. Defect. 3. Time: title must be marketable at closing, so the seller may cure before then, for example by paying off a mortgage with the sale proceeds at closing; under an installment contract, at the final payment. 4. Buyer's notice of the defect and opportunity to cure. 5. Remedies: rescind and recover the deposit, damages, or specific performance with an abatement in price.

**Exceptions / defenses:** The buyer may waive a defect or agree to take subject to listed encumbrances. Some courts treat visible, beneficial easements that the buyer knew of (such as public utility lines serving the property) as not rendering title unmarketable.

**Authority:** Common law; *Lohmeyer v. Bower*, 170 Kan. 442, 227 P.2d 102 (1951) (existing zoning violation makes title unmarketable). Cross-reference R-REM-073 (abatement).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** Two weeks before closing, B learns the house sits five feet into the required setback in violation of the zoning code. Title is unmarketable; B may rescind and recover the deposit unless S cures before closing.

### R-PRP-081 — Remedies for breach of a land-sale contract
**Rule (write this):** A nonbreaching buyer may seek specific performance, because land is unique, or damages measured by the market value at the time of breach minus the contract price, plus incidental and consequential damages and the return of any deposit. A nonbreaching seller may also seek specific performance (mutuality) or damages measured by the contract price minus the market value at breach, and may keep a deposit only as valid liquidated damages or to the extent of its actual damages.

**Elements:** 1. Breach. 2. Specific performance (R-REM-071). 3. Damages (R-REM-015, R-REM-016). 4. Liquidated damages: a deposit that is a reasonable forecast (often 10% or less) is enforceable. 5. Restitution of the buyer's payments in excess of the seller's damages.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Minority "English rule": a seller who in good faith cannot convey because of a title defect owes only the deposit and expenses. California presumes that money cannot adequately remedy a breach of an agreement to transfer real property, conclusively so for a single-family dwelling the buyer intends to occupy (Civ. Code § 3387). For a residential purchase of one to four units the buyer intends to occupy, a liquidated-damages deposit of up to 3% of the price in a separately signed or initialed clause is presumed valid (Civ. Code §§ 1675, 1677). Even a willfully defaulting buyer may recover payments that exceed the seller's damages (*Freedman v. Rector* (1951) 37 Cal.2d 16).

**Authority:** Common law; Civ. Code §§ 1675, 1677, 3306, 3307, 3387; cross-reference R-REM-015, R-REM-016, R-REM-071.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule; the Remedies files carry the detail. On a Real Property essay, a one-paragraph remedies section is usually enough unless the call asks for remedies.

**Example:** B backs out of a $900,000 home purchase; the contract's separately initialed clause makes B's $27,000 deposit liquidated damages. Because 3% of the price is $27,000, the clause is presumptively valid in California, and S keeps the deposit.

### R-PRP-082 — Equitable conversion and risk of loss
**Rule (write this):** Under equitable conversion, once a specifically enforceable land contract is signed, the buyer becomes the equitable owner of the land and the seller holds legal title in trust as security for the price. Under the majority common-law rule, the risk of casualty loss not caused by the seller passes to the buyer at signing, though the buyer receives credit for any insurance proceeds the seller collects. Under the Uniform Vendor and Purchaser Risk Act, adopted in California, the risk stays with the seller until legal title or possession passes to the buyer.

**Elements:** 1. Enforceable contract. 2. Loss between signing and closing. 3. Who bears risk: majority (buyer), UVPRA (seller until title or possession), contract allocation controls. 4. Insurance: credit to buyer to avoid double recovery. 5. Death of a party: the buyer's interest passes as real property and the seller's as personal property (the right to the price).

**Exceptions / defenses:** The parties may allocate risk by contract. Under the UVPRA, if a material part of the property is destroyed without fault of the buyer before title or possession passes, the seller cannot enforce the contract and the buyer may recover any payments made.

**Authority:** Common law; *Skelly Oil Co. v. Ashmore*, 365 S.W.2d 582 (Mo. 1963) (insurance credit); Civ. Code § 1662 (UVPRA).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: the majority rule (risk on buyer) unless a statute is given. CA essay: give the majority rule, then "California adopted the UVPRA (Civ. Code § 1662), so the risk remains on the seller until title or possession passes."

**Example:** S and B sign a contract on May 1; the house burns down on May 20 without fault of either; B has not taken possession. Majority: B must pay the price (with credit for S's insurance). California: S bears the loss, and B may recover the deposit.

### R-PRP-083 — Options and rights of first refusal
**Rule (write this):** An option is a contract by which the owner (optionor) gives the holder the right to buy the land on stated terms within a stated time; it must be supported by consideration, satisfy the statute of frauds, and be exercised strictly in accordance with its terms and within its time. A right of first refusal (preemptive right) gives the holder the right to buy on the same terms as a bona fide third-party offer that the owner is willing to accept; the owner must give notice and a reasonable time, and a buyer who takes with notice of the right takes subject to it.

**Elements:** 1. Option: consideration; writing; exercise (unequivocal acceptance meeting the terms and deadline). 2. ROFR: triggering event (owner's decision to sell); notice; matching terms. 3. RAP: at common law an option in gross and (majority) a ROFR must vest within the perpetuities period; an option in a lease to buy the leased premises is exempt. 4. Restraint on alienation: a ROFR at a fixed price or of unlimited duration may be unreasonable.

**Exceptions / defenses:** California's USRAP excludes most nondonative (commercial) options and rights of first refusal (Prob. Code § 21225) [S-766]. An option in a lease runs with the leasehold to an assignee (R-PRP-041).

**Authority:** Common law; Prob. Code § 21225 [S-766].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** T's lease gives T a right of first refusal. L contracts to sell to X for $1 million without telling T; X knows of T's lease. T may compel a conveyance to T on the same terms.

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: compare a lessee's statutory priority purchase right (承租人优先购买权) in the PRC Civil Code, which works like a statutory right of first refusal (approx.).

### R-PRP-084 — Fitness and suitability: seller's disclosure duties
**Rule (write this):** A seller of used real property gives no implied warranty of quality (caveat emptor), but the seller is liable for fraud (affirmative misrepresentation), active concealment, and, under the modern majority rule, failure to disclose known material latent defects that the buyer could not discover by reasonable inspection. A general "as is" clause does not protect a seller from liability for fraud or for concealing known latent defects.

**Elements:** 1. Defect is material (affects value or desirability). 2. Seller knew. 3. Not reasonably discoverable by the buyer. 4. Nondisclosure, misrepresentation or concealment. 5. Buyer's reliance and damages; remedy: rescission or damages.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Specific "as is" clauses that name the defect, and patent defects, bar claims. California requires a seller of residential property of one to four units to give a written Transfer Disclosure Statement (Civ. Code § 1102 et seq.), and recognizes a duty to disclose known facts materially affecting value that the buyer cannot readily observe (*Lingsch v. Savage* (1963) 213 Cal.App.2d 729 [VERIFY V-668]; *Reed v. King* (1983) 145 Cal.App.3d 261 (multiple murders in the house can be material)); a seller need not disclose a death on the property that occurred more than three years before the offer (Civ. Code § 1710.2) [VERIFY V-656].

**Authority:** *Johnson v. Davis*, 480 So. 2d 625 (Fla. 1985); *Stambovsky v. Ackley*, 169 A.D.2d 254, 572 N.Y.S.2d 672 (1991); Civ. Code §§ 1102 et seq., 1710.2; *Reed v. King*.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** S knows the roof leaks every winter and repaints the stained ceiling before showing the house in summer. Active concealment makes S liable despite an "as is" clause.

### R-PRP-085 — Implied warranty for new homes; California's Right to Repair Act
**Rule (write this):** A builder-vendor of a new home impliedly warrants that it is built in a workmanlike manner and is fit for habitation. The warranty runs to the first purchaser and, in many states, to later purchasers for latent defects that appear within a reasonable time, and it is difficult to disclaim. In California, claims by homeowners for construction defects in residential units sold on or after January 1, 2003 are governed by the Right to Repair Act (SB 800), which sets building standards, a mandatory prelitigation notice-and-repair procedure, and limitations periods.

**Elements:** 1. Builder-vendor (not a casual seller). 2. New residence. 3. Latent defect. 4. Within a reasonable time. 5. California: SB 800 standards and procedure.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Disclaimers must be clear and specific. The economic-loss rule may bar negligence claims by remote purchasers in some states. In California, the Act is the exclusive remedy for covered construction-defect claims (*McMillin Albany LLC v. Superior Court* (2018) 4 Cal.5th 241) [VERIFY V-667], and a 10-year repose period applies to latent construction defects (CCP § 337.15).

**Authority:** Common law; Civ. Code §§ 895–945.5 (Right to Repair Act); CCP § 337.15.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: the builder's implied warranty. CA essay: mention SB 800 briefly if a new home's defects are at issue.

**Example:** Two years after B buys a new tract home from the developer, the foundation cracks because of poor soil compaction. B has a claim against the builder; in California it proceeds under SB 800, starting with notice to the builder.

### R-PRP-086 — Merger of the contract into the deed
**Rule (write this):** Once the buyer accepts the deed at closing, the land-sale contract merges into the deed, so the buyer can sue only on the deed's covenants of title, not on the contract's promises about title. Promises collateral to the conveyance (such as a promise to make repairs, to finish construction or about the land's condition), claims for fraud or mistake, and provisions the parties expressly agreed would survive closing are not merged.

**Elements:** 1. Closing and acceptance of the deed. 2. Is the promise about title (merged) or collateral (survives)? 3. Fraud or mistake exception. 4. Survival clause.

**Exceptions / defenses:** If the contract called for a warranty deed and the buyer accepted a quitclaim, the buyer has no title claim after closing.

**Authority:** Common law.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** The contract promised "marketable title free of encumbrances," but B accepts a quitclaim deed and later finds an easement. B cannot sue on the contract; the quitclaim gives no covenants. S's contract promise to repave the driveway survives closing.

## IV Mortgages/security devices

### R-PRP-087 — Mortgages and deeds of trust: nature, obligation and theories
**Rule (write this):** A mortgage is a transfer of an interest in land to secure performance of an obligation, usually a promissory note; without a secured obligation there is no mortgage, and the mortgage follows the obligation. A deed of trust serves the same function: the borrower conveys title to a trustee for the lender's benefit, with a power of sale on default. Under the majority lien theory, adopted in California, the mortgagee holds only a lien, and the mortgagor keeps title and possession until foreclosure; in title-theory states the mortgagee holds legal title and may take possession; intermediate-theory states pass title on default.

**Elements:** 1. Obligation (may be another person's debt or future advances). 2. Security instrument (mortgage or deed of trust). 3. Theory: lien, title or intermediate. 4. Consequences: possession before foreclosure, effect on joint tenancy (R-PRP-022).

**Exceptions / defenses:** The parties may agree to a mortgagee in possession. Deeds of trust are the dominant form in California.

**Authority:** Common law; Civ. Code §§ 2920, 2924, 2927 (mortgage does not entitle mortgagee to possession).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: lien theory is the majority; questions state the theory when it matters. CA essay: California is a lien-theory, deed-of-trust state.

**Example:** B borrows $400,000 from Bank and signs a note secured by a deed of trust naming T Title Co. as trustee. B keeps title and possession; on default, the trustee may sell under the power of sale.

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: a US lien-theory mortgage is close to 抵押权 under the PRC Civil Code: the mortgagor keeps possession and the creditor has priority in the proceeds; but PRC real-property mortgages require registration to be created, while US recording only protects priority (approx.).

### R-PRP-088 — Equitable mortgages; absolute deed given as security
**Rule (write this):** A deed that is absolute on its face but was given to secure a debt is treated as a mortgage (an equitable mortgage). The borrower may prove the security purpose by parol evidence, usually by clear and convincing evidence, and the lender must then foreclose rather than treat the property as its own, while the borrower may redeem by paying the debt.

**Elements:** Factors: 1. A debt or loan negotiations before the deed. 2. Price far below the property's value. 3. Grantor stays in possession. 4. Grantor's right or promise to repurchase. 5. Financial distress and the parties' relationship.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A genuine sale with a repurchase option at fair value is not a mortgage. A bona fide purchaser from the grantee who had no notice may take free of the grantor's equity.

**Authority:** Common law; Civ. Code § 2924 (every transfer of an interest in property made only as security for the performance of another act is deemed a mortgage), § 2925 (defeasance may be proved). Cross-reference R-TRS-037 for purchase-money resulting trusts.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** Facing foreclosure, O deeds her $500,000 house to L for $150,000, stays in possession and has an oral right to buy it back for $165,000. The deed is an equitable mortgage; L must foreclose, and O may redeem.

### R-PRP-089 — Installment land contracts
**Rule (write this):** Under an installment land contract, the seller finances the sale and keeps legal title until the buyer pays the full price in installments; on the buyer's default the contract typically provides for forfeiture, letting the seller retake the land and keep all payments. Because forfeiture can be harsh, modern courts limit it by granting a grace period or a right to cure, requiring restitution of payments that exceed the seller's damages, finding waiver where the seller accepted late payments without notice, or treating the contract as a mortgage that must be foreclosed.

**Elements:** 1. Contract with title retained as security. 2. Buyer default. 3. Forfeiture clause. 4. Limits: equity of redemption-type relief; restitution; waiver; treatment as a mortgage. 5. Seller's title obligation arises at the final payment (R-PRP-080).

**Exceptions / defenses:** In California a buyer who has paid a substantial part of the price or made substantial improvements, and whose default consists only of failing to pay, has a right to redeem by paying the full balance, even if the default was willful (*Petersen v. Hartell* (1985) 40 Cal.3d 102) [S-773], and a willfully defaulting buyer may recover payments exceeding the seller's damages (*MacFadden v. Walker* (1971) 5 Cal.3d 809) [S-773].

**Authority:** Common law; Civ. Code §§ 2985, 3275; *Petersen*; *MacFadden* [S-773].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** B has paid $120,000 of a $150,000 installment price and misses three payments. S declares a forfeiture. Most modern courts give B a chance to cure or to recover the excess of payments over S's damages; in California B may redeem by paying the balance.

### R-PRP-090 — Purchase-money mortgages
**Rule (write this):** A purchase-money mortgage secures money used to buy the land, whether given to the seller (seller financing) or to a third-party lender whose loan funds the price. A purchase-money mortgage executed as part of the purchase has priority over earlier liens against the buyer, such as judgment liens, that attach when the buyer acquires title, subject to the recording acts. Between a seller's and a third-party lender's purchase-money mortgages, the seller's usually has priority unless the seller agrees otherwise.

**Elements:** 1. Loan proceeds used to buy the land. 2. Executed at or as part of the acquisition. 3. Priority over prior liens against the purchaser. 4. Competing purchase-money mortgages: seller first; between two third-party lenders, the recording act decides.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A later bona fide mortgagee who records first may defeat an unrecorded purchase-money mortgage under the recording act. Seller-over-lender priority is the Restatement (Third) of Property: Mortgages § 7.2(c) position; several courts follow it (e.g., Oklahoma), but not all (e.g., Idaho has rejected it) [S-781] [S-1609]. In California, purchase-money loans also receive anti-deficiency protection (CCP § 580b; R-PRP-104).

**Authority:** Common law; Restatement (Third) of Property: Mortgages § 7.2 [S-781] [S-1609]; Civ. Code § 2898 [S-770]; CCP § 580b [S-777].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** C holds a recorded judgment against B. B buys Blackacre with a loan from Bank secured by a mortgage recorded the same day. C's judgment lien attaches when B gets title, but Bank's purchase-money mortgage has priority over it.

### R-PRP-091 — Future-advance mortgages
**Rule (write this):** A mortgage may secure advances the lender makes after the mortgage is recorded, such as under a construction loan or line of credit. Obligatory advances (ones the lender was contractually bound to make) take priority as of the mortgage's recording, ahead of intervening liens; optional advances made after the lender has actual notice of an intervening lien are subordinate to it under the majority rule, although many states now give all advances up to a stated maximum the original priority by statute.

**Elements:** 1. Mortgage securing future advances (and stating a maximum, in some statutes). 2. Intervening lien. 3. Obligatory vs. optional advance. 4. Notice (actual vs. record).

**Exceptions / defenses:** "Dragnet" clauses that sweep in unrelated debts are construed narrowly. The Restatement (Third) of Property: Mortgages (§ 2.3) departs from the traditional rule: it gives future advances, optional as well as obligatory, the mortgage's original priority, subject to limits such as a mortgagor's notice cutting off further advances [VERIFY V-995]. Not all courts follow it; the Washington Supreme Court (2022) declined to and kept the obligatory/optional distinction [S-1605]. On the MBE, apply the traditional rule unless the question gives a statute or the Restatement.

**Authority:** Common law; Civ. Code § 2884 (lien may secure obligations not yet in existence); Restatement (Third) of Property: Mortgages § 2.3 [VERIFY V-995]; *In re General Receivership of EM Property Holdings, LLC* (Wash. 2022) [S-1605].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule (traditional obligatory/optional distinction; mention the Restatement view as the alternative).

**Example:** Bank records a $1 million construction mortgage and agrees to advance funds as work progresses. A judgment creditor of the owner records a lien midway. Bank's later obligatory advances keep first priority.

### R-PRP-092 — Rights and duties before foreclosure
**Rule (write this):** In a lien-theory state, the mortgagor keeps possession, rents and profits until foreclosure, and the mortgagee may not take possession without consent, though on default it may enforce an assignment of rents or seek appointment of a receiver. A mortgagee who does take possession must manage the property prudently, account for rents and apply them to the debt. The mortgagor must not commit waste or otherwise impair the security, and the mortgagee may obtain an injunction or damages for impairment.

**Elements:** 1. Mortgage theory. 2. Possession and rents (assignment-of-rents clause; receiver). 3. Mortgagee in possession: duties of care and accounting. 4. Waste and impairment of security.

**Exceptions / defenses:** In title-theory states the mortgagee may take possession at any time absent contrary agreement.

**Authority:** Common law; Civ. Code §§ 2927, 2929 (no act impairing the security) [VERIFY V-671], 2938 (assignment of rents) [VERIFY V-671].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** B defaults on an apartment-building loan. In California, Bank cannot simply move in and collect rents; it may enforce its assignment of rents or have a receiver appointed.

### R-PRP-093 — Equity of redemption; clogging; reinstatement
**Rule (write this):** Before a foreclosure sale, the mortgagor (and any junior interest holder) may redeem the property by paying the full amount due, which is the entire accelerated debt plus interest and costs if the loan has been accelerated. This equity of redemption cannot be waived or restricted in the mortgage itself; a clause that gives the lender an option to buy the property or that otherwise cuts off redemption is void as a clog on the equity of redemption, although a later, separately bargained transfer for new consideration is valid.

**Elements:** 1. Before sale. 2. Pay full amount due. 3. Who may redeem (mortgagor, junior lienors, successors). 4. Clogging: void if part of the original transaction.

**Exceptions / defenses:** California also gives a statutory right to reinstate a loan under a deed of trust by curing the arrears (rather than paying the full accelerated debt) until five business days before a trustee's sale (Civ. Code § 2924c). Contracts for forfeiture of property subject to a lien, and contracts restraining the right of redemption, are void (Civ. Code § 2889).

**Authority:** Common law; Civ. Code §§ 2889, 2903, 2924c.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: equitable redemption (full accelerated debt) vs. statutory redemption (after sale, R-PRP-103). CA essay: add § 2924c reinstatement.

**Example:** Bank's mortgage says that on default Bank may buy the property for $1. The clause is a void clog; B may still redeem before sale by paying the debt.

### R-PRP-094 — Transfers by the mortgagor: "subject to" vs. assumption
**Rule (write this):** A mortgagor may sell the mortgaged land unless a due-on-sale clause is enforced (R-PRP-096), and the mortgage remains on the land. A buyer who takes "subject to" the mortgage is not personally liable on the debt, so the lender can look only to the land and to the original mortgagor; a buyer who expressly "assumes" the mortgage becomes personally liable to the lender as the primary obligor, while the original mortgagor remains liable as a surety unless the lender releases it.

**Elements:** 1. Transfer. 2. Subject to (no personal liability) or assumption (express promise to pay). 3. Original mortgagor's continuing liability. 4. Lender's rights: foreclose on the land; sue the original mortgagor; sue an assuming grantee (third-party beneficiary).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Courts rarely imply an assumption, although some find one where the price was reduced by the mortgage balance and the grantee's conduct shows a promise. A novation (lender's release of the original mortgagor) ends its liability.

**Authority:** Common law.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule. MBE favorite: who can the lender sue after two transfers?

**Example:** M sells to A, who assumes the loan; A sells to B "subject to" the loan. On default, Bank may foreclose on the land and sue M and A for any deficiency, but not B.

### R-PRP-095 — Suretyship and subrogation
**Rule (write this):** After an assumption, the assuming grantee is the principal debtor and the original mortgagor is a surety: a surety who pays the debt is subrogated to the lender's rights against the grantee and the land, and a surety is discharged to the extent the lender and grantee, without the surety's consent, materially modify the obligation in a way that prejudices it (for example, by raising the interest rate or extending time). Equitable subrogation also lets a lender who pays off a senior mortgage take that mortgage's priority over intervening liens when it expected to be secured and the intervening lienholders are not prejudiced.

**Elements:** 1. Principal–surety relationship. 2. Modification without surety's consent → discharge to the extent of prejudice. 3. Subrogation on payment. 4. Refinancing lender's equitable subrogation. 5. Modification of a senior mortgage that increases the burden is subordinate, as to the increase, to existing junior liens.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A volunteer who pays another's debt without obligation or expectation of security is not subrogated.

**Authority:** Common law of suretyship; Restatement (Third) of Property: Mortgages (subrogation and modification) [VERIFY V-673].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** A assumes M's mortgage; Bank and A later agree to raise the rate from 5% to 8%. On A's default, M is liable only for what she would have owed at 5%.

### R-PRP-096 — Restrictions on transfer: due-on-sale clauses
**Rule (write this):** A due-on-sale clause lets the lender accelerate the debt if the borrower transfers the property without the lender's consent. Federal law makes such clauses enforceable nationwide, preempting state rules that restricted them, subject to statutory exceptions for residential property of one to four units, including transfers to a spouse or children, transfers on a borrower's death to a relative or on a joint tenant's death, transfers under a divorce decree, transfers into an inter vivos trust in which the borrower remains a beneficiary (and, under the regulation, an occupant), the creation of junior liens, and leases of three years or less without purchase options.

**Elements:** 1. Clause in the mortgage. 2. Transfer. 3. Exception? 4. Lender may accelerate and foreclose if the debt is not paid.

**Exceptions / defenses:** California's former rule limiting enforcement to cases where the transfer impaired the lender's security (*Wellenkamp v. Bank of America* (1978) 21 Cal.3d 943) is preempted by federal law.

**Authority:** Garn–St Germain Depository Institutions Act, 12 U.S.C. § 1701j-3(d) [S-1608]; 12 C.F.R. § 191.5 [S-1608]; *Fidelity Federal Savings & Loan Ass'n v. de la Cuesta*, 458 U.S. 141 (1982).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** B sells her house to X, who agrees to keep paying B's 3% loan. Bank discovers the sale and accelerates under its due-on-sale clause. Bank may do so; no exception applies.

### R-PRP-097 — Transfers by the mortgagee
**Rule (write this):** A mortgagee may transfer the note and mortgage; the mortgage follows the note, so a transfer of the note carries the mortgage with it even without a separate assignment unless the parties agree otherwise, and only a person entitled to enforce the note may foreclose. Traditionally an assignment of the mortgage without the note is a nullity, because the mortgage is only an incident of the debt, but the Restatement (Third) of Property: Mortgages presumes that a transfer of the mortgage also transfers the note unless the parties agree otherwise (subject to the UCC). A transferee who takes a negotiable note as a holder in due course takes free of the borrower's personal defenses (such as fraud in the inducement) but not real defenses (such as forgery or fraud in the factum).

**Elements:** 1. Transfer of the note (negotiation or assignment). 2. Mortgage follows; the person entitled to enforce the note forecloses. 3. Mortgage assigned without the note: traditional nullity vs. Restatement presumption that the note passed too. 4. Holder in due course status. 5. Borrower's payments: who must be paid.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Under the Restatement (Third) of Property: Mortgages and the 2002 revision of UCC Article 3, a borrower who pays the original lender before receiving notice of the transfer is protected; under the traditional negotiable-instrument rule, payment to a person no longer holding the note does not discharge it [VERIFY V-673]. In California, a borrower whose home was sold in a nonjudicial foreclosure has standing to challenge an assignment as void (*Yvanova v. New Century Mortgage Corp.* (2016) 62 Cal.4th 919).

**Authority:** Common law; UCC §§ 3-302, 3-305; *Carpenter v. Longan*, 83 U.S. (16 Wall.) 271 (1872) (mortgage follows the note); Restatement (Third) of Property: Mortgages § 5.4(a) (transfer of the obligation carries the mortgage), § 5.4(b) (transfer of the mortgage presumptively carries the obligation) [S-779] [VERIFY V-676], § 5.4(c) (only a person entitled to enforce the obligation may enforce the mortgage) [VERIFY V-676].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule. MBE: the mortgage follows the note, and the party who may foreclose is the person entitled to enforce the note; if only the mortgage was assigned, state the traditional rule (a nullity) and the Restatement's contrary presumption.

**Example:** Bank sells B's note to Fund by indorsement and delivery but never records an assignment of the mortgage. Fund may foreclose because the mortgage followed the note.

### R-PRP-098 — Discharge: payment, prepayment and deed in lieu
**Rule (write this):** A mortgage is discharged when the secured obligation is paid, and the lender must then record a release or reconveyance. Under the traditional "perfect tender in time" rule the borrower has no right to prepay before maturity unless the loan documents or a statute allow it, while the Restatement (Third) of Property: Mortgages § 6.1 lets the borrower prepay unless the note or mortgage prohibits or limits prepayment; under either view, a bargained-for prohibition or a reasonable prepayment fee is generally enforced, subject to statute. A deed in lieu of foreclosure, in which the borrower voluntarily conveys the property to the lender in satisfaction of the debt, is valid if made fairly and for adequate consideration in a transaction separate from and later than the loan (typically after default), but the lender takes title subject to junior liens, which a deed in lieu does not extinguish.

**Elements:** 1. Payment in full → release or reconveyance. 2. Prepayment: traditional rule (no right absent a loan term or statute) vs. Restatement § 6.1 (right unless the loan terms restrict it); express terms control under both; statutory rights for residential loans. 3. Deed in lieu: voluntariness, adequate consideration, a later transaction separate from the loan, typically after default (if exacted at the loan's inception, a clog: R-PRP-093); effect on junior liens and on the debt (usually satisfied).

**Exceptions / defenses:** California requires reconveyance of a deed of trust after payment within a statutory period (Civ. Code § 2941) and regulates prepayment charges on residential loans (Civ. Code § 2954.9) [VERIFY V-658].

**Authority:** Common law; Restatement (Third) of Property: Mortgages § 6.1 [S-780] [VERIFY V-676]; Civ. Code §§ 2941, 2954.9.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule. MBE: if the loan documents address prepayment, apply them; if they are silent, state the traditional no-right-to-prepay rule and note the Restatement's contrary default. CA essay: add Civ. Code § 2954.9 for residential loans.

**Example:** After default, B gives Bank a deed in lieu of foreclosure. B's second lender, Credit Co., still has its lien, so Bank owns the house subject to Credit Co.'s junior lien; a foreclosure would have wiped it out.

### R-PRP-099 — Foreclosure: types and acceleration
**Rule (write this):** On default, the mortgagee may foreclose to sell the property and apply the proceeds to the debt. Judicial foreclosure, available everywhere, is a court action ending in a supervised sale; nonjudicial (power-of-sale) foreclosure, used with deeds of trust, is a private sale by the trustee after statutory notices; strict foreclosure (title vests in the lender without a sale) survives in only a few states. If the instrument has an acceleration clause, the lender may declare the entire debt due on default; without one, it may foreclose only for the overdue installments.

**Elements:** 1. Default. 2. Acceleration (clause; notice; waiver by accepting late payments without notice). 3. Method of foreclosure and statutory notices. 4. Fair sale: a low price alone does not invalidate a regularly conducted sale. A court may set the sale aside if the price is so grossly inadequate that it shocks the conscience (Restatement (Third) of Property: Mortgages § 8.3(a): gross inadequacy alone suffices; cmt. b: gross inadequacy cannot be fixed as a percentage, but a court is generally warranted in setting aside a sale for less than 20% of fair market value, and usually not above that absent other defects; courts differ on following the comment) [S-1603], or if a substantially inadequate price is coupled with fraud, unfairness or an irregularity in the sale, even a slight one. California does not set aside a trustee's sale for inadequacy of price alone; gross inadequacy plus even slight unfairness or irregularity can suffice, and the irregularity must have contributed to the low price or otherwise injured the trustor (*Foge v. Schmidt* (1951) 101 Cal.App.2d 681; *6 Angels, Inc. v. Stuart-Wright Mortgage, Inc.* (2001) 85 Cal.App.4th 1279) [S-1604].

**Exceptions / defenses:** California nonjudicial foreclosure requires a recorded notice of default, at least three months' wait, and a notice of sale before the trustee's sale (Civ. Code §§ 2924, 2924f), plus the reinstatement right in § 2924c.

**Authority:** Common law; Civ. Code §§ 2924, 2924c, 2924f; CCP § 726 (judicial foreclosure); Restatement (Third) of Property: Mortgages § 8.3 [S-1603]; *Foge*; *6 Angels* [S-1604].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** B misses three payments; the deed of trust has an acceleration clause. Lender accelerates, and the trustee records a notice of default and later sells at auction.

### R-PRP-100 — Priorities among mortgages and liens
**Rule (write this):** Interests in the same land generally rank by time of creation ("first in time, first in right"), as adjusted by the recording act, so an earlier unrecorded mortgage loses to a later mortgagee for value without notice who qualifies under the act. Further exceptions are purchase-money mortgages (R-PRP-090), subordination agreements, future advances (R-PRP-091), modification of a senior mortgage that increases its burden (subordinate as to the increase), equitable subrogation by a refinancing lender (R-PRP-095), fixture filings (R-PRP-069), and statutory liens such as property-tax liens and, in many states, mechanics' liens.

**Elements:** 1. Order of creation. 2. Recording act adjustment. 3. Special-priority exceptions. 4. Effect: foreclosure wipes out juniors only (R-PRP-101).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Property-tax liens take priority over private liens regardless of timing [VERIFY V-661]. A federal tax lien's priority against a later purchaser or mortgagee depends on filing notice (26 U.S.C. § 6323).

**Authority:** Common law; Civ. Code §§ 1214, 2897, 2898 [S-770]; 26 U.S.C. §§ 6321, 6323.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule. Tested Feb 2025 Q3 (race-notice priority of a mortgage) [VERIFY V-071].

**Example:** O gives Bank A a mortgage (unrecorded), then gives Bank B a mortgage; Bank B has no notice and records first. In a race-notice jurisdiction, Bank B is senior.

### R-PRP-101 — Parties to foreclosure; effect on senior and junior interests
**Rule (write this):** Foreclosure of a mortgage extinguishes that mortgage and all interests junior to it whose holders are properly joined (judicial) or given the required notice (nonjudicial), and the buyer takes subject to all senior interests, which are unaffected. A junior interest holder who should have been joined but was omitted keeps its interest and its right to redeem, and the buyer may have to foreclose again against it.

**Elements:** 1. Identify the foreclosing interest. 2. Senior interests: survive; the buyer takes subject to them. 3. Junior interests: wiped out if joined or noticed; they share in surplus. 4. Necessary parties: mortgagor, current owner, junior lienholders and junior tenants. 5. Omitted junior: survives.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A lease senior to the mortgage survives foreclosure; a junior lease is terminated if the tenant is joined (subject to tenant-protection statutes).

**Authority:** Common law.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule. Tested Feb 2014 Q4 (claim to share in foreclosure proceeds) [VERIFY V-071].

**Example:** O's land has a first mortgage (Bank 1), a second (Bank 2) and a third (Bank 3). Bank 2 forecloses, joining Bank 3. Buyer takes subject to Bank 1's mortgage; Bank 3's lien is extinguished and Bank 3 looks to the surplus.

### R-PRP-102 — Distribution of proceeds; deficiency and surplus
**Rule (write this):** Sale proceeds are applied first to the costs of the sale, then to the foreclosing mortgage's debt, then to junior interests in order of priority, with any remaining surplus paid to the mortgagor; senior interests are not paid from the proceeds. If the proceeds do not satisfy the debt, the mortgagee may obtain a personal deficiency judgment against the mortgagor (and any assuming grantee), unless a statute bars or limits it, for example to the difference between the debt and the property's fair market value.

**Elements:** 1. Order of distribution. 2. Surplus to juniors, then mortgagor. 3. Deficiency judgment: availability and limits (fair-value and anti-deficiency statutes). 4. Personal liability: original mortgagor, assuming grantee; not a "subject to" grantee.

**Exceptions / defenses:** California sharply limits deficiencies (R-PRP-104).

**Authority:** Common law; CCP §§ 580a, 726(b) [S-763].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule. MBE favorite: compute who gets what from the proceeds.

**Example:** Second mortgagee forecloses: debt $100,000, third mortgage $50,000; sale yields $130,000 (costs $5,000). Second gets $100,000, third gets $25,000, O gets nothing, and the first mortgage stays on the land.

### R-PRP-103 — Statutory redemption after foreclosure
**Rule (write this):** About half of the states give the mortgagor (and sometimes junior lienholders) a statutory right to redeem the property for a fixed period after the foreclosure sale by paying the sale price plus costs and interest, not the full debt; the mortgagor usually keeps possession during the period. This is distinct from the equity of redemption, which ends at the sale.

**Elements:** 1. Statute. 2. Period after sale. 3. Amount: foreclosure-sale price. 4. Who may redeem.

**Exceptions / defenses:** In California, after a judicial foreclosure sale the redemption period is one year if the proceeds are insufficient to satisfy the debt and a deficiency is available, and three months if the proceeds satisfy the secured debt, interest and costs (CCP § 729.030) [S-763]; there is no statutory redemption after a nonjudicial trustee's sale.

**Authority:** State statutes; CCP §§ 726, 729.010–729.090 [S-763].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: know the two kinds of redemption. CA essay: rarely tested; include if a judicial sale is involved.

**Example:** After a judicial foreclosure sale in California for less than the debt, with a deficiency sought, O has one year to redeem by paying the sale price.

### R-PRP-104 — California's one-action and anti-deficiency rules
**Rule (write this):** California's one-action rule requires a creditor whose debt is secured by real property to enforce the debt in a single action that forecloses on the security first; a creditor who sues on the note without foreclosing risks losing the security. No deficiency judgment is allowed after a nonjudicial (power-of-sale) foreclosure, or on a purchase-money loan (a seller-financed loan, or a third-party loan used to buy an owner-occupied dwelling of one to four units); after a judicial foreclosure that allows a deficiency, the deficiency is limited to the amount by which the debt exceeds the property's fair value.

**Elements:** 1. One-action / security-first rule (CCP § 726). 2. § 580d: no deficiency after a trustee's sale. 3. § 580b: purchase-money protection. 4. § 726(b) and § 580a: fair-value limits. 5. Advance waivers are unenforceable.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A "sold-out" junior lienholder whose security was wiped out by a senior's nonjudicial sale may sue on its note unless § 580b applies (*Roseleaf Corp. v. Chierighino* (1963) 59 Cal.2d 35). Anti-deficiency protection cannot be waived in the loan documents (*Palm v. Schilling* (1988) 199 Cal.App.3d 63) [S-777].

**Authority:** CCP §§ 580a, 580b, 580d, 726 [S-763] [S-777].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Not MBE (the MBE uses general deficiency rules). CA essay: add a short "In California, …" paragraph whenever a lender seeks a deficiency.

**Example:** B bought her home with a bank loan and lives in it. After a trustee's sale leaves $80,000 unpaid, Bank sues for the deficiency. Barred twice over: § 580d (nonjudicial sale) and § 580b (purchase-money loan on an owner-occupied dwelling).

## V Titles

### R-PRP-105 — Adverse possession: elements
**Rule (write this):** A person who possesses land actually, openly and notoriously, exclusively, adversely (hostile, without the owner's permission) and continuously for the statutory period acquires title to it; the owner's action to recover possession is barred, and the possessor gains a new title by operation of law. Under the majority objective view, the possessor's state of mind is irrelevant; it is enough that the possession was without the owner's consent.

**Elements:** 1. Actual possession (use as an average owner would, given the land's character). 2. Open and notorious (visible enough to put a reasonable owner on notice). 3. Exclusive (not shared with the owner or the public). 4. Hostile (no permission; objective test; minority good-faith or bad-faith tests). 5. Continuous for the statutory period (seasonal use suffices if typical for the land). 6. Some states (including California) add color of title or payment of taxes requirements.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Permission from the owner defeats hostility; possession that begins with permission becomes hostile only after a clear repudiation communicated to the owner. Possession by a cotenant requires ouster (R-PRP-024). A possessor who acknowledges the owner's title (e.g., offers to buy) may lose hostility in jurisdictions using a subjective test.

**Authority:** Common law; *Van Valkenburgh v. Lutz*, 304 N.Y. 95, 106 N.E.2d 28 (1952); *Manillo v. Gorski*, 54 N.J. 378, 255 A.2d 258 (1969) (minor boundary encroachment not open and notorious without actual knowledge).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: use the period stated in the question and the objective test. CA essay: state the general elements, then California's five-year period and tax-payment requirement (R-PRP-106). Tested Oct 2020 Q3 [VERIFY V-071].

**Example:** A fences, farms and pays nothing for a neighbor's 2-acre strip for 12 years, believing it hers. In a ten-year state, A has title by adverse possession.

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: PRC law has no acquisitive prescription for real property; registration controls, so adverse possession has no direct counterpart (approx.).

### R-PRP-106 — Adverse possession in California
**Rule (write this):** In California, adverse possession requires possession that is actual, open and notorious, hostile and under a claim of right or color of title, and continuous and uninterrupted for five years, together with timely payment of all state, county and municipal taxes levied and assessed on the land during those five years. Under color of title (a written instrument the possessor relies on), occupying part of the described land gives constructive possession of the whole; without color of title, only land actually occupied and protected by a substantial enclosure, or usually cultivated or improved, is acquired.

**Elements:** 1. The general elements (R-PRP-105). 2. Five years (CCP §§ 318, 321). 3. Taxes levied and assessed during the five years, timely paid and proved by county tax-collector records (CCP § 325(b)) [S-756]. 4. Color of title (CCP §§ 322, 323) or claim of right with enclosure, cultivation or improvement (CCP §§ 324, 325) [S-756].

**Exceptions / defenses:** The tax requirement usually defeats boundary-strip claims, because the record owner pays the taxes on the strip as part of its own parcel; the possessor then argues prescriptive easement (no tax requirement unless separately assessed: *Gilardi v. Hallam* (1981) 30 Cal.3d 317 [S-757]) or agreed boundary (R-PRP-108). No adverse possession runs against public entities or land dedicated to public use (Civ. Code § 1007).

**Authority:** CCP §§ 318, 321, 322, 323, 324, 325 [S-756]; Civ. Code § 1007; *Gilardi v. Hallam* [S-757].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Not on the MBE. CA essay: add "In California, the period is five years and the possessor must have paid all taxes levied and assessed (CCP § 325)" after the general rule.

**Example:** For six years D fenced and gardened a 10-foot strip of P's lot, but the strip was assessed with P's lot and P paid the taxes. D fails to acquire title in California; D may claim a prescriptive easement, but courts rarely grant one that excludes P entirely [VERIFY V-654].

### R-PRP-107 — Adverse possession: tacking, disabilities, scope and future interests
**Rule (write this):** Successive adverse possessors may tack their periods together if they are in privity, meaning the possession passed by a voluntary transfer such as a deed, will, inheritance or even an oral transfer of possession, but not by abandonment or ouster. The statute does not run against an owner who was under a disability (minority, insanity, imprisonment) when the adverse possession began, and it does not run against the holder of a future interest until that interest becomes possessory.

**Elements:** 1. Tacking: privity between possessors. 2. Disabilities: only those existing when possession began; later disabilities do not stop the clock; statutes often allow a grace period after the disability ends. 3. Scope: actual possession, or constructive possession of the whole parcel described in a color-of-title instrument. 4. Future interests: possession against a life tenant gains only the life estate.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Tacking on the owner's side is automatic: a sale by the record owner does not interrupt the period. No adverse possession of government land.

**Authority:** Common law; *Howard v. Kunto*, 3 Wash. App. 393, 477 P.2d 210 (1970) (tacking where deeds misdescribed the land); CCP § 328 (disabilities) [VERIFY V-666].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule. MBE favorite: compute whether the period ran, using tacking and disabilities.

**Example:** O, the owner, is 10 when A enters adversely in a ten-year state with a disability rule. The statute cannot run against O until O turns 18 (plus any grace period). If O instead became insane five years after A entered, the clock keeps running.

### R-PRP-108 — Boundary doctrines: agreed boundaries, acquiescence, estoppel and encroachments
**Rule (write this):** Neighbors who are uncertain where the true boundary lies may fix it by agreement, and a boundary so agreed and accepted (acquiesced in) for a substantial period binds them and their successors even if it differs from the deed line. Some states also fix a boundary by long acquiescence alone or by estoppel, where one owner represents the line and the other relies on it. A building that encroaches slightly on a neighbor's land is a trespass, but a court may deny removal of a good-faith, minor encroachment and award damages instead.

**Elements:** 1. Uncertainty about the true line. 2. Agreement (express or implied). 3. Acceptance and acquiescence. 4. Encroachments: relative hardship balancing.

**Exceptions / defenses:** California applies the agreed-boundary doctrine narrowly: it requires real uncertainty and is not used where the true line can be determined from the legal records (*Bryant v. Blevins* (1994) 9 Cal.4th 47).

**Authority:** Common law; *Bryant v. Blevins* (1994) 9 Cal.4th 47.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** Two neighbors unsure of their line build a fence together where they guess it lies and treat it as the line for 20 years. A survey shows the fence is 3 feet off. Most courts hold the fence line binds them as an agreed boundary.

### R-PRP-109 — Deed formalities and descriptions
**Rule (write this):** A deed must be in writing, signed by the grantor, identify the grantor and the grantee, describe the land with enough certainty to identify it, and contain words showing an intent to transfer an interest presently; consideration, a seal and witnesses are not required, and acknowledgment is needed only for recording. In construing a description, courts give priority to natural monuments, then artificial monuments, then courses (directions), then distances, then area (quantity).

**Elements:** 1. Writing and signature. 2. Grantor and an existing grantee (a deed to a dead person is void). 3. Description: metes and bounds, government survey or recorded plat; extrinsic evidence may resolve latent ambiguities. 4. Words of grant. 5. Delivery (R-PRP-110).

**Exceptions / defenses:** If the grantee's name is left blank, the person to whom the deed is delivered is presumed authorized to fill it in; if the description is left blank, the deed is void unless the grantee was expressly authorized to complete it. A description bounded by a road generally carries title to the center line.

**Authority:** Common law; Civ. Code §§ 1091, 1092 (statutory grant-deed form), 1105; Gov. Code § 27287 (acknowledgment for recording).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** A deed describes land as "north 200 feet to the old oak, then east 300 feet." The oak is actually 230 feet north. The monument (the oak) controls over the distance.

### R-PRP-110 — Delivery and acceptance; escrow
**Rule (write this):** A deed passes title only when delivered, meaning the grantor by words or conduct shows an intent that the deed be presently operative, even if the grantee's possession is postponed. Handing the deed to the grantee raises a rebuttable presumption of delivery, as does recording; the grantor's keeping the deed raises a presumption of no delivery. Once title passes by delivery, the grantee's return or destruction of the deed does not reconvey title.

**Elements:** 1. Intent to make the deed presently effective. 2. Presumptions (physical transfer, recording, retention). 3. Conditional delivery to the grantee: the condition is ineffective and delivery is absolute (majority). 4. Delivery to a third party: commercial escrow (title passes when conditions are met; relation back where necessary) or death escrow (valid if the grantor gives up all power to recall). 5. Acceptance (presumed if beneficial).

**Exceptions / defenses:** A deed found in the grantor's own safe-deposit box after death, with no relinquishment of control, was never delivered. A deed given to a grantee with instructions "effective at my death" but with the grantor keeping control may be an invalid attempt at a will.

**Authority:** Common law; Civ. Code §§ 1054 (grant takes effect on delivery), 1055 (presumed delivered at its date), 1056 (no conditional delivery to the grantee), 1057 (escrow).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule. Tested Oct 2020 Q3 [VERIFY V-071].

**Example:** G signs a deed to her son S, hands it to her lawyer with irrevocable instructions to give it to S when G dies, and keeps no right to retrieve it. At G's death the lawyer delivers it. Valid delivery: S had a future interest from the moment of the deposit.

### R-PRP-111 — Covenants of title; types of deeds
**Rule (write this):** A general warranty deed contains six covenants of title: three present covenants (seisin, right to convey, and against encumbrances), which are breached, if at all, at delivery, and three future covenants (general warranty, quiet enjoyment and further assurances), which are breached only when the grantee is actually or constructively evicted by someone with paramount title. A special warranty deed contains the same covenants but only against defects arising from the grantor's own acts; a quitclaim deed contains no covenants.

**Elements:** 1. Type of deed. 2. Which covenant is breached. 3. Timing: present covenants at delivery (statute of limitations runs then; majority, they do not run to remote grantees); future covenants on eviction (they run with the land to successors). 4. Damages: usually limited to the purchase price received by the covenantor plus interest (or, for an encumbrance, the cost of removal or the loss in value).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Some courts hold that an open, visible easement known to the buyer does not breach the covenant against encumbrances. In California, the word "grant" implies two covenants: that the grantor has not previously conveyed the same estate or any right in it, and that the estate is free from encumbrances done, made or suffered by the grantor or anyone claiming under the grantor (Civ. Code § 1113), so a California grant deed works like a limited special warranty deed.

**Authority:** Common law; Civ. Code § 1113.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule. Tested Feb 2025 Q3 (claims under a general warranty deed) [VERIFY V-071].

**Example:** B buys from S by general warranty deed; a recorded mortgage from S's predecessor still encumbers the land. The covenant against encumbrances was breached at delivery; if the mortgagee later forecloses and ousts B, the covenants of warranty and quiet enjoyment are breached too.

### R-PRP-112 — Estoppel by deed (after-acquired title)
**Rule (write this):** If a grantor conveys land, usually by warranty deed, that the grantor does not then own, and later acquires title, the title passes automatically to the grantee (or the grantee may instead sue for breach of the covenants). The doctrine does not apply to a quitclaim deed, which conveys only whatever interest the grantor had at the time.

**Elements:** 1. Deed purporting to convey title the grantor lacks (warranty deed; in California any deed purporting to grant a fee simple). 2. Grantor later acquires title. 3. Title inures to the grantee.

**Exceptions / defenses:** A bona fide purchaser who buys from the grantor after the grantor acquires title may prevail over the first grantee in states that treat the earlier deed as outside the chain of title (R-PRP-118).

**Authority:** Common law; Civ. Code § 1106.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** In 2025, S gives B a warranty deed to Blackacre, which S does not own. In 2026, S inherits Blackacre from its owner. Title passes to B automatically.

### R-PRP-113 — Void and voidable deeds
**Rule (write this):** A void deed passes no title, even to a later bona fide purchaser; deeds are void if forged, never delivered (e.g., stolen), executed by a person adjudicated incompetent, or obtained by fraud in the factum (the grantor did not know the document was a deed). A voidable deed passes title that the grantor may set aside, but not against a bona fide purchaser; deeds are voidable if obtained by fraud in the inducement, undue influence or duress, or executed by a minor or a person lacking capacity who has not been adjudicated incompetent.

**Elements:** 1. Identify the defect. 2. Void (no title to anyone) vs. voidable (good title to a BFP). 3. Estoppel: an owner whose negligence enabled the fraud may be estopped against a BFP in some states.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Recording does not validate a forged deed (R-PRP-119).

**Authority:** Common law.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** X forges O's signature on a deed to himself and sells to B, a BFP. O still owns the land; B's remedy is against X (and B's title insurer).

### R-PRP-114 — Persons authorized to execute documents
**Rule (write this):** Only the owner, or someone with actual authority, can convey an interest in land. An agent's authority to convey land or sign a land contract must be in writing (equal dignities), corporate officers must be authorized by the board or bylaws, fiduciaries such as trustees, personal representatives and conservators act within the powers given by the instrument, statute or court order, and one cotenant can convey only her own share.

**Elements:** 1. Who holds title (individual, co-owners, spouses, entity, trust, estate). 2. Source and scope of authority. 3. Formalities (written authority; court approval when required).

**Exceptions / defenses:** In California, a corporate instrument signed by the chair, president or a vice president and by the secretary, an assistant secretary, the chief financial officer or an assistant treasurer is not invalid for lack of authority as against a person without actual knowledge of the lack (Corp. Code § 313). Community real property generally requires both spouses to join in a sale, lease over one year or encumbrance (Fam. Code § 1102; R-CMP-067).

**Authority:** Common law of agency; Civ. Code § 2309; Corp. Code § 313; Fam. Code § 1102; Prob. Code § 4000 et seq. (powers of attorney).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** H alone signs a deed selling the couple's community-property house to B. In California, W may void the sale within the statutory period, subject to protections for a purchaser without notice of the marriage (R-CMP-067).

### R-PRP-115 — Transfer by will and by operation of law
**Rule (write this):** Title to land also passes at death by will or intestacy under the law of the situs, outside probate by survivorship, a revocable trust or (in California) a revocable transfer-on-death deed, and by operation of law through adverse possession, eminent domain, foreclosure and tax sales, escheat and court judgments such as partition and quiet title. Specific devises of land are affected by ademption (the land is no longer in the estate), lapse and anti-lapse, and the rule on whether a devisee takes subject to a mortgage.

**Elements:** 1. Mode of transfer. 2. Situs law (R-PRP-049). 3. Will doctrines: ademption, lapse/anti-lapse (R-WIL-063), exoneration. 4. Nonprobate transfers. 5. Involuntary transfers.

**Exceptions / defenses:** California's revocable transfer-on-death deed must be signed and dated by the owner, acknowledged before a notary and signed by two witnesses, and must be recorded within a short period after execution to be effective [S-767] [VERIFY V-660]. In California, a specific devise passes subject to any mortgage, without exoneration (Prob. Code § 21131).

**Authority:** Prob. Code §§ 5600 et seq., 5624 [S-767], 21131; cross-reference R-WIL-062, R-WIL-063, R-REM-093.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule. Tested with Wills Oct 2020 Q3 [VERIFY V-071].

**Example:** T's will devises "my Elm Street house to my niece N." T sells the house before death. The devise is adeemed by extinction; N takes nothing under that clause.

### R-PRP-116 — Recording acts: types and California
**Rule (write this):** An unrecorded conveyance is valid between the parties, but recording acts protect later purchasers. Under a race statute, whoever records first wins regardless of notice; under a notice statute, a later bona fide purchaser for value without notice prevails over an earlier unrecorded interest, even if the later purchaser never records; under a race-notice statute, a later bona fide purchaser prevails only if it also records before the earlier interest. California has a race-notice statute.

**Elements:** 1. Identify the statute type from its language ("first duly recorded" = race-notice; "without notice" alone = notice; no notice language = race). 2. Is the later taker a purchaser or mortgagee for value? 3. Without notice when value was paid? 4. Race-notice: recorded first? 5. Recorded instruments give constructive notice to later takers.

**Exceptions / defenses:** California's act excludes leases for a term not exceeding one year (Civ. Code § 1214) and also protects a judgment affecting title where notice of the action is recorded before the earlier conveyance. Shelter rule and void deeds: R-PRP-119.

**Authority:** Civ. Code §§ 1213 (recording is constructive notice), 1214 (race-notice), 1215 (conveyance defined), 1217 (unrecorded instrument valid between the parties).

**MBE vs. CA essay:** MBE: read the statute quoted in the question. CA essay: use the statute the call names; otherwise analyze all three types briefly, then apply California's race-notice rule. Tested Feb 2015 Q2, Jul 2024 Q2, Feb 2025 Q3 [VERIFY V-071].

**Example:** O sells to A, who does not record. O then sells to B, who pays value without notice. A records; then B records. Race: A wins (recorded first). Notice: B wins. Race-notice (California): A wins, because B did not record first.

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: PRC real-property rights generally arise only on registration (登记生效); in the US the deed passes title on delivery, and recording only protects priority against later purchasers (approx.).

### R-PRP-117 — Bona fide purchaser status: value and notice
**Rule (write this):** To be protected by a notice or race-notice act, a later taker must be a purchaser (or mortgagee) for value who took without notice of the earlier interest at the time value was paid. Value means more than nominal consideration, not necessarily market value; donees, heirs and devisees are not protected. Notice may be actual, constructive (record notice from properly recorded instruments in the chain of title), or inquiry notice from facts that would lead a reasonable buyer to investigate, such as someone other than the seller in possession or a reference in a recorded document to an unrecorded one.

**Elements:** 1. Purchaser or mortgagee. 2. Value (majority: a mortgage taken for a preexisting debt is not for value; partial payment protects pro tanto). 3. Good faith without notice when value is paid. 4. Types of notice: actual, record, inquiry, imputed (through an agent).

**Exceptions / defenses:** A buyer is charged with what a reasonable inspection and inquiry of persons in possession would reveal. A quitclaim grantee can be a BFP in most states.

**Authority:** Common law; Civ. Code §§ 1213, 1214.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule. Inquiry notice was tested Jul 2024 Q2 [VERIFY V-071].

**Example:** O sells to A, who does not record but moves in. O then sells to B, who never inspects. B is charged with inquiry notice of A's possession, so B is not a BFP and A prevails under any notice-based statute.

### R-PRP-118 — Chain-of-title problems
**Rule (write this):** A purchaser has constructive notice only of instruments that are recorded within the chain of title, meaning those a reasonable search of the grantor-grantee index would find. A wild deed (one whose grantor's own acquisition was never recorded) gives no constructive notice; under the majority rule, a deed recorded late, after the grantor's later conveyance to another, or recorded early, before the grantor acquired title, is also outside the chain of title.

**Elements:** 1. Index system (grantor-grantee vs. tract). 2. Search period for each owner: from acquisition to recording of the conveyance out. 3. Wild deeds. 4. Late-recorded and early-recorded deeds (majority vs. minority "extended search" states). 5. Misindexed instruments (split: effective on filing vs. not notice).

**Exceptions / defenses:** In a tract-index jurisdiction, all instruments affecting the parcel are found together, which reduces these problems.

**Authority:** Common law.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** O conveys to A, who does not record. A conveys to B, who records. O then conveys to C, a BFP who records. B's deed is a wild deed because O-to-A was never recorded; C had no record notice and prevails.

### R-PRP-119 — Shelter rule and the limits of recording
**Rule (write this):** Under the shelter rule, a person who takes from a bona fide purchaser stands in the BFP's shoes and is protected even if the person had notice of the earlier interest, except that a prior owner who conveyed in breach of a duty cannot reacquire and claim shelter. Recording does not validate a void instrument (such as a forged deed), and the recording acts do not protect against interests that arise outside the record, such as title by adverse possession, prescriptive easements and rights of persons in possession.

**Elements:** 1. Did a BFP intervene in the chain? 2. Shelter for later takers. 3. Exception for the wrongdoer. 4. Off-record risks.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Some implied easements and easements by necessity also bind purchasers without record notice (often through inquiry notice from visible use).

**Authority:** Common law.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** O conveys to A (unrecorded), then to B, a BFP who records. B gives the land to her son C, who knows of A's deed. C is sheltered by B's status and prevails over A.

### R-PRP-120 — Title insurance
**Rule (write this):** Title insurance is a contract of indemnity under which the insurer agrees, up to the policy amount, to pay for loss caused by covered defects in the insured's title as of the policy date and to defend covered title claims. The owner's policy protects only the named insured (and limited successors such as heirs) and does not run with the land to a later buyer; the lender's policy protects the mortgagee and follows the loan. Standard policies exclude matters not shown by the public records (rights of parties in possession, unrecorded easements, survey matters), governmental regulations such as zoning, and defects the insured knew of but did not disclose.

**Elements:** 1. Policy type (owner's or lender's; standard or extended). 2. Covered defect within the insuring clause. 3. Exceptions and exclusions. 4. Loss and the duty to defend.

**Exceptions / defenses:** Most courts treat the insurer's liability as contractual only and do not impose a separate tort duty to search; an abstractor who negligently searches may be liable to its client. In California, a preliminary title report is an offer to issue a policy, not a representation of the state of title (Ins. Code § 12340.11) [VERIFY V-657].

**Authority:** Common law and policy terms; Ins. Code § 12340 et seq. [VERIFY V-657].

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** B's owner's policy lists no exception for a recorded utility easement that the insurer missed. When the utility enforces it, B recovers the resulting loss in value from the insurer, up to the policy limit.

### R-PRP-121 — Special problems: judgment liens, tax liens and lis pendens
**Rule (write this):** A judgment becomes a lien on the debtor's real property in a county when it is recorded or docketed there, attaching to land the debtor then owns or later acquires, but only to the debtor's actual interest; a purchase-money mortgage takes priority over it. A federal tax lien arises on assessment but is not valid against purchasers, secured creditors and judgment lien creditors until notice is filed. A recorded notice of a pending action affecting title (lis pendens) gives constructive notice, so later purchasers take subject to the judgment.

**Elements:** 1. Judgment lien: recording or docketing; scope; duration (California: an abstract of judgment creates a lien for ten years, CCP § 697.310). 2. Judgment creditor is not a purchaser for value at common law. 3. Tax liens: property-tax priority; federal tax lien filing. 4. Lis pendens: effect on later takers; expungement where the claim lacks probable validity. 5. Estoppel by deed (R-PRP-112).

**Exceptions / defenses:** Whether an unrecorded conveyance by the debtor beats a later judgment lien depends on whether the state's recording act protects lien creditors.

**Authority:** CCP §§ 405.20 et seq. [VERIFY V-671], 697.310; 26 U.S.C. §§ 6321, 6323; cross-reference R-REM-074.

**MBE vs. CA essay:** Same rule.

**Example:** C records an abstract of judgment against D in County X. A year later D inherits a house there. C's lien attaches to the house immediately on D's acquisition.
