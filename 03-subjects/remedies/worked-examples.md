# Remedies — worked examples

All fact patterns are original. Each gives the **rule** (by ID in [`rule-statements.md`](rule-statements.md)), a short **application** that argues both sides where the facts allow, and a **conclusion**, in the shape a California grader expects.

---

## Damages

### WE-01 — Foreseeability and certainty of lost profits (R-REM-009, R-REM-010)
**Facts.** Baker orders a custom oven from Supplier for delivery June 1, telling Supplier only that it is "for the shop." Delivery is six weeks late. Baker, open for three years, claims $18,000 in lost profits for the six weeks and $40,000 for a catering contract with a hotel that it lost because of the delay.

**Analysis.**
- **Ordinary profits.** Lost sales from a missing oven at a bakery arise naturally from late delivery (first limb of *Hadley*). Baker's three-year sales history makes the $18,000 reasonably certain.
- **Hotel contract.** This is a special circumstance. Supplier knew only that the oven was "for the shop," not of a pending hotel deal, so the loss was not foreseeable at contracting.

**Conclusion.** Baker likely recovers the $18,000 but not the $40,000.

### WE-02 — Mitigation (R-REM-011)
**Facts.** Landlord wrongfully locks Tenant, a florist, out of her shop for two months. A comparable shop next door is available for $2,000 a month, but Tenant does nothing and claims $15,000 in lost profits.

**Analysis.** Tenant must take reasonable steps to limit her loss; Landlord bears the burden of proving she could have. Renting next door was reasonable, available and not unduly burdensome. Had she moved, most of the lost profits would have been avoided, and the $4,000 rent would have been a recoverable mitigation cost.

**Conclusion.** Tenant's recovery is likely limited to the losses she could not have avoided, plus $4,000 for the substitute rent. She does not recover the full $15,000.

### WE-03 — Fraud: two measures and California § 3343 (R-REM-023)
**Facts.** Dealer sells Collector a baseball for $30,000, saying it was signed by a famous player. It is a forgery worth $100. Genuine, it would be worth $45,000.

**Analysis.**
- **Out-of-pocket:** $30,000 − $100 = $29,900.
- **Benefit of the bargain:** $45,000 − $100 = $44,900. The majority rule for intentional fraud allows this measure if the represented value is proven with reasonable certainty.
- **California:** fraud in the purchase of property is limited to out-of-pocket loss plus additional damages (§ 3343); Dealer is not a fiduciary.
- **Punitive damages:** a deliberate forgery is "fraud" under § 3294(c), so punitive damages are available on clear and convincing evidence.

**Conclusion.** Under general principles, Collector likely recovers $44,900; under California law, $29,900. In either case punitive damages are available. Rescission and return of the $30,000 is the alternative (WE-12).

### WE-04 — Harm to land: diminution vs. restoration (R-REM-019, R-REM-020)
**Facts.** Without checking the survey, Neighbor's crew cuts down six mature oaks on Owner's rural home lot. The lot's market value falls by $12,000; replanting comparable trees would cost $60,000. Owner has lived there twenty years and chose the lot for its trees.

**Analysis.**
- **Diminution vs. restoration.** The usual measure is the lesser: $12,000. But Owner has a personal reason to restore the lot, so a reasonable restoration cost above diminution may be awarded (*Heninger*).
- **California § 3346.** Injury to trees on another's land is trebled, or only doubled if the trespass was casual and involuntary. Not checking the survey looks negligent rather than willful, so double damages are the more likely outcome [VERIFY V-354].

**Conclusion.** Owner likely recovers a reasonable restoration cost (up to $60,000), multiplied under § 3346.

### WE-05 — Conversion vs. restitution (R-REM-021, R-REM-037)
**Facts.** Roommate takes Owner's laptop, worth $1,200, and sells it online for $1,900.

**Analysis.**
- **Damages:** the laptop's value at conversion, $1,200, plus interest.
- **Restitution:** Roommate is a conscious wrongdoer and must disgorge his gain, the $1,900 sale price.
- **Replevin:** available if the buyer can be found. The buyer's good faith does not protect it: a thief has void title and can pass none, so Owner can recover the laptop from the buyer (R-REM-049).

**Conclusion.** Owner should elect restitution of $1,900 from Roommate, or replevy the laptop from the buyer.

### WE-06 — Punitive damages and the due-process ratio (R-REM-027, R-REM-028)
**Facts.** Insurer, following a company-wide policy set by its claims vice-president, deliberately underpays an elderly policyholder's fire claim by $50,000 to pressure her into settling. The jury awards $50,000 compensatory and $5 million punitive damages.

**Analysis.**
- **Liability.** A bad-faith insurance claim sounds in tort in California. Targeting a financially vulnerable victim through deliberate policy is "malice" or "oppression" under § 3294, and a vice-president who sets claims policy is a managing agent (*White*).
- **Amount.** The 100:1 ratio far exceeds single digits. The harm was economic, and the compensatory award is substantial. Reprehensibility is high (a vulnerable target, intentional and repeated policy), but *State Farm* suggests a ratio near single digits, perhaps far lower.

**Conclusion.** Punitive damages are proper, but $5 million is likely reduced to a single-digit multiple of $50,000.

### WE-07 — Land-sale damages and specific performance (R-REM-015, R-REM-071)
**Facts.** Seller agrees to sell Buyer a single-family house for $800,000; Buyer plans to live there. Before closing, the market value rises to $880,000 and Seller refuses to close.

**Analysis.**
- **Damages:** market value at breach − contract price = $80,000, plus Buyer's deposit and escrow costs (Civ. Code § 3306).
- **Specific performance:** the contract is definite, Buyer is ready to pay, and land is unique. Under § 3387 the inadequacy of damages is conclusively presumed for a single-family home the buyer intends to occupy. Ordering a deed is easy to enforce, and no defense appears.

**Conclusion.** Buyer will likely get specific performance and should record a lis pendens at once to prevent a sale to a BFP. Damages of $80,000 are the fallback.

## Restitution

### WE-08 — Quasi-contract and the officious intermeddler (R-REM-038, R-REM-040)
**Facts.** (a) Painter mistakenly paints House A instead of House B; A's owner watched from the window and said nothing. (b) Gardener, unasked, trims a neighbor's hedges while the neighbor is away and sends a bill.

**Analysis.**
- **(a)** A's owner knowingly accepted a benefit he could have stopped. Retaining it without paying is unjust, so Painter recovers its reasonable value, or the increase in the house's value if that is lower (owner is innocent).
- **(b)** Gardener conferred the benefit officiously, giving the neighbor no chance to decline.

**Conclusion.** Painter likely recovers; Gardener does not.

### WE-09 — Constructive trust on appreciated stock (R-REM-044, R-REM-046)
**Facts.** Bookkeeper embezzles $50,000 from Employer and buys shares now worth $95,000. Bookkeeper has many unpaid creditors.

**Analysis.**
- **Damages or quasi-contract** would give Employer a $50,000 money judgment, shared with the other unsecured creditors.
- **Constructive trust.** Bookkeeper holds title to specific property acquired by wrongdoing, traceable to Employer's money. The legal remedy is inadequate because of Bookkeeper's insolvency and the appreciation.

**Conclusion.** A constructive trust over the shares gives Employer $95,000 and priority over Bookkeeper's creditors.

### WE-10 — Equitable lien on a depreciated asset (R-REM-045, R-REM-046)
**Facts.** Same embezzled $50,000, but Bookkeeper uses it to buy a boat now worth $30,000.

**Analysis.** A constructive trust would give Employer only a $30,000 boat. An equitable lien lets Employer force a sale of the boat ($30,000) and obtain a deficiency judgment for the remaining $20,000 as a general creditor.

**Conclusion.** Employer should elect an equitable lien.

### WE-11 — Commingled account and tracing (R-REM-048)
**Facts.** Agent holds $4,000 of her own money in an account. She deposits $20,000 of Principal's funds, withdraws $15,000 to buy a car, withdraws $7,000 for a vacation, then deposits $10,000 of her own salary.

**Analysis.**

| Step | Balance | Principal's traceable share |
|---|---|---|
| Start | $4,000 | — |
| + $20,000 Principal | $24,000 | $20,000 |
| − $15,000 (car) | $9,000 | $9,000 in account; Agent's $4,000 deemed spent first, so $11,000 of Principal's went into the car |
| − $7,000 (vacation) | $2,000 | $2,000 (lowest intermediate balance) |
| + $10,000 salary | $12,000 | still $2,000 |

Principal may claim $2,000 from the account. Principal may also trace into the car: under the spend-own-money-first presumption, $11,000 of the car's $15,000 price came from Principal, supporting a proportionate constructive trust (11/15 of the car) or a lien (R-REM-046). Courts that let the claimant choose the allocation most favorable to it may treat the whole car as bought with Principal's money, leaving Agent's $4,000 to the vacation [VERIFY V-350]. The vacation money is dissipated.

**Conclusion.** Principal recovers $2,000 from the account plus a proportionate trust or lien on the car. The remainder of the $20,000 is a personal money claim against Agent.

### WE-12 — Rescission for fraud; California § 1692 (R-REM-043, R-REM-005)
**Facts.** Buyer buys a condo for $600,000 after Seller falsely says it has never flooded. A month after a flood, Buyer learns the truth, notifies Seller and offers to reconvey. Buyer spent $8,000 moving in.

**Analysis.**
- **Rescission grounds:** fraud. Buyer acted promptly after discovery, gave notice and offered to restore.
- **Relief:** return of the $600,000 against reconveyance. In California, damages are not inconsistent with rescission, so Buyer also recovers the $8,000 as consequential damages, without duplication (§ 1692).

**Conclusion.** Rescission with restitution of the price and recovery of $8,000 is likely. Alternatively, Buyer may keep the condo and sue for fraud damages.

### WE-13 — Bona fide purchaser vs. donee (R-REM-049)
**Facts.** Swindler defrauds Victim into transferring a valuable painting. Swindler sells one print from the same deal to Dealer for full value; Dealer knows nothing. Swindler gives the painting to his niece.

**Analysis.**
- **Dealer.** Swindler had voidable title, which he could pass to a good-faith purchaser for value (UCC § 2-403(1)), so Dealer keeps the print. Victim pursues Swindler for the proceeds.
- **Niece.** She paid nothing; a donee takes subject to Victim's equitable claim.

**Conclusion.** Victim can impose a constructive trust on the painting in the niece's hands but cannot recover the print from Dealer.

## Equitable remedies

### WE-14 — TRO to stop an auction (R-REM-065, R-REM-067, R-REM-072)
**Facts.** Buyer contracted to buy a 19th-century family portrait from Seller for $25,000. Seller instead lists it with an auction house for sale in two days. Buyer's lawyer phones Seller's lawyer, gets no answer, and seeks a TRO.

**Analysis.**
- **Irreparable harm before a hearing:** once the portrait sells to a good-faith bidder, it is gone, and it is unique.
- **Likely success:** a signed contract for a unique chattel supports specific performance (UCC § 2-716).
- **Balance:** a brief delay costs Seller little; the sale would end Buyer's rights.
- **Notice:** counsel's certified notice attempt satisfies FRCP 65(b)(1). In state court, CCP § 527 allows a TRO with an OSC hearing within 15 days.
- **Bond:** required.

**Conclusion.** A TRO barring the sale is likely, followed by a preliminary injunction.

### WE-15 — Preliminary injunction against a city (R-REM-066, R-REM-091)
**Facts.** A city ordinance takes effect in a week, banning outdoor religious processions. A congregation that holds weekly processions sues in federal court.

**Analysis.**
- **Winter factors.** Likely success: the ordinance targets religious activity, so strict scrutiny likely applies. Irreparable harm: loss of First Amendment freedoms, even for short periods. Balance of equities: the city gives up little by waiting for a ruling. Public interest: favors protecting constitutional rights.
- **Declaratory relief.** Also available now, because enforcement is imminent (*Steffel*).

**Conclusion.** A preliminary injunction is likely, with declaratory relief on the merits.

### WE-16 — Encroaching garage and the equitable easement (R-REM-061, R-REM-081)
**Facts.** Relying on a licensed surveyor's stakes, Homeowner builds a $120,000 garage that extends three feet onto Neighbor's unused side yard. The strip is worth $4,000. Neighbor learns of it at once and sues within a month.

**Analysis.**
- **Innocence.** Homeowner relied on a professional survey, so the encroachment was neither willful nor negligent.
- **Irreparable injury.** Neighbor loses an unused strip, with no special use shown.
- **Hardship.** Removal would cost far more than the strip's $4,000 value, so the hardship is greatly disproportionate.
- **Laches.** Neighbor sued promptly, so laches does not apply.

**Conclusion.** A California court likely denies removal and grants Homeowner an equitable easement on payment of the strip's value plus any diminution to Neighbor's lot. Had Homeowner built after a warning, removal would be ordered.

### WE-17 — Nuisance: partial injunction (R-REM-062, R-REM-024)
**Facts.** Buyer buys a house next to an outdoor amphitheater that has operated for 15 years. Concerts run until 1 a.m. and exceed local noise limits. Buyer sues.

**Analysis.**
- **Damages.** The nuisance is continuing, so Buyer recovers past annoyance and loss of use.
- **Injunction.** Damages are inadequate for a continuing nightly harm. Closing the venue would impose great hardship on it, its workers and the public, and Buyer came to the nuisance, which weighs against her but does not bar relief.
- **Tailored relief.** A court can limit hours and volume.

**Conclusion.** A partial injunction (for example, no amplified sound after 10 p.m., within noise limits) plus past damages is the likely result.

### WE-18 — Personal services and a negative injunction (R-REM-064)
**Facts.** Muralist signs a written two-year contract to paint exclusively for Hotel Chain, then quits after six months to paint for a rival chain.

**Analysis.**
- **Specific performance.** Ordering Muralist to paint would compel personal services: it is unenforceable and raises Thirteenth Amendment concerns.
- **Negative injunction.** Barring work for the rival during the remaining term is possible if the services are unique and the order leaves Muralist able to earn a living. In California, the contract must also meet § 3423(e)'s writing and minimum-compensation conditions.
- **Damages.** The cost of a replacement artist.

**Conclusion.** No specific performance. A negative injunction is possible if § 3423(e) is satisfied; otherwise damages only.

### WE-19 — Reformation, then replevin (R-REM-075, R-REM-041)
**Facts.** Buyer and Seller agree that a house sale includes two antique mirrors. The typed contract lists one. After closing, Seller removes the second mirror and offers it for sale online.

**Analysis.**
- **Reformation.** The parties had a prior agreement, and the writing omitted a term by mutual mistake in expression (or Seller's fraud). Clear and convincing proof (emails, the listing) supports reformation, and the parol evidence rule does not bar it.
- **Enforcement.** Once the contract is reformed, Buyer may seek specific performance or replevin of the unique mirror, plus a TRO against the online sale.
- **Defenses.** Seller's claim that Buyer should have read the contract fails: negligence does not bar reformation.

**Conclusion.** Reformation and recovery of the mirror are likely.

### WE-20 — Insurance rescission vs. reformation (R-REM-076, R-REM-085)
**Facts.** Applicant's agent mistakenly lists Shop's address in the wrong county on a liability-insurance application. Insurer issues the policy, learns of the error, keeps collecting premiums for a year, then rescinds after a claim.

**Analysis.**
- **Rescission.** Under Ins. Code §§ 331 and 359, even an unintentional material misstatement allows rescission. Materiality depends on whether the correct county would have changed the premium or Insurer's decision.
- **Defenses.** Insurer accepted premiums after learning the truth, which supports waiver or estoppel.
- **Insured's alternative.** Reformation of the address, to state what both parties intended to insure.

**Conclusion.** If Insurer would have charged the same premium, the misstatement is immaterial. Even if it was material, waiver likely bars rescission, and the policy may be reformed.

### WE-21 — Contempt (R-REM-078)
**Facts.** The court orders Developer to stop grading a hillside pending trial. Developer keeps grading for ten days.

**Analysis.**
- **Coercive civil contempt.** A daily fine until grading stops; Developer "holds the keys."
- **Compensatory civil contempt.** Payment of the plaintiffs' losses from the ten days.
- **Criminal contempt.** A fixed fine for the completed defiance, with criminal procedural protections (*Bagwell*). In California, statutory limits apply (CCP § 1218).

**Conclusion.** The court may impose all three kinds of sanction, each through its proper procedure.

## Defenses and declaratory relief

### WE-22 — Laches and estoppel (R-REM-081, R-REM-084, R-REM-090)
**Facts.** Owner watches Neighbor build a pool house that extends onto Owner's land, says nothing for a year, and then sues for removal and damages within the limitations period.

**Analysis.**
- **Laches.** Owner knew of the encroachment, delayed a year without excuse, and Neighbor was prejudiced by finishing construction.
- **Estoppel.** Owner's silence while knowing Neighbor was mistaken may also estop him from seeking removal.
- **Damages.** The damages claim is legal and was filed within the statute of limitations, so laches does not bar it.

**Conclusion.** Removal is likely barred; Owner recovers damages for the land taken.

### WE-23 — Unclean hands (R-REM-082)
**Facts.** Buyer sues for specific performance of a ranch contract. Evidence shows Buyer secretly paid Seller's broker to discourage other offers.

**Analysis.** Buyer's misconduct relates directly to the transaction he asks equity to enforce, so unclean hands bars equitable relief. Damages may remain, though the same fraud may give Seller a defense.

**Conclusion.** No specific performance.

### WE-24 — Declaratory judgment before breach (R-REM-091, R-REM-092)
**Facts.** Franchisee and Franchisor disagree whether a renewal clause lets Franchisee renew on the same terms. Renewal is due in four months, and Franchisor says it will treat any renewal notice as void.

**Analysis.** The parties have adverse positions on a concrete, current dispute over a written contract. Franchisor's statement makes the controversy real and ripe, and a declaration now avoids a breach later (28 U.S.C. § 2201; CCP § 1060).

**Conclusion.** Declaratory relief is likely available.
