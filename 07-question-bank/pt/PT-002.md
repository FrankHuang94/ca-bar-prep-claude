To all attorneys:

1. **Argument headings carry the argument.** Each major heading must be a complete sentence that states the conclusion we want the court to reach and the key facts that require it. ### Columbia Code of Civil Procedure

**§ 1420. Preliminary injunction: when granted.** A preliminary injunction may be granted at any time before judgment, on a verified complaint or on declarations showing sufficient grounds, in any of the following cases:
(a) when it appears that the plaintiff is entitled to the relief demanded, and all or part of that relief consists of restraining the commission or continuance of the act complained of;
(b) when it appears that the commission or continuance of an act during the litigation would produce waste, or great or irreparable injury, to a party; or
(c) when money damages would not afford adequate relief, or it would be extremely difficult to determine the amount of compensation that would afford adequate relief.

**§ 1422. Undertaking.** On granting a preliminary injunction, the court shall require the applicant to give an undertaking, in an amount the court fixes, to pay the enjoined party any damages that party sustains because of the injunction if the court finally decides that the applicant was not entitled to it.

### Columbia Civil Code

**§ 812. Easements created by transfer.** A transfer of real property passes all easements attached to it. It also creates, in favor of the transferred property, an easement to use other real property of the transferor in the same manner and to the same extent as that other property was obviously and permanently used by the transferor for the benefit of the transferred property at the time the transfer was agreed upon or completed.

### *Castellano v. Mill Creek Irrigation District* (Columbia Supreme Court, 2009)

For 31 years, Rafael Castellano and his predecessors irrigated a 200-acre alfalfa farm through a turnout (a gated outlet) on a canal now owned by the Mill Creek Irrigation District. In June 2007, in the middle of the irrigation season, the District welded the turnout shut, asserting that Castellano had no right to the water. Castellano sued to establish his right and moved for a preliminary injunction. The trial court ordered the District, pending trial, (1) to reopen the turnout and not to interfere with deliveries in the amounts historically received, and (2) to build, at its own expense, a new metering station at the turnout. The Court of Appeal affirmed. We affirm in part and reverse in part.

**The standard.** In deciding whether to issue a preliminary injunction under Code of Civil Procedure § 1420, the trial court weighs two interrelated factors: (1) the likelihood that the plaintiff will prevail on the merits at trial, and (2) the interim harm the plaintiff is likely to suffer if the injunction is denied, compared with the harm the defendant is likely to suffer if it is granted. The factors operate on a sliding scale. The greater the plaintiff's showing on one, the less it must show on the other. But the court may not issue an injunction unless there is at least some reasonable probability that the plaintiff will prevail. A preliminary injunction does not decide the merits. Its purpose is to preserve the court's ability to give effective relief at the end of the case.

**Interim harm.** The trial court found that without water, Castellano would lose his second and third cuttings of alfalfa and risk the loss of a stand that takes three years to re-establish. It found that the District's only harm was the loss of the use of water it had delivered to the turnout for three decades. That comparison was well within the court's discretion. The District argued that Castellano's losses could be measured in money. Some could be, but the loss of a living crop in mid-season, and of a stand that cannot be replaced for years, is the kind of injury § 1420(b) and (c) describe. The District also stressed the administrative burden of resuming deliveries. But the District shut the turnout knowing that Castellano claimed a right to the water. A defendant that changes the situation on the ground with knowledge of the plaintiff's claim cannot rely heavily on hardships of its own making.

**The status quo and mandatory relief.** The District contends that an order to reopen the turnout is a mandatory injunction, which a court should issue only on a clear showing that the plaintiff is entitled to it. A preliminary injunction ordinarily preserves the status quo, meaning the last actual, peaceable, uncontested state of affairs that preceded the controversy. Here that was the turnout open and water flowing, as it had for 31 years. A defendant cannot create a new status quo by its own unilateral act and then insist that undoing it is "mandatory" relief. An order that simply requires the defendant to undo its own recent act, and so restores the last peaceable status, is prohibitory in substance whatever its form, and the ordinary standard applies. Even if the order were treated as mandatory, Castellano's showing was clear.

The metering-station order is different. It did not restore anything. It required the District to build something that had never existed, at its own expense, before any adjudication. That is mandatory relief in substance as well as form. It required a clear showing of necessity, and the record contained none. We therefore reverse that part of the order. A preliminary injunction should go no further than needed to preserve the status quo pending trial.

### *Whitfield v. Olander* (Columbia Court of Appeal, 2012)

Carl Olander owned a 60-acre parcel. On its western 50 acres were a vineyard and a barn used for wine production. A dirt driveway ran from Hollins Road, a county road on the east, across the eastern 10 acres to the barn. In 2003 Olander sold the western 50 acres, with the vineyard and barn, to Diane Whitfield. The deed said nothing about access. The western parcel had frontage on Ridge Road, another public road, but that frontage lay at the top of a steep slope. An engineer testified that a driveway able to carry delivery and grape trucks from Ridge Road would need switchbacks and would cost about $300,000. Whitfield used the eastern driveway for eight years. In 2011, Olander's successor, who had bought the eastern 10 acres, blocked it. Whitfield sued, claiming (1) an easement implied from prior use over the driveway and (2) a similar easement over a footpath from the barn to a creek on the eastern parcel, which Olander had occasionally used for walks. The trial court rejected the driveway claim because Whitfield's parcel "has access to a public road," and granted the footpath claim. We reverse on both.

**Easements implied from prior use.** Civil Code § 812 codifies the common-law rule. When an owner conveys part of his land, an easement over the part he keeps is implied in favor of the part conveyed if (1) there was a separation of title; (2) before the separation, the owner used the retained part for the benefit of the conveyed part, and that use had been so long continued and so obvious as to show it was meant to be permanent; and (3) the easement is reasonably necessary to the beneficial enjoyment of the land conveyed. The claimant bears the burden of proving each element, and courts do not lightly imply easements that the parties did not write down. The question is what the parties intended at the time of the conveyance, which the law infers from the circumstances then existing: the nature and visibility of the use, the needs of the conveyed land, and what the grantee paid for. Evidence of the parties' later conduct may shed light on that intent, but it is the intent at the time of the conveyance that controls. The inference is stronger when, as here, the easement is claimed by the grantee. A grantor who wanted to keep his remaining land free of an obvious, existing use could have said so in his deed.

*Obvious and continuous use.* The driveway was a visible, worn road leading to the barn. A use need not be daily to be continuous. Use that is regularly repeated according to the needs of the property, such as seasonal use of a farm road at harvest, is continuous.

*Reasonable necessity.* The trial court confused this easement with an easement by necessity. An easement by necessity requires strict necessity: the land must have no other access at all, so any access to a public road defeats it. An easement by necessity also ends when the necessity ends. An easement implied from prior use requires only reasonable necessity. It is enough that the easement is reasonably necessary to the use and enjoyment of the land in the way it was being used when the land was conveyed. Alternative access does not defeat the claim if the alternative is substantially less suitable for that use, or could be made suitable only at a cost disproportionate to the benefit. Whitfield's Ridge Road frontage could not carry grape trucks to the barn without a $300,000 project. The driveway was reasonably necessary.

*The footpath.* The footpath was a faint trail used for occasional recreational walks. It was neither obvious nor permanent, and a walk to a creek is not reasonably necessary to the use of a vineyard. The trial court erred in finding an easement over it.

*Successors.* An easement created under § 812 is appurtenant to the conveyed land. It passes with that land and binds later owners of the servient land, at least those who take with notice of the visible use.

Reversed and remanded with directions to enter judgment for Whitfield on the driveway claim and for the defendant on the footpath claim.

### *Nakamura Nursery, Inc. v. Tamsen Holdings, LLC* (Columbia Court of Appeal, 2018)

Nakamura Nursery grows ornamental plants in greenhouses. For several years it reached its loading dock over a paved private lane on land owned by Tamsen Holdings, under a written license that allowed Tamsen to revoke it on 30 days' notice. Tamsen revoked the license and blocked the lane. Nakamura could still reach its loading dock by a paved public street, but the route added about 2.5 miles to each trip. Five months later, Nakamura sued, claiming an easement implied from prior use, and moved for a preliminary injunction. Its evidence of harm was a declaration estimating about $6,000 in added fuel and driver time over the remaining growing season. The trial court denied the motion. We affirm.

**Likelihood of success.** Nakamura's merits case was weak. Its use of the lane rested on a written, revocable license. A use that the parties expressly agreed was revocable does not show an intent that it be permanent.

**Interim harm.** Section 1420 allows a preliminary injunction where the harm would be great or irreparable, or where money damages would be inadequate or extremely difficult to calculate. We agree with Nakamura that disputes over rights in land often involve irreparable harm. Each parcel of land is unique, and the loss of the use of a particular route across land is often irreparable, because money cannot buy a substitute right of way that the servient owner refuses to sell. Likewise, the loss of perishable crops, or of established customer relationships and market position, may be irreparable when its extent is speculative or difficult to compute.

But the fact that a dispute concerns land does not make every harm irreparable. Nakamura's own evidence showed that the alternative public route was fully usable, and that the only consequence of the closure was a modest, readily calculated increase in operating costs. Damages can fully compensate a harm of that kind.

**Delay.** Nakamura waited five months after the lane was closed to seek relief and offered no explanation. Unexplained delay in seeking a preliminary injunction undercuts a claim that the harm is urgent or irreparable. Delay that is reasonably explained, as by good-faith efforts to resolve the dispute, does not carry the same weight.

With a weak merits showing and a modest, compensable harm, the sliding scale did not favor an injunction. The order denying the preliminary injunction is affirmed.

## Model Answer

**MEMORANDUM OF POINTS AND AUTHORITIES IN SUPPORT OF PLAINTIFF FERRANTE ORCHARDS, LLC'S MOTION FOR PRELIMINARY INJUNCTION**

### I. INTRODUCTION

For 48 years, every load of fruit from the 120-acre orchard now owned by Ferrante Orchards has left over the same gravel road across the neighboring Front Parcel. When Walter Pruett sold the orchard to Ferrante in 2014, that road passed with it as an easement implied from prior use under Civil Code § 812. Ridgecrest bought the Front Parcel in 2025 knowing about the road. It has now locked the road and plans to grade it into house lots on September 14, six days into Ferrante's harvest. The only other route crosses a bridge posted at 10 tons, which loaded harvest trucks cannot legally use. Without an injunction, Ferrante will lose most of a $1.15 million perishable crop and risk its place at its packing cooperative. With one, Ridgecrest need only leave a 2-acre strip alone until trial and unlock a gate during harvest. Ferrante Orchards asks the Court to restore the status quo that existed until June 15, 2026, pending trial.

### II. STATEMENT OF FACTS

**The orchard and its road.** Walter Pruett owned the 160-acre Pruett Ranch from 1976. In 1978 he built a graveled, 18-foot-wide ranch road (the "Orchard Road") from State Route 14 across the 40-acre Front Parcel to the orchard behind it. (Ybarra Decl. ¶ 2.) For 36 years it was the ranch's only route for hauling fruit, driven many times a day at harvest and used all year for tractors and crews. It was plainly visible from Route 14. (Id. ¶¶ 2–3.)

**The 2014 sale.** In October 2013, Lucia Ferrante toured the orchard as loaded fruit trucks came out on the Orchard Road. Mr. Pruett told her, "The road goes with the orchard. That's how the fruit gets out." (Ferrante Decl. ¶ 3.) In March 2014 he sold the 120-acre orchard to Ferrante Orchards for $2.1 million as a working orchard, by a deed silent on access. (Id. ¶¶ 2–3; Ex. A.) Ferrante has used the road every season since and re-graveled part of it in 2018. (Ferrante Decl. ¶ 4.)

**Bell Canyon Road.** The orchard's northeast corner touches Bell Canyon Road: 3.4 miles of single-lane dirt, grades of up to 16 percent, and a bridge posted at 10 tons since at least 2009. (Id. ¶ 5; Ybarra Decl. ¶ 4.) The County will not upgrade the bridge or issue overweight permits. (Reyes Decl. Ex. D.) A loaded harvest truck weighs about 26 tons. Neither Pruett Ranch nor Ferrante has ever hauled fruit that way. (Reyes Decl. ¶ 2; Ybarra Decl. ¶ 4; Ferrante Decl. ¶ 5.)

**Ridgecrest's purchase and the gate.** In September 2025, Ridgecrest's managing member saw Ferrante's bin trucks on the road and was told it was how the fruit got out. (Ferrante Decl. ¶ 7.) Ridgecrest's title report excepted rights in the "visible roadway," and the listing brochure said the road "provides access to neighboring orchard." (Compl. Exs. E, F.) Ridgecrest bought in November 2025. On June 15, 2026, it locked a gate across the road. (Ferrante Decl. ¶¶ 7–8.) Ferrante sought to negotiate at once. Ridgecrest offered only a different easement for $250,000, built at Ferrante's cost and available in mid-2027; its counsel ended talks on July 24. (Id. ¶ 9; Compl. Exs. G, I.) Ridgecrest will begin grading the road corridor on September 14. (Ferrante Decl. Ex. C; Compl. Ex. G.)

**The harvest.** Ferrante's harvest runs from about September 8 to October 30: about 2,600 bins worth about $1.15 million, picked by 46 contracted workers. (Ferrante Decl. ¶ 10.) The fruit must reach the Cooperative in Merrow within 24 hours of picking or be sold as juice at about one-fifth of the price. A member that delivers less than 80 percent of its commitment may lose its allocation to a four-year waiting list. (Compl. Ex. H.) Moving the crop in trucks small enough for the bridge would take about 650 trips, and the trucks and drivers are not available. (Reyes Decl. ¶¶ 3–4.)

### III. ARGUMENT

The Court weighs two interrelated factors: (1) the likelihood that Ferrante Orchards will prevail at trial, and (2) the interim harm to Ferrante if the injunction is denied, compared with the harm to Ridgecrest if it is granted. The greater the showing on one factor, the less is required on the other. (*Castellano v. Mill Creek Irrigation District*; Code Civ. Proc. § 1420.) Ferrante makes a strong showing on both.

#### A. FERRANTE ORCHARDS IS LIKELY TO PREVAIL BECAUSE THE ORCHARD ROAD WAS OBVIOUSLY AND PERMANENTLY USED TO SERVE THE ORCHARD FOR 36 YEARS BEFORE WALTER PRUETT SOLD IT, AND IT REMAINS THE ONLY ROUTE LOADED HARVEST TRUCKS CAN USE.

When an owner conveys part of his land, Civil Code § 812 creates an easement over the part he keeps "in the same manner and to the same extent" as it "was obviously and permanently used" for the conveyed part at the time of the transfer. Under *Whitfield v. Olander*, the claimant shows (1) separation of title, (2) a prior use so long continued and obvious as to show it was meant to be permanent, and (3) reasonable necessity. Each element is established.

**1. Walter Pruett's 2014 sale of the orchard severed common ownership of the two parcels.** Mr. Pruett owned both parcels from 1976 and in 2014 conveyed the orchard (Parcel 2) while keeping the Front Parcel. (Ferrante Decl. ¶ 2; Ex. A.) The deed's silence is exactly the situation § 812 addresses. Ferrante is the grantee, which makes the inference of an easement stronger: a grantor who wanted his retained land free of an obvious, existing road "could have said so in his deed." (*Whitfield*.)

**2. The Orchard Road was an obvious, permanent, year-round farm road serving the orchard when the orchard was sold.** Like the worn driveway in *Whitfield*, the Orchard Road is an 18-foot graveled road whose entrance and route were plainly visible from Route 14. Trucks were hauling fruit on it the day Ms. Ferrante first saw the orchard. (Ybarra Decl. ¶ 2; Ferrante Decl. ¶ 3.) It had served the orchard since 1978. Ridgecrest may stress that traffic outside harvest was light. But *Whitfield* holds that use "regularly repeated according to the needs of the property, such as seasonal use of a farm road at harvest, is continuous." This road was used every harvest for 36 years and all year round besides. (Ybarra Decl. ¶ 3.) It is the opposite of the "faint trail used for occasional recreational walks" rejected in *Whitfield*.

**3. The road is reasonably necessary because Bell Canyon Road cannot carry a commercial harvest.** Ridgecrest's lead argument, that Bell Canyon Road gives "alternative access," confuses two different easements. Public-road access defeats an easement by necessity, which Ferrante does not claim. It does not defeat an easement implied from prior use, which requires only that the easement be reasonably necessary to use the land "in the way it was being used when the land was conveyed." An alternative is no answer if it is "substantially less suitable for that use." (*Whitfield*.) In 2014 the orchard was a commercial operation shipping fruit by truck. Bell Canyon Road was not suitable for that use then and is not now. Its bridge has been posted at 10 tons since at least 2009, a loaded truck weighs 26 tons, and the County will not permit overweight vehicles or upgrade the bridge. (Ybarra Decl. ¶ 4; Reyes Decl. ¶ 2, Ex. D.) Whitfield's Ridge Road frontage was at least usable after a $300,000 project. Here no amount of money Ferrante could spend would make the County's bridge carry its trucks.

Ridgecrest's other points confirm the rule rather than defeat it:
- *The parcel map.* Ridgecrest says the County found that Parcel 2 had "legal access" by Bell Canyon Road. (Compl. Ex. I.) So did Whitfield's parcel, which fronted a public road. Whether a parcel has legal access is the question for an easement by necessity, not for reasonable necessity.
- *Occasional use of Bell Canyon Road.* Ferrante took a spray rig over Bell Canyon Road for a few days in 2015 and 2021, and it has used pickups there this summer. That shows only that the road can carry light vehicles in dry weather. Ferrante has never moved fruit over it, and Pruett Ranch never did either. (Ferrante Decl. ¶¶ 5, 8; Ybarra Decl. ¶ 4.)

**4. Walter Pruett's friendly 2015 note does not turn an easement created in 2014 into a revocable license.** Ridgecrest calls the Orchard Road a license because Mr. Pruett wrote in 2015 that Ferrante was "welcome to use the ranch road, just as you have been." (Ex. B.) But under *Whitfield*, the easement arises from the parties' intent "at the time of the conveyance." Later conduct may shed light on that intent, but "it is the intent at the time of the conveyance that controls." That intent is clear: Mr. Pruett told Ms. Ferrante that "the road goes with the orchard," and she paid $2.1 million for a working orchard whose fruit could leave no other way. (Ferrante Decl. ¶ 3.) The note, written by a neighbor more than a year later, reads as a courtesy: it acknowledges existing use ("just as you have been") and asks Ferrante's crews to drive slowly near the grandchildren. It grants and limits nothing. *Nakamura* is not to the contrary. There, the use began under a written license expressly revocable on 30 days' notice, which showed that the parties never intended a permanent right. Ferrante never asked for or signed any license, and Mr. Pruett never asked it to stop. (Ferrante Decl. ¶ 6.)

**5. The easement binds Ridgecrest, which bought with notice of the visible road.** An easement under § 812 is appurtenant, passes with the orchard, and binds later owners of the Front Parcel "who take with notice of the visible use." (*Whitfield*.) Ridgecrest had notice three times over: its managing member watched Ferrante's bin trucks on the road and was told it was how the fruit got out; its title report excepted rights in the "visible roadway"; and the listing brochure said the road "provides access to neighboring orchard." (Ferrante Decl. ¶ 7; Compl. Exs. E, F.)

Ferrante Orchards is therefore likely, not merely reasonably probable, to prevail.

#### B. THE BALANCE OF INTERIM HARM STRONGLY FAVORS AN INJUNCTION BECAUSE FERRANTE WILL OTHERWISE LOSE A $1.15 MILLION PERISHABLE HARVEST AND ITS COOPERATIVE ALLOCATION, WHILE RIDGECREST NEED ONLY DEFER WORK ON A 2-ACRE STRIP IT KNEW WAS BURDENED.

**1. Ferrante faces irreparable injury that money cannot measure or repair.** Section 1420(b) and (c) authorize an injunction where an act would cause "great or irreparable injury" or where damages would be inadequate or "extremely difficult to determine." Ferrante's harm qualifies on three grounds that *Nakamura* recognizes and *Castellano* applied.
- *Land and a unique route.* "The loss of the use of a particular route across land is often irreparable, because money cannot buy a substitute right of way that the servient owner refuses to sell." (*Nakamura*.) Ridgecrest's only offer is a different route that does not yet exist, that Ferrante must build, and that would come a year too late. (Ferrante Decl. ¶ 9.)
- *A perishable crop.* Like the mid-season alfalfa in *Castellano*, Ferrante's apples cannot wait. They must reach Merrow within 24 hours of picking, and apples left on the trees drop. (Compl. Ex. H; Ferrante Decl. ¶ 11.)
- *Market position that cannot be valued.* If Ferrante delivers less than 80 percent of its 2,500-bin commitment, the Cooperative may reassign its allocation to growers on a four-year waiting list. Its manager "cannot predict" what the Board would do. (Compl. Ex. H.) That loss of "established customer relationships and market position" is exactly the speculative, hard-to-compute harm *Nakamura* calls irreparable.

Ridgecrest's reliance on *Nakamura*'s holding is misplaced. Nakamura's alternative public route was "fully usable," and its only loss was "about $6,000" in fuel and driver time. Ferrante's alternative cannot be used at all for its harvest. Moving the crop in trucks small enough for the bridge would take about 650 trips with trucks and drivers that are not available during harvest, and even then the fruit would miss the 24-hour window on peak days. (Reyes Decl. ¶¶ 3–4.) The $210,000 figure is not the cost of a workable alternative. It is Mr. Reyes's estimate of what an impractical one would cost "even if it could be done."

**2. Ferrante acted promptly.** Ridgecrest says Ferrante waited six weeks. *Nakamura* criticized an *unexplained* five-month delay, and it expressly recognized that delay "reasonably explained, as by good-faith efforts to resolve the dispute, does not carry the same weight." Ferrante wrote to Ridgecrest the morning after the gate went up, met with it twice, asked it to keep the road open just through October 30, retained counsel four days after Ridgecrest's counsel ended talks, and is moving more than a month before harvest. (Ferrante Decl. ¶ 9; Compl. Ex. G.) Summer work could be done with pickups over Bell Canyon Road, so the irreparable harm begins with the harvest on September 8. (Ferrante Decl. ¶ 8.)

**3. Ridgecrest's claimed harms are modest, avoidable and of its own making.**
- *The narrow order does not stop Phase 2.* The road corridor is about 2 of Phase 2's 25 acres. (Ex. C.) Ridgecrest's own contractor priced re-sequencing Phase 2 around the road at about $85,000, a small fraction of the harm Ferrante faces. The $400,000 figure assumes that all Phase 2 grading is pushed into the rainy season, and the requested order does not require that.
- *The loan interest.* The $3,100-a-day interest accrues on Ridgecrest's whole construction loan whether or not the order issues. Ridgecrest does not show that leaving one strip untouched until trial would add to it, given that it can grade the rest of Phase 2.
- *Hardship of its own making.* Ridgecrest bought knowing of the road and of Ferrante's claim, then gated it and scheduled its destruction for harvest season. As in *Castellano*, a defendant that "changes the situation on the ground with knowledge of the plaintiff's claim cannot rely heavily on hardships of its own making."
- *Safety.* Ferrante will limit its use to 6:00 a.m.–8:00 p.m., provide flaggers through any active construction area, insure Ridgecrest for $2 million and maintain the road. (Ferrante Decl. ¶ 13.)
- *Protection if Ferrante loses.* Ridgecrest is protected by the undertaking the Court will require under § 1422, which Ferrante is prepared to post in whatever amount the Court sets. (Id.)

**4. On the sliding scale, both factors point the same way.** Unlike *Nakamura*, which combined a weak merits case with a modest, compensable harm, this case combines a strong merits case with severe, irreparable and lopsided harm. *Castellano* requires no more.

#### C. THE REQUESTED ORDER IS PROPER IN SCOPE BECAUSE IT ONLY RESTORES THE OPEN ROAD THAT EXISTED FOR 48 YEARS UNTIL RIDGECREST LOCKED THE GATE ON JUNE 15, 2026, AND IT ASKS RIDGECREST TO BUILD NOTHING.

A preliminary injunction preserves "the last actual, peaceable, uncontested state of affairs that preceded the controversy." (*Castellano*.) Here that state of affairs is the Orchard Road open and in use, as it was from 1978 through Ferrante's last trip on June 12, 2026. (Ybarra Decl. ¶ 2; Ferrante Decl. ¶ 8.) Paragraphs (1) and (2) of the proposed order are prohibitory on their face: Ridgecrest must refrain from obstructing the road and from grading the corridor.

Ridgecrest calls paragraph (3), unlocking the gate, a disfavored mandatory injunction. *Castellano* rejects that argument. "A defendant cannot create a new status quo by its own unilateral act and then insist that undoing it is 'mandatory' relief." An order that "simply requires the defendant to undo its own recent act" is "prohibitory in substance whatever its form." Removing a lock that Ridgecrest added on June 15 is the counterpart of reopening the turnout the District welded shut in *Castellano*. It is not like the metering station *Castellano* struck down, which required the defendant to build "something that had never existed." Ferrante does not ask Ridgecrest to build anything, to change its subdivision, or to stop grading the other 23 acres of Phase 2. Paragraph (3) also gives Ridgecrest the choice of removing the lock or providing keys, and limits the unlocked hours to the harvest day. Even if paragraph (3) were treated as mandatory, the clear showing made in Parts A and B would justify it, as the Supreme Court held in *Castellano*.

### IV. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Ferrante Orchards respectfully requests that the Court issue a preliminary injunction ordering that, pending trial, Ridgecrest Development Partners, LLC, and its agents and contractors:
1. shall not obstruct or interfere with Ferrante Orchards' use of the Orchard Road for agricultural purposes;
2. shall not grade, excavate or build within the 30-foot Orchard Road corridor shown on Exhibit C; and
3. shall, within two days of the order, remove the lock from the Route 14 gate, or deliver keys to Ferrante Orchards and keep the gate unlocked from 6:00 a.m. to 8:00 p.m. while harvest continues;

on the conditions that Ferrante Orchards use the road only between 6:00 a.m. and 8:00 p.m., provide a flagger whenever its trucks pass through an active construction area, maintain $2 million in liability insurance naming Ridgecrest as an additional insured, and maintain the road surface at its own expense; and upon Ferrante Orchards' posting of an undertaking in the amount the Court sets under Code of Civil Procedure § 1422.
