### Columbia Civil Code (selected sections)

**§ 3301. Liquidated damages.**
(a) The parties to a contract may agree in the contract on an amount, or a formula for determining an amount, payable as damages for a breach of the contract.
(b) Except as provided in subdivision (c), a provision described in subdivision (a) is valid unless the party seeking to invalidate it establishes that the provision was unreasonable under the circumstances existing at the time the contract was made.
(c) In a contract for the purchase or rental of goods or services primarily for personal, family or household purposes, a provision described in subdivision (a) is void unless, from the nature of the case, it would be impracticable or extremely difficult to fix the actual damage.
(d) A provision that is invalid under this section is unenforceable, and the injured party may recover its actual damages as otherwise provided by law. The name the parties give a provision, such as "penalty," "liquidated damages," "credit" or "fee," does not determine whether it is valid.

**§ 3302. Contracts exempting a party from responsibility.**
Every provision of a contract that has for its object, directly or indirectly, to exempt a party from responsibility for its own fraud, for willful injury to the person or property of another, or for its own gross negligence, is against the policy of this State and is void.

### *Marrowstone Logistics, Inc. v. Pallas Data Systems, Inc.* (Columbia Supreme Court, 2015)

Marrowstone Logistics, Inc., a freight brokerage with annual revenue of about $40 million, licensed a cloud-based load-tracking platform from Pallas Data Systems, Inc. for $9,000 a month. The parties used Pallas's standard form. Marrowstone's counsel negotiated changes to the price and the term but did not change Section 7, which read:

> IN NO EVENT SHALL PALLAS BE LIABLE FOR ANY LOST PROFITS, LOST DATA OR CONSEQUENTIAL DAMAGES, OR FOR ANY DAMAGES WHATSOEVER IN EXCESS OF ONE MONTH'S FEES, REGARDLESS OF THE FORM OF ACTION OR THE CAUSE, INCLUDING PALLAS'S NEGLIGENCE, GROSS NEGLIGENCE OR INTENTIONAL MISCONDUCT.

Section 14 of the contract provided that an invalid provision "shall be deemed modified to the minimum extent necessary to make it enforceable."

Over five months in 2012, Pallas's outside security consultant warned Pallas in writing three times that a server holding customer data was running software with a publicly known, critical vulnerability. Pallas's head of engineering deferred the patch so that his staff could finish a product launch. Attackers then used the vulnerability to encrypt Marrowstone's records. Marrowstone spent $310,000 restoring and re-creating its data and claims $1.2 million in lost profits. The trial court granted summary judgment limiting Pallas's liability to $9,000, and the Court of Appeal affirmed. We reverse.

**Limitations of liability generally.** Commercial parties of comparable bargaining strength may allocate the risk of loss between themselves, and a limitation of liability is one way they do so. The price a vendor charges reflects the risks it accepts, and courts should be slow to give a customer a bargain it did not pay for. Section 3302 does not prevent a party from limiting, or even excluding, its liability for breach of contract or for ordinary negligence. A limitation is not invalid merely because the amount it allows is small compared with the loss that later occurs.

**What § 3302 forbids.** Section 3302 voids every provision that has for its object, "directly or indirectly," to exempt a party from responsibility for its own fraud, willful injury or gross negligence. A cap that limits recovery to a fraction of the loss exempts the party from responsibility for the rest just as surely as a release does, so the statute reaches limitations as well as total exclusions. A provision has the forbidden object when, by its express terms, or by words such as "regardless of the cause" that necessarily include them, it purports to limit a party's liability for its own fraud, willful injury or gross negligence. Section 7 did so expressly.

Gross negligence is either a want of even scant care or an extreme departure from the ordinary standard of conduct. Ordinary carelessness, even costly carelessness, is not enough. But a conscious decision to leave a known and serious risk unremedied, after repeated warnings, for reasons of convenience, may be found to be an extreme departure. On this record a jury could so find. A party that has identified a risk and is carrying out a reasonable plan to address it stands differently from one that, like Pallas, chose to do nothing.

**The remedy.** Pallas asks us, in reliance on Section 14, to enforce Section 7 to the extent it could lawfully have been written: as a cap on liability for ordinary negligence and breach of contract. We decline. Section 3302 declares such a provision void, not voidable to the extent of its excess. If courts trimmed every overreaching exculpatory clause back to its lawful limit, a drafter would lose nothing by overreaching, and the statute would lose its force. Parties may agree that a court should sever an invalid provision and enforce the rest of their contract, but they cannot by contract direct a court to rewrite a provision that the Legislature has declared void. Section 7 in its entirety, including its cap and its exclusion of lost profits and lost data, is unenforceable.

Nothing in this opinion casts doubt on a limitation that expressly excepts claims arising from a party's fraud, willful misconduct or gross negligence. Such a provision does not have the object § 3302 forbids, and it is enforceable according to its terms as to all other claims.

**Guidance on remand.** Because Section 7 is void, we need not decide whether its exclusion of "lost data" would have reached Marrowstone's restoration costs. For the guidance of the trial court and of drafters, we note that clauses limiting or excluding liability are strictly construed against the party that drafted them, and any ambiguity is resolved against the limitation. The cost of restoring or re-creating data that a vendor promised to safeguard is ordinarily a direct loss, flowing naturally from the breach, not a consequential one. Profits lost on business the customer could not conduct are consequential. Parties who want a different allocation must say so clearly.

Reversed and remanded for trial without regard to Section 7.

*Justice Hale, concurring in part and dissenting in part:* I agree that a jury could find gross negligence. I would hold, however, that Section 14 permits a court to enforce Section 7 as a cap on liability for ordinary negligence, which is what sophisticated parties would have agreed to had they known of the defect.

### *Ostrander Floral Co. v. Vireo Hosting, Inc.* (Columbia Court of Appeal, 2019)

Ostrander Floral Co. sells flowers online and earns much of its revenue in the days before holidays. Vireo Hosting, Inc. hosted Ostrander's website for $12,000 a month. The hosting agreement committed Vireo to 99.9% monthly availability and provided a credit of 5% of the monthly fee for each full hour of unavailability beyond that commitment, up to 30% of the monthly fee. During negotiations, Ostrander's counsel redlined the service-level schedule and obtained an increase in the monthly cap from 15% to 30%. The agreement provided:

> Service Credits, together with Customer's right to terminate under Section 8.4, are Customer's sole and exclusive remedy for any failure to meet the Availability Commitment, except a failure caused by Vireo's gross negligence or willful misconduct. The parties agree that Service Credits are a reasonable estimate of Customer's damages from unavailability, which would be difficult to determine.

Section 8.4 allowed Ostrander to terminate, and to recover prepaid fees, if availability fell below 99% in any three months within a six-month period.

On February 13, 2017, a regional power failure cut both of the utility feeds to Vireo's data center, and its backup generator failed to start. Ostrander's site was down for 14 hours. Vireo's maintenance schedule called for a load test of the generator every quarter. Because the responsible technician was on leave, the last test before the outage had been five months earlier, two months late. The generator had passed every earlier test. Ostrander claims $600,000 in lost sales. Vireo issued the maximum credit of $3,600. The trial court granted summary judgment to Vireo. We affirm.

**The credits as stipulated damages.** Under § 3301(b), Ostrander bears the burden of proving that the credit provision was unreasonable under the circumstances existing when the contract was made. Reasonableness is judged at that time, not with hindsight. A stipulated sum is unreasonable when it bears no reasonable relationship to the range of harm the parties could have anticipated when they contracted.

The rule against penalties protects the party who must pay a stipulated sum. When the sum is smaller than the anticipated loss, no one is punished. The provision operates instead as an agreed limitation of the injured party's remedy, and commercial parties may agree to such a limitation for breach of contract and ordinary negligence (*Marrowstone Logistics, Inc. v. Pallas Data Systems, Inc.*). In deciding whether such a provision is reasonable, we consider: (1) whether, when the parties contracted, the harm from a breach was uncertain and likely to vary; (2) whether the remedy increases with the seriousness of the breach; (3) whether the provision was negotiated by sophisticated parties; and (4) whether the injured party keeps a meaningful remedy for serious or repeated failures.

Each factor favors Vireo. When the parties contracted, the cost of an outage to Ostrander depended on when it happened, and no one could predict that. The credits increase with each hour of unavailability. Ostrander's counsel negotiated the schedule and doubled the cap. And Section 8.4 gave Ostrander a way out, with a refund, if failures became chronic. That Ostrander's loss turned out to be many times the credit is the kind of hindsight § 3301(b) excludes.

The parties' recital that the credits are "a reasonable estimate" of Ostrander's damages does not end the inquiry, as Vireo contends, nor is it meaningless boilerplate, as Ostrander contends. A recital is evidence of what the parties contemplated when they contracted, and we give this one weight because counsel negotiated around it. But a court looks to the substance of the provision; the name or description the parties give it does not decide its validity (§ 3301(d)). Similarly, where a contract makes credits cumulative with other remedies, the credits are not a limitation at all, and a customer may recover both the credits and its proven damages.

We would view differently a credit so small, or capped so low, that it bore no relation to any harm the parties could have anticipated and left the customer without a meaningful remedy for a prolonged or repeated failure. Such a provision would not be an estimate of anything. It would be an exemption dressed as a remedy, and if it reached failures caused by a party's gross negligence, its validity would turn on § 3302.

**Section 3302.** The sole-remedy clause expressly excepts failures caused by Vireo's gross negligence or willful misconduct, so it does not have the object § 3302 forbids (*Marrowstone*). Nor does the record permit a finding of gross negligence. A generator test two months late because of a staff absence, at a facility with redundant power feeds and a generator that had passed every earlier test, is at most ordinary negligence. It is far from Pallas's conscious decision to ignore repeated warnings in *Marrowstone*.

Affirmed.

### *Brannock Systems Group, Inc. v. Feld Valley Credit Union* (Columbia Court of Appeal, 2021)

Brannock Systems Group, Inc., an information-technology consulting firm with about 140 employees, managed Feld Valley Credit Union's computer network from 2016 to 2019 and placed three engineers on site full time. Section 9 of the parties' contract provided:

> Customer shall not, during the term of this Agreement and for two years after its termination, directly or indirectly hire, employ or engage any person who is or was employed by Brannock at any time during the term. If Customer breaches this Section, Customer shall pay Brannock, as liquidated damages and not as a penalty, a placement fee equal to 100% of the individual's annual compensation at Brannock.

Brannock did not tell its employees about Section 9.

The contract ended in June 2019. In October 2019, Feld Valley's chief information officer emailed Maria Ortega, a Brannock network engineer who had worked on site at Feld Valley for two years, and invited her to apply for a newly created network manager position. She applied and was hired. In February 2020, Feld Valley posted a help-desk position on public job boards. Kevin Dunn, a Brannock help-desk technician who had never worked on the Feld Valley account, applied and was hired. Brannock sued for placement fees of $118,000 (Ortega) and $56,000 (Dunn). The trial court awarded both. We affirm in part and reverse in part.

**Section 4410 applies.** A contract between two businesses that limits where a third person may work restrains that person from engaging in a lawful profession. That the employee did not sign the contract, and may not even know of it, makes the restraint more troubling, not less. Brannock's reliance on § 4411 is misplaced: that section permits restraints by persons who sell the goodwill of, or their ownership interest in, a business. Feld Valley bought services, not a business.

**The test.** Section 4410 voids a contract only "to that extent," that is, to the extent it restrains someone from engaging in a lawful profession. The question is whether the provision, as applied, operates on an employee's freedom to work, or instead regulates the conduct of the contracting business in a way reasonably necessary to protect a legitimate interest of the other party. A customer's promise not to target and recruit the vendor's staff assigned to its account, for a limited period, regulates the customer's conduct. The employees remain free to seek work with the customer on their own initiative or in answer to its general advertising. A promise not to hire anyone who works for the vendor operates directly on the employee. It closes a door to her that she may not know is closed.

We therefore consider: (1) whether the provision bars hiring or only active solicitation by the customer; (2) whether it is limited to employees who worked on the parties' relationship; (3) whether its duration is reasonably related to the interest it protects; (4) whether it leaves employees free to apply on their own initiative or in response to general advertising; and (5) whether it protects a legitimate interest beyond freedom from competition for labor, such as the vendor's investment in training staff it places on the customer's premises in reliance on the relationship, and the vendor's ability to perform its contracts with others.

**Application.** As applied to Mr. Dunn, Section 9 is void. He never worked on the Feld Valley account, and he answered a public posting. Barring his hiring protected no interest of Brannock's except freedom from competition for his labor.

As applied to Ms. Ortega, Section 9 is enforceable. Brannock placed her on Feld Valley's premises for two years in reliance on the contract, and Feld Valley's access to her came from that relationship. Feld Valley's CIO initiated the contact. Brannock's own witness testified that the risk of losing trained staff to a customer lasts about a year after a project ends, so a two-year tail is longer than the interest requires. But the solicitation of Ms. Ortega four months after termination falls within any reasonable period. Unlike § 3302, which declares a forbidden exemption void (*Marrowstone Logistics, Inc. v. Pallas Data Systems, Inc.*), § 4410 voids a contract only "to that extent," so a court may enforce the lawful core of an overbroad restraint, as we do here. A drafter who writes a broad no-hire clause nevertheless takes the risk that a court will find no lawful core, and should not expect a court to write a narrower clause for it.

**The placement fee.** Under § 3301(b), Feld Valley bears the burden of proving that the fee was unreasonable when the contract was made, and it has carried that burden. Brannock's chief financial officer testified that, when the contract was signed, recruiters charged 20–30% of first-year salary and that a replacement needed about three months of training; Brannock's internal estimate of replacement cost was 35–45% of annual compensation. A fee of 100% of compensation, two to three times the loss the parties could reasonably have anticipated, applied uniformly to a senior engineer and a help-desk technician, is not a reasonable estimate. It is a penalty, whatever the parties called it (§ 3301(d)). A fee calibrated to the vendor's reasonably anticipated cost of recruiting and training a replacement, such as a percentage of the departing employee's salary within the range of those costs, would likely be upheld even if it proved higher than the actual loss in a particular case.

The judgment is reversed as to Mr. Dunn. As to Ms. Ortega, the finding of liability is affirmed, the award of the placement fee is reversed, and the matter is remanded for proof of Brannock's actual damages.

**The numbers are judgment calls.** Give full credit for any figure the memo ties to the Library and the File. Do not require the model's numbers.
- *Credit cap:* a monthly cap of about 15–30%, with tiers that climb with Downtime. Thirty percent is the cap *Ostrander* upheld; a lower cap needs a reason, and a higher one moves further from Ms. Okafor's 5% than the Library requires. A cap of 10% or less, or per-hour, uncapped or cumulative credits, does not earn full credit (Ms. Okafor ruled out per-hour credits; H&P calls low caps "worthless").
- *Data Security Cap:* $1,440,000 to $2,000,000 (H&P's 3x floor to Ms. Okafor's insurance ceiling).
- *Liquidated damages:* about 50–65% of base salary, or another formula calibrated to recruiting and training costs (Ms. Whitcomb's $70,000–$80,000 is about 53–61% of the $132,000 average). A flat sum for every role, or 100% of salary or more, does not earn full credit.
- *Tail:* 6–12 months after the Term (*Brannock*'s evidence put the interest at about a year). A Restricted Period that starts at Go-Live earns full credit only if another clause covers the January–June 2027 on-site work.

**Length note.** This model runs to about 3,250 words: about 1,300 for the provisions and 1,950 for the memo. An answer scoring 70 or more can be about 2,300–2,600 words. Keep each provision tight, and spend the words on the memo's "departures" and "enforceability" paragraphs, where most of the points are.

**Common mistakes**
1. Drafting the cap with "no carve-outs," as Ms. Okafor asked, or relying on the severability clause to save it. *Marrowstone* voids the whole clause and rejects the "deemed modified" argument.
2. Writing a no-hire clause, or a flat $150,000 fee. *Brannock* treats both as unenforceable, and Lumenfield's own HR numbers would prove the penalty.
3. Making the credits the sole remedy for "anything," or cumulative with damages as H&P proposed. Either loses the protection the client wants.
4. Taking instructions from the VP Sales (uncapped data liability, 10% per hour) when the task memo gives Ms. Okafor final authority.
5. Starting the non-solicitation period with the Term (Go-Live), which leaves the January–June 2027 on-site engineers unprotected.
6. Redrafting § 1, § 10 or payment terms, or adding the training promise. All are expressly excluded.
