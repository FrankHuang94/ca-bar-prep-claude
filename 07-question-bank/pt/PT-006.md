> **Study note (closed universe):** The statutes, jury instructions, cases and ethics rule in the Library are fictional Columbia law written for this PT. Columbia's burglary statute requires entry *without consent*. California's burglary statute and common-law burglary are different (R-CRM-050, R-CRM-051). The scope of the claim-of-right defense, especially for property taken because of a debt, varies by jurisdiction; use the Library's version inside this PT only (compare R-CRM-044 and R-CRM-010). Columbia Rule 3.4(g) is modelled on the broader ABA formulation. California's rule 3.4(g) is worded more narrowly (R-PRF-066). The rule against commenting on a defendant's silence is general (R-CRM-101).

**OFFICE OF THE PUBLIC DEFENDER, RIDGEMONT COUNTY**
300 Courthouse Plaza, Ridgemont, Columbia

**MEMORANDUM**

**TO:** Applicant
**FROM:** Malcolm Tran, Deputy Public Defender
**DATE:** October 22, 2026
**RE:** *People v. Eli Navarro*, No. CR-26-0418: closing argument

Our client, Eli Navarro, is on trial for **burglary** (Count 1, Columbia Penal Code § 2201) and **theft** of a pressure washer (Count 2, § 2301). Both charges arise from his entry into Grant Holloway's detached garage on September 12, 2026. The evidence closed this afternoon. Closing arguments begin tomorrow at 9:00 a.m. The prosecutor, Deputy District Attorney Lauren Pike, argues first, I follow, and she has the last word in rebuttal. **I will not be able to answer her rebuttal, so my argument must anticipate it.**

Please draft my **closing argument to the jury**:

- Write it as a **script I will speak**, about 20 minutes (roughly 2,500 words). Use short sentences and plain words, with clear signposts. Put record citations in parentheses, for example (Tr. 24) or (Ex. 3), so that I can check them. I will drop them when I speak.
- **Organize it around the court's instructions** in the Library. Open with a theme. Explain the burden of proof, and how CCJI 106 and CCJI 1863 apply here. Argue each **contested element** of each count. **Confront the strongest evidence against us**: name it and answer it. End by asking for a specific verdict on each count.
- Mr. Navarro has authorized me to acknowledge that he **entered the garage and took the pressure washer**. Those facts are not disputed. **Do not concede anything else.** Do not argue for a lesser offense; none will be instructed.

**Ethical limits.** Follow Columbia Rule of Professional Conduct 3.4(g) and *People v. Lindgren* (both in the Library):
- **No personal opinion** about guilt, innocence or any witness's credibility: no "I believe," "I think" or "I know." Argue from the evidence.
- **Only the evidence admitted at trial**, and reasonable inferences from it. The investigator's memorandum (Document 4) and Mr. Navarro's note (Document 5) are **not evidence**. Say nothing about Karen Holloway, the small-claims judgments the court excluded, or Mr. Navarro's record, family or business.
- **No reference to punishment, and no appeal to sympathy.**
- **Mr. Navarro did not testify.** You may remind the jury of CCJI 115 in one or two sentences. Do not explain his decision or suggest reasons for it.
- **Do not cite cases to the jury.** The Library cases explain the instructions and the limits on argument. The jury hears only the instructions, which you may quote.

## Library

### Columbia Penal Code (selected sections)

**§ 2201. Burglary.** Every person who enters any building or structure without the consent of its owner or lawful occupant, with intent to commit theft or any felony inside, is guilty of burglary. "Building" includes a garage, whether or not it is attached to a dwelling.

**§ 2301. Theft.** Every person who takes and carries away the personal property of another, without the owner's consent, with intent to deprive the owner of it permanently, is guilty of theft.

### Columbia Criminal Jury Instructions (CCJI): instructions the court will give

**CCJI 100. Presumption of innocence; burden of proof; reasonable doubt.** The defendant is presumed innocent. This presumption requires the People to prove each element of each charge beyond a reasonable doubt. The defendant does not have to prove anything. Proof beyond a reasonable doubt is proof that leaves you firmly convinced that the charge is true. The People do not have to remove every conceivable doubt. But if, after considering all the evidence, you are not firmly convinced that the defendant is guilty of a charge, you must find him not guilty of that charge.

**CCJI 104. Evidence.** You must decide what the facts are using only the evidence admitted in this trial: the sworn testimony of witnesses, the exhibits admitted, and any facts to which the parties agreed. Nothing that the attorneys say is evidence. If I told you to disregard testimony, you must not consider it for any purpose.

**CCJI 106. Circumstantial evidence.** A fact, including a person's intent, may be proved by circumstantial evidence. Before you may rely on circumstantial evidence to conclude that a fact essential to guilt has been proved, you must be convinced that the People have proved that fact beyond a reasonable doubt. If you can draw two or more reasonable conclusions from the circumstantial evidence, and one of those reasonable conclusions points to innocence while another points to guilt, you must accept the one that points to innocence. But if only one conclusion is reasonable, you must accept it and reject any that are unreasonable.

**CCJI 110. Believability of witnesses.** You alone judge the believability of the witnesses. In doing so, you may consider anything that tends to show whether testimony is accurate, including: how well the witness could see, hear or otherwise perceive the things described; how well the witness was able to remember and describe what happened; whether the witness had any bias, interest or reason to testify in a particular way; whether the witness made an earlier statement that is consistent or inconsistent with the testimony; and whether other evidence proved or disproved the testimony. If you find that a witness's earlier statement is inconsistent with his testimony, you may consider the earlier statement both to evaluate his testimony and as evidence that the earlier statement was true.

**CCJI 115. Defendant's right not to testify.** The defendant has an absolute constitutional right not to testify. He may rely on the state of the evidence and argue that the People have failed to prove the charges beyond a reasonable doubt. Do not consider, for any reason at all, the fact that the defendant did not testify. Do not discuss that fact during your deliberations or let it influence your decision in any way.

**CCJI 120. Defendant's statements.** You have heard evidence that the defendant made statements out of court. You must decide whether he made any such statement, in whole or in part, and what it meant. Consider with caution any statement made by the defendant tending to show his guilt unless the statement was written or otherwise recorded.

**CCJI 130. Punishment; sympathy.** You must not consider or discuss punishment in reaching your verdict. You must not let bias, sympathy, prejudice or public opinion influence your decision.

**CCJI 1700. Burglary (Penal Code § 2201).** To prove that the defendant is guilty of burglary, the People must prove that:
1. The defendant entered a building;
2. He entered without the consent of the owner or lawful occupant; and
3. When he entered the building, he intended to commit theft inside.

A detached garage is a building. Consent may be given by words or by conduct. Consent given for a limited purpose extends only to entries within that purpose. Consent that the owner withdrew, by words or conduct communicated to the defendant before the entry, is no longer consent.

The People must prove that the defendant intended to commit theft *at the time he entered*. If you find that the defendant formed that intent only after he entered, he is not guilty of burglary. If the defendant entered intending only to take property that he believed in good faith he had a right to take, as explained in Instruction 1863, he did not enter with intent to commit theft. The People do not have to prove that the defendant actually committed theft.

**CCJI 1800. Theft (Penal Code § 2301).** To prove that the defendant is guilty of theft, the People must prove that:
1. The defendant took possession of property owned by someone else;
2. He took the property without the owner's consent;
3. When he took the property, he intended to deprive the owner of it permanently; and
4. He moved the property, even a small distance, and kept it for any period of time, however brief.

A person intends to deprive the owner of property permanently if he intends to keep it permanently, to keep it for so long that the owner would lose a major portion of its value or enjoyment, or to return it only if the owner pays a reward, ransom or other compensation.

**CCJI 1863. Claim of right.** If the defendant took property under a good-faith claim of right, he did not have the intent required for theft. The defendant acted under a claim of right if:
1. When he took the property, he actually believed in good faith that he had a right to that specific property; and
2. He did not try to conceal the taking.

A belief may be held in good faith even if it is mistaken or unreasonable. But if the defendant was aware of facts that made his belief completely unreasonable, you may conclude that the belief was not held in good faith.

The claim-of-right defense does not apply if the defendant took the property to collect, satisfy or secure a debt, **unless** he believed in good faith that the owner had agreed to give him that specific property.

The People must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant did not act under a claim of right. If the People have not met this burden, you must find the defendant not guilty of theft.

### *People v. Ashworth* (Columbia Supreme Court, 2016)

Martin Ashworth moved out of an apartment he had shared with Reed Lowry, claiming that Lowry owed him $900 in back rent. Two weeks later, while Lowry was at work, Ashworth used a key he had kept to enter the apartment. He removed Lowry's television, carried it out through the building's rear service entrance, and told no one. When police located the television at a pawnshop, Ashworth said that Lowry "owed me, so I took what was fair." There was no evidence that Lowry had ever offered Ashworth the television or anything else. A jury convicted Ashworth of theft. He argues that the trial court should have instructed on claim of right. We affirm.

**The defense.** A person who takes property in the good-faith belief that it is his own does not intend to steal, because he does not intend to deprive *another* of property. The belief must be actually and honestly held. It need not be reasonable. But the more unreasonable the belief, the less likely a jury is to find that it was actually held, and a defendant who knows facts that make the belief completely unreasonable does not hold it in good faith.

**Debts.** The defense protects a claim to *specific property*. It does not protect self-help debt collection. A creditor who picks out some item of the debtor's property and takes it to satisfy or to secure the debt does not claim the item as his own. He claims money, and he has decided for himself to take the debtor's property in place of it. Extending the defense to such takings would license private seizures whenever one person believes another owes him money. The line is different when the defendant believes that the owner agreed to give him that particular item, including as payment of a debt. Such a defendant claims the item itself, and the defense is available if his belief is held in good faith. The question is not whether the owner in fact agreed. It is whether the defendant actually believed that he had.

**Evidence of good faith.** Juries commonly look at how the property was taken. An open taking, prompt disclosure to the owner, taking only the item claimed, and ready return when the dispute surfaced tend to show good faith. Concealment, taking more than the item claimed, sale of the property, and statements showing that the defendant knew the property was not his tend to show the opposite. Words that make the property's return depend on payment are strong evidence that the defendant regarded the property as security for a debt rather than as his own. They must be weighed with the rest of the evidence.

Ashworth claimed no agreement by Lowry to give him the television. His own statement showed that he took it to collect a debt. He removed it secretly and sold it. No reasonable jury could have found a claim of right, so the court did not err in refusing the instruction.

### *People v. Corrigan* (Columbia Court of Appeal, 2019)

Liam Corrigan and Tessa Byrne lived together until Byrne ended the relationship and Corrigan moved out. Byrne texted him, "Your PlayStation is still here. I'll box it up. Don't come over." Corrigan still had a key. Three days later, while Byrne was out, he used it to enter the apartment. He took the game console and also Byrne's laptop. He later told police that he went "to get my PlayStation" and "grabbed the laptop because she owes me for the TV I bought." He was convicted of burglary and theft. The trial court refused his request to instruct the jury on claim of right as to the burglary count. We reverse the burglary conviction and affirm the theft conviction.

**Intent at the time of entry.** Burglary requires intent to commit theft or a felony at the moment of entry. Intent formed after entry is not enough. Intent is rarely proved directly. A jury may infer it from the circumstances, including the defendant's statements before the entry, the time and manner of entry, any tools or bags brought for carrying property, the defendant's conduct inside, and what he took.

**Claim of right and burglary.** Because burglary requires intent to commit *theft*, a defendant who enters intending only to take property he believes in good faith is his own does not enter with the required intent. That remains true even if, once inside, he decides to take something else. That later decision may be theft, but it does not relate back to make the entry a burglary. Corrigan's statement that he went "to get my PlayStation" was substantial evidence that his purpose at entry was to recover his own property. The jury should have been told that such a purpose is not intent to commit theft. On this record, a properly instructed jury could have found that the decision to take the laptop came only after Corrigan entered. The refusal was prejudicial.

By contrast, the claim of right did not extend to the laptop. Corrigan did not suggest that Byrne had agreed to give it to him. He took it because "she owes me," which is debt collection (*People v. Ashworth*). The theft conviction stands.

**Consent.** Corrigan argued that he had Byrne's consent because she never asked for the key back. Whether consent existed, and whether it was withdrawn, is a question for the jury. A key or code given for a limited purpose is evidence of consent only for entries within that purpose. An owner's message may withdraw consent, but the jury decides what a message, read in context, actually communicated. On retrial, the jury may weigh Byrne's "Don't come over" against the key and against her own statement that the console was still there.

---

### *People v. Lindgren* (Columbia Supreme Court, 2020)

Dale Lindgren was convicted of assault after a trial in which both sides' closing arguments drew objections. The prosecutor told the jury, "I've been doing this for fifteen years, and I know a liar when I see one. The defendant's brother is lying." She described a 911 call that the court had excluded. And she said, "If he really acted in self-defense, he would have gotten up there and told you so." Defense counsel told the jury, "I believe in my client's innocence," and asked jurors to "think about what a felony conviction would do to his children." We reverse because of the prosecutor's misconduct. We also use this case to restate the limits that bind **every** lawyer in closing argument.

1. **Latitude.** Counsel may argue vigorously, summarize the evidence, draw reasonable inferences from it, and urge the jury to reject testimony. Counsel may argue that a witness should not be believed, pointing to inconsistencies, bias, interest, a lack of corroboration and contradiction by other evidence.
2. **No personal opinion.** Counsel may not state a personal belief in a client's guilt or innocence, or in the credibility of a witness. "The evidence shows that his account changed" is argument. "I believe he is lying" or "I believe in my client" is opinion. Opinion invites the jury to rely on the lawyer's credibility instead of the evidence.
3. **Only the record.** Counsel may not refer to facts that are not in evidence, including evidence the court has excluded, information obtained outside the trial, or what an uncalled witness would have said. Counsel may comment on the absence of evidence that the other side could reasonably be expected to produce, such as a record within a party's control. Counsel may not suggest what that evidence would have shown.
4. **The defendant's silence.** The prosecution may not comment, directly or by implication, on a defendant's failure to testify. Defense counsel may remind the jury of the instruction that the defendant's silence may not be considered. Defense counsel may not tell the jury why the defendant did not testify or what he would have said, because those are facts outside the record.
5. **Punishment and sympathy.** Neither side may refer to punishment or to the consequences of a conviction for the defendant or his family, or ask the jury to decide the case on sympathy or prejudice.
6. **The law.** Counsel may explain how the evidence satisfies or fails the court's instructions but may not misstate the law or the burden of proof. A defense lawyer must not suggest that the defendant bears any burden on a defense that the instructions place on the People.

Defense counsel's statements in this case were improper too. They were promptly corrected by the court, however, and could not have prejudiced the People. The prosecutor's statements, taken together, deprived Lindgren of a fair trial. Reversed.

---

### Columbia Rules of Professional Conduct, Rule 3.4(g) (Fairness to opposing party and counsel)

A lawyer shall not, in trial, allude to any matter that the lawyer does not reasonably believe is relevant or that will not be supported by admissible evidence; assert personal knowledge of facts in issue, except when testifying as a witness; or state a personal opinion as to the justness of a cause, the credibility of a witness, the culpability of a civil litigant, or the guilt or innocence of an accused.

## Model Answer

**CLOSING ARGUMENT ON BEHALF OF DEFENDANT ELI NAVARRO**
*People v. Navarro*, Superior Court of Columbia, County of Ridgemont, No. CR-26-0418

#### Theme

Members of the jury, good morning.

Broad daylight. A truck with his own name painted on the doors. A wave to the neighbor across the street. A text to the owner the night before, saying he was coming. And another text within the hour after he left, telling the owner exactly what he had taken and why.

That is not how a burglar behaves. It is not how a thief behaves. It is how a man behaves when he believes he is taking what is his.

This case comes down to one question: when Eli Navarro took that pressure washer, did he believe he had a right to it? Mr. Holloway himself told you that he offered Eli that pressure washer "toward what I owe you" (Tr. 20, 24). The People must prove, beyond a reasonable doubt, that Eli did *not* believe it was his. The evidence does not come close.

#### What is not in dispute

Let me start with what no one disputes. On September 12, Eli went into Mr. Holloway's garage, and he took the pressure washer. We do not dispute those facts.

Here is what else is undisputed. The leaf blower and the hedge trimmer were Eli's (Tr. 20). Eli finished Mr. Holloway's backyard on August 28, and Mr. Holloway still owes him $1,450 (Ex. A; Tr. 23). As of today, Mr. Holloway has not paid a dollar of it, and he has not decided whether he will (Tr. 23).

What is disputed is what was in Eli's mind. That is what the People must prove.

#### The burden of proof

Judge Alcott will instruct you that Eli is presumed innocent. The People must prove each element of each charge beyond a reasonable doubt, which means proof that leaves you "firmly convinced." Eli "does not have to prove anything" (CCJI 100).

Two more instructions matter in this case.

**First, circumstantial evidence.** Intent is proved by circumstances. And the judge will tell you that if you can draw two reasonable conclusions from the circumstantial evidence, one pointing to innocence and one to guilt, "you must accept the one that points to innocence" (CCJI 106). Not "may." *Must*.

**Second, claim of right.** If Eli took the property under a good-faith claim of right, he did not have the intent required for theft. And it is the People who "must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant did not act under a claim of right" (CCJI 1863). Eli does not have to prove what he believed. The People must disprove it.

The judge will also tell you that Eli has an absolute constitutional right not to testify, and that you may not consider, for any reason, the fact that he did not (CCJI 115). The burden stays where the law puts it: on the People.

Here is where we are going. First, the question at the heart of both charges: claim of right. Second, the two burglary elements the People cannot prove, consent and intent at the moment of entry. Along the way, we will look squarely at every piece of evidence the prosecutor relies on.

#### Count 2, theft: the People cannot disprove Eli's claim of right

The judge will tell you that a claim of right has two parts (CCJI 1863). Let's take them one at a time.

**Part one: Eli believed in good faith that he had a right to that specific pressure washer.**

Where did that belief come from? It came from Mr. Holloway. On August 30, Eli came by about the money. Mr. Holloway told you what he said: "I never use that pressure washer, maybe take it toward what I owe you" (Tr. 20). On cross-examination he agreed those were his words, or "something like that" (Tr. 24).

The judge's instruction says that the defense does not apply when someone takes property just to collect a debt. But listen to the rest of that sentence: "*unless* he believed in good faith that the owner had agreed to give him that specific property" (CCJI 1863). That is this case. Eli did not walk into the garage and pick something to hold until he was paid. He took the one item the owner himself had offered him toward the bill.

Look at what Eli wrote within an hour of leaving, before any police and before any accusation: "Took the pressure washer like you said, toward what you owe me. Pay me the rest and we're square" (Ex. 3, Sept. 12, 3:05 p.m.).
- "**Like you said.**" He is repeating Mr. Holloway's own offer back to him.
- "**Toward what you owe me.**" Those are Mr. Holloway's words from August 30.
- "**Pay me the rest.**" *The rest.* Someone who is holding property as security does not talk about "the rest." Eli was treating the washer as part payment, which is exactly what Mr. Holloway had proposed.

The next morning, when Officer Ortiz came to his door, Eli said the same thing: "Grant told me to take the pressure washer for what he owes me" (Tr. 34). He said it twice (Tr. 37).

The People's answer will be that Mr. Holloway took the offer back in a phone call. Ask yourselves how strong that evidence is.
- There is no phone record of that call (Tr. 25). Mr. Holloway is not even sure of the date. It "could have been the 4th or the 5th" (Tr. 25).
- He never mentioned that call to Officer Ortiz (Tr. 37).
- That same evening he told the officer, "I never offered him anything" (Tr. 37). In this courtroom he admitted that he did make the offer. And the first time he told law enforcement about it was when the prosecutor asked him, last week (Tr. 24). The judge will tell you that you may consider an earlier inconsistent statement both in judging his testimony and as evidence that the earlier statement was true (CCJI 110).
- His September 8 text, the only written message from Mr. Holloway before the 12th, mentions Eli's blower and trimmer. It says nothing about the pressure washer. It does not say "I've changed my mind" (Ex. 3; Tr. 25).
- And consider his interest in the outcome. He still owes $1,450, and he has not decided whether to pay it (Tr. 23). The judge will tell you that you may consider whether a witness has "any bias, interest or reason to testify in a particular way" (CCJI 110).

Even if you accepted that Mr. Holloway said "forget it" on some day in early September, the question is not whether his offer was legally still open. The question is what *Eli believed*. The judge will tell you that a belief can be held in good faith "even if it is mistaken or unreasonable" (CCJI 1863). Consider a brief phone call that left no record, whose date Mr. Holloway cannot pin down, and which he did not think worth mentioning to the police. That is not a fact that made Eli's belief "completely unreasonable."

**Part two: Eli did not try to conceal the taking.**

This part is not close.
- It was a sunny Saturday afternoon (Tr. 16).
- His truck, with "Navarro Landscaping" on the doors, was parked in the driveway (Tr. 14, 16).
- He waved to Mrs. Finch, who was in plain view in her front yard. He did not hide his face or look up and down the street. In her words, he "just went about it, like someone doing a job" (Tr. 16).
- Within the hour, he told Mr. Holloway in writing what he had taken (Ex. 3).
- The next morning he told the officer where the washer was, walked him to it and handed it over within a couple of minutes, "calm and cooperative" (Tr. 35, 37). It came back undamaged (Tr. 27).

And look at what he did *not* take. Mr. Holloway's garage held a $1,200 generator, a table saw, two bicycles and a set of golf clubs (Ex. 4; Tr. 27). All of them were still there. If Eli had come to grab whatever would cover his $1,450, the generator alone was worth more. He took his own two tools and the one item Mr. Holloway had offered him. Nothing else.

**The prosecutor's best evidence on Count 2.** Ms. Pike will point to the officer's account that Eli said, "He can have it back when he pays me" (Tr. 34). There are three answers.

*First*, those are not Eli's recorded words. The body camera's battery was dead. There is no audio. The officer took no notes during the conversation and wrote his report two days later. He told you the words in quotation marks are his "best recollection, not word for word" (Tr. 36). The judge will instruct you to "consider with caution" any statement of the defendant tending to show guilt "unless the statement was written or otherwise recorded" (CCJI 120). This one was not.

*Second*, we do have Eli's own written words, from the day before that conversation. They say, "Pay me *the rest* and we're square" (Ex. 3). The officer never asked Eli what he meant by "when he pays me": the whole $1,450, or the rest (Tr. 37). The written words are the reliable evidence of what Eli meant. And the judge struck the officer's view of what the statement "seemed like" (Tr. 38). You must disregard it (CCJI 104).

*Third*, in the same conversation the same officer heard Eli say, twice, that Grant told him to take it (Tr. 37).

At the very least, there are two reasonable readings: that Eli was holding the washer as collateral, or that he believed it was his as part payment and was asking for the balance. Under CCJI 106 you must accept the reading that points to innocence. Under CCJI 1863, the People must disprove the claim of right beyond a reasonable doubt. They have not. On Count 2, the verdict the instructions require is not guilty.

#### Count 1, burglary: two elements the People cannot prove

For burglary, the People must prove three things (CCJI 1700). We do not dispute the first: Eli entered a building. The People cannot prove the second or the third.

**Element 2: the People have not proved that Eli entered without consent.**

The judge will tell you that consent can be given "by words or by conduct." Consent ends only if the owner withdrew it "by words or conduct communicated to the defendant before the entry" (CCJI 1700).
- Mr. Holloway gave Eli the keypad code so that he could keep his equipment in that garage (Tr. 19). He never changed it ("I still haven't"), and he never told Eli that it no longer worked for him (Tr. 26).
- Eli's tools were in that garage with Mr. Holloway's permission (Tr. 20). Going in to get them was within the very purpose for which he was given the code.
- Mr. Holloway promised to leave the tools on the porch on Saturday. He forgot (Tr. 25). So on Saturday afternoon, Eli's tools were still locked inside (Tr. 26).

Ms. Pike will point to Mr. Holloway's message, "Don't come by without calling first" (Ex. 3). Look at what happened next. The night before, Eli sent a message: "I'm coming by tomorrow to get my stuff." Mr. Holloway read it at 10:15 p.m. (Ex. 3; Tr. 26). He did not reply, "Don't come." He did not call. He did not change the code (Tr. 26). And Eli came on the very day Mr. Holloway had chosen: Saturday.

That is not consent withdrawn and communicated. It is consent left in place by an owner who knew Eli was coming and did nothing to stop him. At the very least, it is reasonable doubt.

**Element 3: the People have not proved that Eli intended to commit theft when he walked in.**

The judge will tell you that the intent must exist "at the time he entered." If the intent was formed only after entering, it is not burglary. And if he "entered intending only to take property that he believed in good faith he had a right to take," he "did not enter with intent to commit theft" (CCJI 1700).

What did Eli say his purpose was before he came? "I'm coming by tomorrow to get my stuff" (Ex. 3, Sept. 11, 9:47 p.m.). What did he carry out first? His own leaf blower and hedge trimmer (Tr. 15). And his hands were empty going in: no bags, no tools (Tr. 17).

There are only three possibilities, and none of them is burglary:
- If he went in intending to take only his own tools, that is not burglary.
- If he went in intending to take his tools and the washer he believed Mr. Holloway had given him, that is not intent to commit theft either, for every reason we just covered.
- If you think he decided on the pressure washer only once he was inside, the judge tells you that is not burglary.

**"One way or another."** The prosecutor's centerpiece will be this message: "Tomorrow I'm getting what I'm owed one way or another" (Ex. 3, 10:02 p.m.). Let's face it head-on.

Read it together with the message Eli sent fifteen minutes earlier: "Still waiting on my $1,450. I'm coming by tomorrow to get my stuff." What did Eli think he was owed? His own tools, which were sitting in another man's garage, and the pressure washer that man had offered him toward the bill. He was a man "tired of waiting," as he told the officer (Tr. 35). "One way or another" are the words of frustration, not a plan to steal.

Now look at what he did the next day. He came in daylight, in his marked truck. He took his tools and the one item that had been offered to him. He left the $1,200 generator. He texted the owner within the hour. If "one way or another" meant "I'll take whatever I want," his conduct the next day was the opposite. The People ask you to read those words in the most sinister way possible. The judge will tell you that when circumstantial evidence supports two reasonable conclusions, you must accept the one that points to innocence (CCJI 106).

**The other points you will hear.**
- *"He went when Mr. Holloway was at work."* Mr. Holloway chose Saturday himself (Ex. 3). Eli announced that he was coming (Ex. 3). People who want to avoid being seen do not text the owner first and wave to the neighbor.
- *"He was inside longer the second time."* Mrs. Finch could not see what he was doing (Tr. 17). The pressure washer is a big unit that sat on a shelf (Tr. 15, 20; Ex. 4). It is reasonable to infer that getting it down takes longer than picking up a leaf blower. And nothing suggests he was looking for anything else, because he took nothing else (Tr. 17, 27).
- *"He didn't answer the 3:40 text."* He had already told Mr. Holloway, in writing, exactly what he took and why (Ex. 3). The next morning he gave the police the same account and handed the washer over (Tr. 34–37).
- *"You can't take someone's property just because he owes you money."* That is right, and the judge's instruction says so. But Eli did not pick something to hold hostage. He took the one item that Mr. Holloway had offered him toward that debt, and the instruction tells you that is different (CCJI 1863).

#### Conclusion

Members of the jury, the People must prove what was in Eli Navarro's mind beyond a reasonable doubt, and they must disprove his claim of right beyond a reasonable doubt.

The evidence shows a man who did the work and was not paid. The man who owed him offered him a pressure washer toward the bill. He took it in broad daylight, in a truck with his name on the doors, waving to the neighbor. He told the owner within the hour, and he handed the washer to the police the next morning.

Under the judge's instructions, that is not theft. And without an intent to commit theft at the moment he walked through that garage door, it is not burglary.

When you go back to the jury room, take the instructions with you. Read CCJI 1863, and ask whether the People have proved, beyond a reasonable doubt, that Eli did not believe that pressure washer was his. Read CCJI 106, and ask whether the evidence of his intent can reasonably be read in his favor. It can.

Find Eli Navarro **not guilty of burglary in Count 1**, and **not guilty of theft in Count 2**.

Thank you.

**Length note.** This model runs to about 2,800 words, roughly 20 minutes spoken. An answer scoring 70 or more can be about 2,000–2,400 words. Cut the roadmap and the list of "other points" before you cut the claim-of-right section or the treatment of "one way or another."

**Common mistakes**
1. Personal opinion: "I believe," "I'm confident," "Mr. Holloway is a liar." Rule 3.4(g) and *Lindgren* allow attacks on credibility only through the evidence: inconsistencies, bias, lack of corroboration.
2. Using the File's non-evidence: Karen Holloway's statement to the investigator, the excluded small-claims judgments, Eli's clean record or children, or Eli's note saying he wanted to testify. All are outside the record, and some are covered by the court's in limine order.
3. Shifting the burden, for example "Eli has proved he believed the washer was his." CCJI 1863 puts the burden of disproving claim of right on the People.
4. Missing the debt trap: arguing "Holloway owed him money, so he could take it." CCJI 1863 excludes debt collection. The defense works only through the clause "unless he believed ... the owner had agreed to give him that specific property."
5. Collapsing burglary into theft. Consent and intent at the time of entry are separate elements, each with its own instruction language and facts.
