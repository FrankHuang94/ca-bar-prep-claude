### Columbia Commercial Code (selected sections)

**§ 1201(b)(10). "Conspicuous."** "Conspicuous," with reference to a term, means so written, displayed or presented that a reasonable person against whom it is to operate ought to have noticed it. Whether a term is conspicuous is a question for the court. Conspicuous terms include, without limitation: (A) a heading in capitals equal to or greater in size than the surrounding text, or in a type, font or color that contrasts with surrounding text of the same or smaller size; and (B) language in the body of a record in larger type than the surrounding text, or in a type, font or color that contrasts with surrounding text of the same size, or set off from surrounding text of the same size by symbols or other marks that call attention to it.

**§ 2209(1). Modification.** An agreement modifying a contract within this division needs no consideration to be binding.

**§ 2313. Express warranties by affirmation, promise, description or sample.**
(1) A seller creates an express warranty as follows:
(a) An affirmation of fact or a promise made by the seller to the buyer that relates to the goods and becomes part of the basis of the bargain creates an express warranty that the goods will conform to the affirmation or promise.
(b) A description of the goods that is made part of the basis of the bargain creates an express warranty that the goods will conform to the description.
(c) A sample or model that is made part of the basis of the bargain creates an express warranty that the whole of the goods will conform to it.
(2) The seller need not use formal words such as "warrant" or "guarantee," or have a specific intention to make a warranty. But an affirmation merely of the value of the goods, or a statement purporting to be merely the seller's opinion or commendation of the goods, does not create a warranty.

**§ 2314. Implied warranty: merchantability.**
(1) Unless excluded or modified under § 2316, a warranty that the goods are merchantable is implied in a contract for their sale if the seller is a merchant with respect to goods of that kind.
(2) Goods are merchantable only if they are at least such as: (a) pass without objection in the trade under the contract description; … (c) are fit for the ordinary purposes for which such goods are used; …

**§ 2316. Exclusion or modification of warranties.**
(1) Words or conduct relevant to the creation of an express warranty, and words or conduct tending to negate or limit a warranty, shall be construed wherever reasonable as consistent with each other. A negation or limitation is inoperative to the extent that such a construction is unreasonable.
(2) Subject to subdivision (3), to exclude or modify the implied warranty of merchantability or any part of it, the language must mention merchantability and, if in writing, must be conspicuous. To exclude or modify an implied warranty of fitness, the exclusion must be in a writing and conspicuous.
(3) Notwithstanding subdivision (2):
(a) unless the circumstances indicate otherwise, all implied warranties are excluded by expressions like "as is," "with all faults" or other language that in common understanding calls the buyer's attention to the exclusion of warranties and makes plain that there is no implied warranty; and
(b) when the buyer, before entering into the contract, has examined the goods as fully as the buyer desired, or has refused to examine the goods, there is no implied warranty with regard to defects that an examination ought in the circumstances to have revealed to the buyer.

### *Harlan Farm Supply, Inc. v. Ostrowski* (Columbia Supreme Court, 2011)

Defendant Paul Ostrowski operates a 900-acre grain farm. Over ten years he bought used equipment from plaintiff Harlan Farm Supply, Inc., a dealer in new and used farm machinery, on seven occasions, each time using Harlan's one-page bill of sale. In 2008 he agreed to buy a used combine harvester for $64,000. While the two men stood beside the machine, Harlan's salesman told Ostrowski that "the engine was completely overhauled last winter, with new pistons and new rings," and that "she'll run forever." The front of the bill of sale, immediately above the buyer's signature line, bore a stamp in red ink reading "AS IS — NO WARRANTY" in capital letters roughly twice the height of the surrounding text. Ostrowski signed. The engine seized after forty hours of use. Undisputed evidence showed that it had never been overhauled. When Ostrowski stopped payment on the final installment, Harlan sued for the balance, and Ostrowski cross-claimed for breach of express and implied warranties. The trial court granted summary judgment to Harlan on all warranty claims, reasoning that "as is" means no warranty of any kind. The Court of Appeal affirmed. We affirm in part and reverse in part.

**Implied warranties.** Section 2316(3)(a) provides that, unless the circumstances indicate otherwise, expressions like "as is" exclude all implied warranties. Unlike § 2316(2), subdivision (3)(a) does not require that the word "merchantability" be used. Ostrowski argues that "as is" language must nonetheless be conspicuous. We agree. Subdivision (3)(a) operates only through language that "calls the buyer's attention to the exclusion." Language that a reasonable buyer would not notice calls nobody's attention to anything. We therefore hold that "as is" language excludes implied warranties only if it is conspicuous within the meaning of § 1201(b)(10).

Whether a term is conspicuous is a question of law for the court. The test is objective: whether a reasonable person in the position of the party against whom the term operates ought to have noticed it. The examples listed in § 1201(b)(10), such as larger type, contrasting type or color, or symbols that set the language apart, are illustrations, not a checklist. Courts should consider the document as a whole, including where the language appears, its size and typeface relative to the surrounding text, whether it is on the face of the document the buyer signs, and the buyer's experience, including prior transactions on the same form.

Harlan's stamp was printed in red, at twice the size of the surrounding text, immediately above the line Ostrowski signed. A reasonable buyer could not miss it, least of all an experienced farmer who had signed the same form seven times. The "as is" language was conspicuous, and Ostrowski's implied warranty claims fail.

**Express warranty.** The lower courts erred, however, in extending the "as is" clause to the express warranty claim. Section 2316(3)(a) speaks only of implied warranties. An "as is" clause tells the buyer that the seller makes no promises beyond those it has actually made. It does not erase the promises the seller did make.

An express warranty arises from an affirmation of fact or promise relating to the goods, or a description of the goods, that becomes part of the basis of the bargain. § 2313(1). The statement that the engine "was completely overhauled last winter, with new pistons and new rings" was a specific statement of past fact about the very goods being sold, capable of being proved true or false. It was not opinion. By contrast, "she'll run forever" is the kind of general commendation that no reasonable buyer takes literally; it is puffery that creates no warranty under § 2313(2). The line between the two is whether the statement is specific and verifiable, or vague and evaluative.

A statement made by the seller about the goods during the bargaining is presumed to be part of the basis of the bargain. The buyer need not prove that he relied on it separately. The presumption may be overcome only by clear affirmative proof that the statement did not become part of the deal, for example because the buyer knew it was untrue. Harlan offered no such proof.

Finally, the general disclaimer language did not negate the overhaul warranty. Under § 2316(1), words creating an express warranty and words negating it are read together where reasonable, but the negation is inoperative to the extent that a consistent reading is unreasonable. A seller may not tell the buyer exactly what he is getting with one breath and, with the next, tell him that the words mean nothing. A general "no warranty" legend cannot reasonably be reconciled with a specific factual statement about the goods that the seller made to induce the sale.

Harlan did not argue in this court that the parol evidence rule bars proof of the salesman's statements, and we express no view on that question. The judgment is affirmed as to the implied warranty claims and reversed as to the express warranty claim, and the case is remanded for trial on that claim.

### *Pell Brothers Catering, LLC v. Summit Restaurant Supply Co.* (Columbia Court of Appeal, 2017)

Pell Brothers Catering bought a used walk-in refrigeration unit from Summit Restaurant Supply Co. for $11,200. Summit's invoice described the unit as a "reconditioned walk-in cooler, 8 x 10, compressor serviced." Among several lines printed in the same 8-point type near the bottom of the front of the invoice was the sentence: "See reverse side for terms and conditions, including warranty terms." Pell's owner signed the front. The reverse contained fourteen paragraphs in 6-point type. Paragraph 11 stated, in the same type as the other paragraphs: "Seller disclaims all implied warranties, including the warranty of merchantability."

Four days after the sale, Summit's installer set up the unit and handed Pell's kitchen manager an "Installation Checklist." It contained a bold, boxed statement: "CUSTOMER ACCEPTS UNIT AS IS. NO WARRANTIES." The kitchen manager, who was responsible for receiving deliveries but had no role in purchasing, signed it. The installer did not explain the document. The compressor failed three weeks later. The trial court ruled that both documents excluded the implied warranty of merchantability and entered judgment for Summit. We reverse.

**The invoice.** Paragraph 11 mentions merchantability, as § 2316(2) requires, but it is not conspicuous. It sits on the back of the document, in the same small type, size and color as the paragraphs around it, with nothing to set it apart. The sentence on the front referring the buyer to the reverse was itself printed in the same type as the lines around it. A general reference to "terms on the reverse" does not make every term on the reverse conspicuous. A front-side reference that is itself conspicuous and that specifically warns the buyer that warranties are excluded might lead to a different result, but that is not this case. Summit stresses that the front-side sentence mentioned "warranty terms." But a reasonable buyer reading that phrase could as easily expect to find a warranty as an exclusion of one.

We construe warranty disclaimers strictly against the seller who drafts them and resolve ambiguities in favor of the warranty. A seller that wants to exclude warranties controls its own forms and can say so plainly and prominently.

Summit also argues that Pell's owner, an experienced caterer, must have known that used equipment is commonly sold without warranties. A buyer who actually knew of the specific disclaimer when contracting cannot later complain that it was inconspicuous, because the purpose of the requirement has been served. But a general awareness of trade practice is not knowledge of this seller's disclaimer, and Summit offered no evidence that Pell's owner read paragraph 11.

**The Installation Checklist.** The contract was formed when Pell's owner signed the invoice. Terms that a seller presents after the contract is formed are not part of the parties' bargain. They can become binding only as a modification. Section 2209(1) dispenses with consideration for a modification, but it does not dispense with agreement: both parties must assent to the change. A signature obtained from a receiving employee who had no authority to negotiate purchase terms, on a document presented as a routine installation form and never explained as a change to the deal, is not assent by the buyer to give up its warranty. Because the kitchen manager's signature was not an agreement to modify, we need not decide whether Summit's attempt to obtain a waiver in this way was consistent with the obligation of good faith.

Reversed and remanded for trial on the implied warranty claim.

### *Lindqvist v. Bayview Motors, Inc.* (Columbia Court of Appeal, 2020)

Ingrid Lindqvist runs a flower-delivery business. She bought a used delivery van from Bayview Motors, Inc., a used-vehicle dealer, for $14,500. Before buying, she test-drove the van for thirty minutes with her brother-in-law, who repairs cars as a hobby. During the drive she heard a grinding noise each time she shifted into third gear. She mentioned the noise to Bayview's salesman, who said nothing in reply. Bayview offered to let her take the van to a mechanic of her choice before buying. She declined and bought the van the same day. Two weeks later the transmission failed. A month after that, the engine overheated because of a cracked cylinder head, which could be detected only by partly disassembling the engine.

The trial court found that Bayview's written disclaimer was not conspicuous, and Bayview does not challenge that ruling. The trial court then held that, under § 2316(3)(b), Lindqvist's pre-purchase examination excluded the implied warranty of merchantability as to both defects. We affirm as to the transmission and reverse as to the cylinder head.

**Merchantability of used goods.** Used goods need not perform as new goods would. Whether used goods are fit for their ordinary purposes is measured by what a reasonable buyer would expect of goods of that description, age and price. A delivery van whose transmission fails after two weeks is not fit for the ordinary purpose of a delivery van, even a used one.

**The examination exclusion.** Section 2316(3)(b) applies when the buyer, before contracting, has examined the goods as fully as the buyer desired or has refused to examine them. It excludes the implied warranty only for "defects that an examination ought in the circumstances to have revealed to the buyer." The circumstances include the buyer's own skill and knowledge, the nature of the goods, the normal method of examining goods of that kind, and anything the seller said or did that bore on the examination. A buyer who is a professional in a particular field is charged with the defects that a professional in that field would observe. A buyer without that expertise is charged only with the defects a person of ordinary knowledge would observe. A buyer is not charged with defects that could be found only by disassembly, unless the buyer refused an inspection that would have included it.

*The transmission.* Lindqvist examined the van as fully as she desired, and she declined the mechanic's inspection Bayview offered. More important, her examination actually revealed the problem: she heard the grinding and asked about it. Bayview's salesman offered no explanation, and Lindqvist was left with her own observation. A buyer who observes a symptom of a defect and buys without further inquiry takes the risk of that defect, even if she did not appreciate how serious it was. The implied warranty does not cover the transmission.

*The cylinder head.* The crack could not be seen, heard or felt on a test drive. Bayview's service manager testified that a standard pre-purchase mechanical inspection does not include disassembling the cylinder head. So even the inspection Lindqvist refused would not have revealed the crack, and her refusal does not charge her with it. A defect that neither the buyer's examination nor the refused inspection ought to have revealed remains within the implied warranty.

The examination exclusion in § 2316(3)(b) applies only to implied warranties. What a buyer observes before buying may bear on whether a seller's statement became part of the basis of the bargain, but an examination does not by itself negate an express warranty.

Affirmed in part, reversed in part, and remanded.

## Model Answer

**MEMORANDUM**

**TO:** Gwen Abernathy, Partner
**FROM:** Applicant
**DATE:** April 22, 2026
**RE:** Hearthstone Bread Co.: express and implied warranty claims against Kessler Kitchen Equipment, Inc. (Marlowe DX-3 oven, P.O. 4471)

### Short Answers

**1. Express warranties: likely yes, and the disclaimers probably do not defeat them.** Kessler described the oven as "Used – Rebuilt" on the face of the Purchase Order, described "Certified Rebuilt" units on its website as having worn elements and thermostats replaced with new parts, and its salesman said the oven had "new heating elements in all three decks, new thermostat" and would "hold five hundred degrees all day long." These are descriptions and specific statements of fact or promises that are presumed to be part of the basis of the bargain (Columbia Commercial Code § 2313; *Harlan Farm Supply v. Ostrowski*). "Best deck ovens ever built" and "outlive both of us" are puffery. "As is" language does not reach express warranties (*Harlan*), and the reverse-side disclaimer cannot reasonably be reconciled with the specific "rebuilt" description, so it is inoperative to that extent (§ 2316(1)). Bayline's findings indicate breach. The main risks are Kessler's evidence that it replaced some parts, and paragraph 8's statement that descriptions are "for identification only."

**2. Implied warranty of merchantability: a closer question, but the claim more likely than not survives.** (a) The front-side "AS IS" sentence is our greatest risk. It is on the signed face, and Ms. Vance is an experienced buyer who had used the same form twice. But it is in the same 8-point type as the text around it, is not set off in any way, and by its own terms applies only "unless otherwise stated on this Purchase Order," and the form states "Used – Rebuilt." A court applying *Harlan*'s conspicuousness requirement and *Pell Brothers*' rule of strict construction will probably find it ineffective. The reverse-side paragraph 8 is likely not conspicuous under *Pell Brothers*. (b) The Delivery Receipt is likely ineffective: it was presented after the contract was formed and signed by a porter with no authority to change the deal, almost exactly as in *Pell Brothers*. (c) The examination exclusion probably does not bar the claim for the internal defects (original corroded elements, original controller, cracked baffle), which a baker could not see (*Lindqvist v. Bayview Motors*). Kessler's best argument concerns the bottom-deck lag that Mr. Bell observed. It is weakened by Kessler's "seasoning" explanation and by its refusal to open the back panel.

### Discussion

#### I. Kessler made express warranties that the disclaimers probably do not defeat

**A. Governing law.** The sale of an oven is a sale of goods governed by the Columbia Commercial Code, and Kessler, which sells commercial kitchen equipment, is a merchant with respect to ovens. An express warranty is created by (a) an affirmation of fact or promise relating to the goods, or (b) a description of the goods, that becomes part of the basis of the bargain (§ 2313(1)). No formal words or intent to warrant are needed, but statements of value, opinion or commendation create no warranty (§ 2313(2)). Under *Harlan*, the line is whether a statement is "specific and verifiable" (warranty) or "vague and evaluative" (puffery). A statement the seller makes during the bargaining is presumed to be part of the basis of the bargain; the seller can rebut that presumption only by "clear affirmative proof" (*Harlan*).

**B. The statements.**

1. *"Used – Rebuilt" on the Purchase Order.* This is a description of the goods in the signed contract itself (§ 2313(1)(b)). It is our strongest warranty. It does not merely identify the oven, because the serial number and stock number do that. It describes the oven's condition, and the condition determined the price.

2. *The website listing.* "Certified Rebuilt," together with the statement that worn "heating elements, thermostats, gaskets and controls are replaced with new parts" and that each unit is "shop-tested at full temperature," is a description and a set of specific factual statements. Ms. Vance read and printed the listing on February 18, two days before the purchase. Kessler will point to the italic line saying the listing is "for general descriptive purposes." But a description is exactly what § 2313(1)(b) turns into a warranty. Reading the two "as consistent" where reasonable (§ 2316(1)), the line at most signals that the Purchase Order controls, and the Purchase Order repeats "Rebuilt." It cannot reasonably mean that the condition described is untrue.

3. *Mr. Kowalski's statements.* "This one came through our rebuild shop last month. New heating elements in all three decks, new thermostat, new door gaskets" is a specific, verifiable statement of past fact about this oven, closely parallel to the "completely overhauled ... new pistons and new rings" statement that *Harlan* held was a warranty. "It'll hold five hundred degrees all day long" is a promise about performance stated with a specific, measurable figure. Kessler will call it sales talk, but unlike "run forever," it can be tested, and the oven failed the test. It is likely a warranty. By contrast, "the best deck ovens ever built" and "outlive both of us" are puffery under *Harlan*, as is "rebuilt units rarely need [Kessler Care]," an opinion.

**C. Basis of the bargain.** The statements are presumed to be part of the bargain (*Harlan*), and the File shows actual reliance as well: Ms. Vance says "rebuilt was the whole point"; she paid $18,500 when an unrebuilt DX-3 sells for $9,000–$10,000; and she texted at the time that the "price is good for rebuilt." Kessler may argue that she relied on her own examination instead, or that declining Kessler Care showed she accepted the risk of failure. Neither is the "clear affirmative proof" *Harlan* requires. She asked about the one symptom she saw and accepted Kowalski's answer, and a service plan covering future maintenance is different from a representation about the oven's present condition. *Lindqvist* adds that an examination does not by itself negate an express warranty.

**D. Breach.** Bayline found that the bottom-deck elements and the controller are original 2011 parts, corroded and out of calibration, and that only one middle-deck element and the gaskets are new. That contradicts "new heating elements in all three decks, new thermostat" and Kessler's own website definition of a rebuild, and the oven cannot hold temperature in the bottom deck. Kessler's best response, previewed in Ms. Caldwell's email, is that its shop "did replace parts ... including elements and gaskets," so the oven was partly rebuilt and the rest is "normal wear." That defense fails against the specific oral statements (all three decks; new thermostat), and it is weak against "Rebuilt" given Kessler's own website definition and Ms. Whitford's opinion on the trade meaning. Proof of breach currently rests on Ms. Whitford, so we will want Kessler's shop records.

**E. Effect of the disclaimers on the express warranties.**

- *Front-side "AS IS."* "As is" excludes implied warranties only. *Harlan* allowed an express warranty claim to proceed despite a far more prominent "AS IS — NO WARRANTY" stamp. The sentence here also applies only "unless otherwise stated on this Purchase Order," and the Purchase Order states "Used – Rebuilt."
- *Reverse paragraph 8* ("no warranties, express or implied ... any description ... is for identification only"). Under § 2316(1), a negation is inoperative to the extent that it cannot reasonably be read consistently with the warranty. A checked box saying "Rebuilt" cannot reasonably be read as "for identification only," because it identifies nothing that the serial number does not. This is the *Harlan* situation of telling the buyer what she is getting "with one breath" and taking it back "with the next." Kessler will rely on the requirement of a "separate written warranty signed by an officer" and on Ms. Vance's experience. Those facts do not make the clause reconcilable with the specific description and statements. Paragraph 8 is also probably not conspicuous (Part II.B.2).
- *Delivery Receipt.* It purports to disclaim "any warranty ... express or implied," but it is a post-contract document that was not agreed to (Part II.C).

**Conclusion on Issue I.** A court will likely find express warranties in the "Rebuilt" description, the website description, and Kowalski's statements about new elements, a new thermostat and holding 500 degrees, and will likely find them breached and not defeated by the disclaimers. This is Ms. Vance's strongest claim.

#### II. The implied warranty of merchantability claim more likely than not survives

**A. The warranty arises and was probably breached.** Because Kessler is a merchant with respect to ovens, a warranty of merchantability is implied unless it was excluded (§ 2314(1)). Goods must be fit for their ordinary purposes (§ 2314(2)(c)). For used goods, that is measured by what a reasonable buyer would expect given the description, age and price (*Lindqvist*). Kessler will stress that the oven is fifteen years old. But a "rebuilt" commercial oven sold for $18,500, whose bottom deck could not exceed 380°F about six weeks after delivery and whose middle deck swings 40 degrees, is not fit for the ordinary purpose of baking bread. The real question is exclusion.

**B. Exclusion by the Purchase Order.**

1. *The front-side "AS IS" sentence (§ 2316(3)(a)).* "As is" excludes implied warranties without mentioning merchantability, but only if it is conspicuous (*Harlan*). Conspicuousness is a question of law, decided by whether a reasonable person in the buyer's position ought to have noticed the language, looking at placement, size and typeface relative to the surrounding text, whether it is on the signed face, and the buyer's experience (*Harlan*; § 1201(b)(10)).
   - *For Kessler:* the sentence is on the face Ms. Vance signed, immediately above the signature lines, as in *Harlan*. "AS IS" is in capitals, which Kessler will call "contrasting type" under § 1201(b)(10)(B). Ms. Vance has twelve years in business, has bought six or seven pieces of used equipment, and has signed this form twice before; *Harlan* treated prior dealings on the same form as significant. She also admits she knows used equipment "is often sold as is."
   - *For Hearthstone:* *Harlan*'s stamp was red, twice the size of the surrounding text, and a stand-alone legend. Here the two words sit mid-sentence in an unheaded 8-point acknowledgment box, in the same typeface and size as the routine "Delivery date" and "Payment" lines, with no bolding, underlining, color or symbols. Capitalizing two words inside boilerplate is the faintest possible contrast. Under *Pell Brothers*, Ms. Vance's general awareness of trade practice is not knowledge of this disclaimer, and there is no evidence she read it on the earlier forms ("I never read the back"; she did not notice "AS IS" on February 20). The clause is also qualified by its own text: equipment is sold as is "unless otherwise stated on this Purchase Order," and the same face states "Used – Rebuilt." Construed strictly against Kessler as drafter (*Pell Brothers*), the clause either does not apply to a rebuilt unit or is ambiguous, and ambiguity is resolved in favor of the warranty. Finally, § 2316(3)(a) applies only "unless the circumstances indicate otherwise." Selling a "Certified Rebuilt" unit at nearly double the unrebuilt price, while saying its key parts are new, is a circumstance indicating that the buyer was not taking the oven with all faults.
   - *Prediction:* This is the closest question in the matter. The signature-line placement and Ms. Vance's experience give Kessler a real argument, but I think a court is more likely than not to find the "AS IS" language not conspicuous, or inapplicable because of the "unless otherwise stated" proviso. I would put our chances somewhat better than even.

2. *Reverse paragraph 8 (§ 2316(2)).* Paragraph 8 mentions merchantability and is in writing, so the question is conspicuousness. Kessler will argue that, unlike the disclaimer in *Pell Brothers*, which was in the same type as the paragraphs around it, paragraph 8 is entirely in capitals among lower-case paragraphs, which is "contrasting type" under § 1201(b)(10)(B). That is a real distinction: *Pell Brothers* stressed that paragraph 11 had "nothing to set it apart," and contrasting type in the body of a record is one of § 1201(b)(10)'s own examples. But *Harlan* treats those examples as illustrations, not a checklist, and directs the court to the document as a whole, and the rest of *Pell Brothers*' reasoning applies fully. Paragraph 8 is on the unsigned back of the form, in 6.5-point gray type with no heading, the smallest print on the document. The front refers only to "Terms and Conditions on the reverse side" and, unlike the invoice in *Pell Brothers*, does not even mention warranties; *Pell Brothers* holds that a generic front-side reference does not make every term on the reverse conspicuous. A reasonable person signing the front ought not be expected to turn the form over and pick out one capitalized paragraph of tiny gray print. *Prediction:* more likely than not inconspicuous, though less clearly than in *Pell Brothers*, and any doubt is resolved against Kessler as drafter.

**C. The Delivery Receipt was not a binding modification.** The contract was formed on February 20 when both parties signed the Purchase Order and Ms. Vance paid in full. The Receipt was presented a week later. Under *Pell Brothers*, post-formation terms bind only as a modification, which needs no consideration (§ 2209(1)) but does require the buyer's agreement. The facts here almost mirror *Pell Brothers*: Danny Ruiz, a 19-year-old porter, signs only for flour and supply deliveries, has never signed a contract, was not authorized to, and signed at 7:40 a.m. on an installation checklist while Ms. Vance was away. Nothing suggests the installer explained that the form changed the deal. Kessler will stress that this disclaimer, unlike the Purchase Order language, is conspicuous: bold, boxed, in capitals, and mentioning merchantability. But conspicuousness does not supply assent. Kessler might also argue that Hearthstone let Mr. Ruiz sign for deliveries, but authority to receive goods is not authority to give up warranty rights, which is the distinction *Pell Brothers* draws. *Prediction:* the Receipt is likely ineffective.

**D. The pre-purchase examination (§ 2316(3)(b)).** When a buyer has examined the goods as fully as she desired, or refused to examine them, there is no implied warranty for defects that an examination "ought in the circumstances" to have revealed. The circumstances include the buyer's skill, the nature of the goods, the normal method of examination, and "anything the seller said or did that bore on the examination" (*Lindqvist*). A professional is charged with what a professional in her field would observe, and no buyer is charged with defects found only by disassembly unless she refused an inspection that would have included it (*Lindqvist*).

1. *The internal defects.* The corroded original bottom elements, the 2011 controller and the cracked baffle were behind rear panels held by 12 screws. Ms. Whitford says a technician removing those panels would likely have seen them, but "a baker looking through the deck doors would not." Like the cracked cylinder head in *Lindqvist*, these were latent. Kessler will argue that Ms. Vance and Mr. Bell are professionals and that an experienced commercial buyer should have brought a technician. But they are professional bakers, not oven technicians, so *Lindqvist* charges them only with what a baker would observe. Unlike Lindqvist, who declined an offered mechanic's inspection, Ms. Vance did not refuse an examination. Mr. Bell asked to see behind the back panel, and Kessler's technician discouraged it by saying it would break the "Certified Rebuilt" seal. Kessler will answer with Ms. Vance's own reply text, "fine, dont break it," and argue that she refused the one inspection that would have revealed these defects, which under *Lindqvist* would charge her with them. The better reading is that Kessler, not the buyer, closed off that inspection: unlike Bayview, Kessler never offered an inspection but discouraged one, by warning that opening the panel would void the very "Certified Rebuilt" seal she was paying for, and *Lindqvist* counts "anything the seller said or did that bore on the examination." Kessler limited the examination; she did not examine "as fully as [she] desired," and the acknowledgment that she "had the opportunity to inspect" is boilerplate contradicted by what happened. These defects are likely not excluded.

2. *The bottom-deck lag.* This is Kessler's best point, and Ms. Caldwell's email already makes it. Under *Lindqvist*, a buyer who observes a symptom and buys without further inquiry takes the risk of that defect, and Mr. Bell's text ("top 2 at 480 already, bottom barely 400") shows that they saw the symptom. But *Lindqvist* turned on the fact that the seller "offered no explanation" and the buyer "was left with her own observation." Here Ms. Vance did inquire, and Kowalski explained the lag as normal for a DX-3 and as new elements needing to "season." According to Bayline, that explanation was false: new elements do not need seasoning, and the lag was a sign of failing elements. Because what the seller said is part of "the circumstances," an examination does not reveal a defect that the seller explains away to a buyer with no DX-3 experience. Kessler may answer that the buyers were warned enough to investigate further and chose not to. *Prediction:* the exclusion probably does not apply, but there is a meaningful risk that a court will treat the bottom-deck temperature problem as "revealed" and exclude it. Even then, the controller drift, the middle-deck instability and the cracked baffle would remain covered, and the express warranty claim is unaffected.

**Conclusion on Issue II.** The merchantability claim is viable but less secure than the express warranty claim. Its weakest link is the front-side "AS IS" sentence, followed by the observed bottom-deck lag.
