### Columbia Government Code § 5520 (Columbia Fair Employment Act) (selected subdivisions)

**(a)** It is an unlawful employment practice for an employer, because of the age of any person 40 years of age or older, to discharge the person from employment or to discriminate against the person in compensation or in the terms, conditions or privileges of employment.

**(f)** It is an unlawful employment practice for an employer to discharge or otherwise discriminate against any person because the person has opposed any practice forbidden by this section or has filed a complaint, testified or assisted in any proceeding under this part.

### Columbia Code of Civil Procedure (selected sections)

**§ 2101. Scope of discovery.**
(a) Unless otherwise limited by order of the court, a party may obtain discovery of any matter, not privileged, that is relevant to any party's claim or defense and proportional to the needs of the case. In deciding proportionality, the court considers the importance of the issues at stake, the amount in controversy, the parties' relative access to relevant information, the parties' resources, the importance of the discovery in resolving the issues, and whether the burden or expense of the proposed discovery outweighs its likely benefit. Information need not be admissible in evidence to be discoverable.
(b) The court shall limit discovery that is unreasonably cumulative or duplicative, or that can be obtained from another source that is more convenient, less burdensome or less expensive.

**§ 2102. Special interrogatories.**
(a) A party may propound to any other party no more than 35 special interrogatories without a declaration of necessity.
(b) Each interrogatory shall be full and complete in itself. No interrogatory shall contain subparts, or a compound, conjunctive or disjunctive question. An interrogatory that asks for the identity of a person by name, job title and last known business address is not compound for that reason.
(c) In the first paragraph immediately below the title of the case, a set of interrogatories shall state the identity of the propounding party, the set number and the identity of the responding party. No preface or instruction shall be included with a set of interrogatories. A term specially defined in a set of interrogatories shall be typed in capital letters wherever it appears.
(d) An interrogatory may ask for facts, for the identity of persons with knowledge of facts, and for the facts on which a party bases a contention. An interrogatory is not objectionable because the answer would involve an opinion or contention that relates to fact or to the application of law to fact.

**§ 2103. Requests for production.**
(a) A party may demand that any other party produce documents and electronically stored information in the responding party's possession, custody or control. There is no numerical limit on requests for production.
(b) Each request shall designate the documents to be produced either by specifically describing each individual item or by reasonably particularizing each category of item. A request for electronically stored information that does not identify a subject matter, a time period and the custodians or sources to be searched will ordinarily not be reasonably particularized.
(c) A request may specify the form in which electronically stored information is to be produced, including its native format with metadata.
(d) A party that withholds a document on a claim of privilege or work product shall expressly make the claim and provide sufficient information to permit the claim to be evaluated, including a privilege log identifying each document withheld, its date, author and recipients, and the privilege claimed.

**§ 2104. Protective orders.** On motion and for good cause, the court may make any order that justice requires to protect any party or other person from unwarranted annoyance, embarrassment, oppression, invasion of privacy, or undue burden or expense, including an order that: (1) discovery be had only on specified terms, including limits on time period, custodians and search terms; (2) information be produced with identifying details redacted or replaced by anonymous identifiers; or (3) information be disclosed only to counsel, designated experts and the court, and used only for purposes of the action.

**§ 2105. Signature on discovery requests.** The signature of an attorney on a discovery request certifies that, to the best of the attorney's knowledge, information and belief formed after reasonable inquiry, the request is consistent with this chapter, is not made for any improper purpose such as to harass or to cause unnecessary delay or expense, and is neither unreasonable nor unduly burdensome given the needs of the case.

### *Ellery v. Northgate Freight Lines, Inc.* (Columbia Supreme Court, 2018)

Thomas Ellery, 57, managed a Northgate Freight Lines terminal for eleven years. In 2016 Northgate eliminated 22 of its 140 terminal-manager positions. Each of Northgate's four regional directors ranked the managers in his or her region using a form that weighted "efficiency metrics," "performance" and "leadership potential." Ellery was selected, and he sued under Government Code § 5520(a), alleging that age was a substantial motivating reason for his selection.

Ellery served requests for (1) the complete personnel files of all 140 terminal managers for the preceding ten years; (2) "all documents referring to the reduction in force"; (3) "all emails referring to plaintiff," without limit as to sender, recipient or date; and (4) the ranking forms, with the ages, of all managers ranked in his region. The trial court compelled production of everything. Northgate petitioned for a writ of mandate. We grant the petition in part.

**Relevance.** Direct evidence of discrimination is rare. Plaintiffs in reduction-in-force cases ordinarily prove discrimination circumstantially: by showing that the employer's stated selection criteria were not applied as stated, were applied differently to older and younger employees, or were themselves a pretext; by comparing the plaintiff with similarly situated younger employees who were retained; by statistical evidence about who was selected from the pool considered; and by statements of decision-makers reflecting bias. Employees are similarly situated when they were considered in the same selection pool, by the same decision-makers, under the same criteria. Discovery directed to these matters is relevant to the plaintiff's claim and to the employer's defense that its reasons were legitimate.

**Privacy.** Employees have a legally protected privacy interest in their personnel files, which often contain medical, financial and family information. That interest does not bar a plaintiff's discovery of comparator information, and the plaintiff's need does not automatically override it. The court balances (1) how directly the information bears on a claim or defense; (2) the seriousness of the intrusion, considering the kind of information sought; and (3) whether less intrusive means, such as anonymous identifiers, redaction, a protective order or a narrower category of documents, will meet the requesting party's legitimate need. A requesting party that builds those measures into its request is far more likely to obtain the discovery.

**Application.**
- *The ranking forms and ages for Ellery's region (request 4).* These go to the heart of the claim, and the intrusion is slight if names are replaced by identifiers and the key is held by counsel under a protective order. The trial court properly compelled them. Because the "performance" score was said to rest on prior evaluations, the court also properly compelled, under a protective order, the annual evaluations and disciplinary records, for the three years before the reduction, of the retained managers in Ellery's selection pool. Ellery was entitled to test whether the scores matched the underlying records.
- *The complete personnel files of all 140 managers for ten years (request 1).* Complete files contain medical-leave records, benefit elections, home addresses and family information with no bearing on the case. Selections were made region by region by different directors, so managers in other regions were not similarly situated. The order was overbroad and disproportionate. We note that the result might differ if a single decision-maker, or a company-wide directive, had shaped the selections in every region. In that event, at least aggregate data on the ages of those considered and selected company-wide could be relevant and proportional.
- *"All documents referring to the reduction in force" (request 2).* As framed, this request would sweep in benefits notices, real-estate files and press releases. It is not reasonably particularized. Documents describing the selection criteria and process, and the decision-makers' communications about the selections in Ellery's pool, are discoverable.
- *"All emails referring to plaintiff" (request 3).* Northgate has about 4,000 employees. A request reaching every mailbox, without limit of time, is not reasonably particularized and is disproportionate to the needs of a single-plaintiff case. In this court Ellery proposed a narrower alternative: the email of four named custodians (the regional director, the two vice presidents who approved the selections and the human resources manager), for the eight months surrounding the reduction, searched with agreed terms including his name, the project's name and the words "old," "retire," "young" and "fresh." That request is reasonably particularized and proportional, and the trial court shall order it on remand.

The petition is granted as to requests 1, 2 and 3, and denied as to request 4. The matter is remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

### *Halberd Industries, Inc. v. Superior Court (Vasquez)* (Columbia Court of Appeal, 2020)

Rosa Vasquez, a warehouse supervisor at Halberd Industries, Inc., complained to human resources that her manager made demeaning comments about women. Halberd retained the law firm Delmore LLP "to investigate the complaint and to advise Halberd regarding its legal obligations." A Delmore attorney interviewed nine employees, took notes, and delivered a written report with findings and recommendations to Halberd's general counsel, who forwarded a summary of the findings to the operations director. Six weeks later the operations director terminated Vasquez for "attendance." She sued for retaliation under Government Code § 5520(f).

To prove retaliation, a plaintiff must show that she engaged in protected activity, that she suffered an adverse employment action and that the two are causally linked. A causal link requires at a minimum that a person who made or influenced the decision knew of the protected activity. Close timing between the protected activity and the adverse action supports an inference of causation.

Halberd's answer alleged that it "promptly and thoroughly investigated plaintiff's complaint through independent counsel and acted in good faith on the results." Vasquez requested the Delmore notes and report, all communications between Halberd and Delmore, and all communications between Halberd and its litigation counsel, Ames & Cole LLP, concerning her. Halberd objected on grounds of attorney-client privilege and work product and produced nothing, not even a privilege log. The trial court ordered everything produced. Halberd petitioned for a writ of mandate.

**Privilege and work product.** Communications between an employer and outside counsel retained to investigate a complaint are privileged if the dominant purpose of the relationship was to obtain legal advice, rather than to have the lawyer perform a business function such as routine personnel administration. Delmore was retained to advise Halberd on its legal obligations, and the dominant purpose was legal advice. The attorney's notes, interview summaries and report are also attorney work product.

**Facts are not privileged.** The privilege protects communications, not facts. Whatever the status of the investigation file, Vasquez may learn by interrogatory who conducted the investigation, who was interviewed and when, what documents were collected and what action Halberd took in response. She may depose the witnesses about what they observed.

**Waiver by placing the investigation at issue.** An employer that relies on the adequacy of its investigation to defend a claim, for example to show that it took the complaint seriously and acted in good faith on the results, places the investigation at issue. It cannot use the investigation as a sword while holding the privilege as a shield. By pleading its investigation as a defense, Halberd waived the privilege and work-product protection for the investigation materials. These include the investigator's notes and interview summaries, the report and its findings, the documents the investigator reviewed, and communications transmitting the investigation's findings or recommendations to the people who made decisions about the plaintiff.

**Limits of the waiver.** The waiver extends no further than the defense requires. It does not reach Halberd's communications with its litigation counsel, Ames & Cole, which Halberd does not rely on. Nor does it reach advice Delmore gave on matters other than the investigation unless Halberd relies on that advice too. The trial court's order compelling those communications was error. An employer may avoid the waiver altogether by withdrawing the defense and stipulating that it will not rely on the investigation at trial. Halberd has not done so.

**Privilege log.** Halberd's blanket refusal to produce anything, without a log, did not comply with Code of Civil Procedure § 2103(d). On remand, Halberd shall produce a privilege log for every document it continues to withhold.

The petition is granted as to the communications with Ames & Cole LLP and any Delmore advice on matters other than the investigation, and is otherwise denied.

**MEMORANDUM**

**TO:** Elena Rivas, Partner

**RE:** *Marchand v. Varnell*: purpose and limits of our first interrogatories and requests for production

#### I. Approach

Every request is tied to what we must prove, or to a defense Varnell has pleaded.
- **Age discrimination (§ 5520(a)).** Varnell may not discharge her "because of" her age. Like the plaintiff in *Ellery v. Northgate Freight Lines*, we allege that age was a substantial motivating reason for her selection. In a reduction in force, *Ellery* says this is proved circumstantially: criteria not applied as stated, or used as a pretext; comparison with similarly situated younger employees who were retained; statistics about the selection pool; and decision-makers' statements.
- **Retaliation (§ 5520(f)).** *Halberd Industries v. Superior Court* requires protected activity (the July 14 complaint), an adverse action (the layoff) and a causal link. The link requires that a decision-maker knew of the complaint, and it is supported by close timing.
- **Varnell's defenses.** Our requests target the First (legitimate reasons, "objective, uniformly applied" criteria), Second (investigation and good faith) and Third (same decision) Affirmative Defenses.

*Ellery* says a party that builds privacy and burden limits into its requests is "far more likely" to obtain the discovery, so the limits are in the requests themselves: identifiers, redaction, Confidential designation, named custodians, date ranges and privilege carve-outs.

**Form.** Each set opens with the identifying paragraph that § 2102(c) requires for interrogatories. The interrogatories have no preface or instructions, and defined terms appear in capitals. Each interrogatory asks a single question, which is why Haldane's and Lisowski's knowledge are asked separately. "IDENTIFY" asks only for name, job title and business address, which § 2102(b) says is not compound. We are using 8 of the 35 interrogatories § 2102(a) allows.

**Urgent.** Teams recordings are deleted after 180 days, so the June 3 recording will be deleted about November 30 (Lee memo), less than three weeks after we serve. We should send Ms. Whitaker a preservation demand today, covering the recording, the June 18 chat and the SharePoint workbook, and ask her to produce the recording early.

#### II. The requests

**Special Interrogatory 1 (who decided).** This identifies whose motives and knowledge matter. It also tests our choice of custodians for Request 5. It asks for Varnell managers' identities, which raises no privilege or privacy issue.

**Special Interrogatory 2 and Request 2 (timing).** These target retaliation causation and the Third Affirmative Defense. The complaint was made July 14. The Selection Summary shows scoring on August 21 and approval on August 28. Varnell says planning began in May. If Ms. Marchand's name first appears in a draft after July 14, timing supports causation (*Halberd*). If it appears in May, we learn that early.

SharePoint keeps a version history (Lee memo), so Request 2 seeks the one workbook in native format with metadata, as § 2103(c) permits. It is a specifically described item, so it is plainly proportional. Other employees' names are replaced by identifiers, as *Ellery* approved for ranking forms.

**Special Interrogatories 3–4, Requests 1 and 3 (the scores).** These attack the First Affirmative Defense as pretext: criteria "not applied as stated" (*Ellery*).
- *Performance score.* The template says Performance is the average of the two most recent reviews, with Exceeds counting as 4–5. Haldane rated her "Exceeds Expectations" in both 2024 and 2025, yet she received a 2. Varnell will likely rely on the 2023 warning. Interrogatory 4 forces it to commit, and the warning falls outside the two-review window.
- *Adaptability score.* "Adaptability" is the plant director's own assessment. Interrogatory 3 tests whether it is a proxy for age, given "digital natives" and "old guard." Contention interrogatories are expressly allowed (§ 2102(d)).
- *Request 1* seeks the criteria, the instructions and any Q&C target. These are the categories *Ellery* held discoverable when it rejected "all documents referring to the reduction in force." The request is limited by subject, to five months, and to Corporate HR and the three custodians, the sources § 2103(b) expects for electronically stored information.
- *Request 3* mirrors what *Ellery* compelled. It covers the reviews and disciplinary records, for the three review years before the layoff (2023–2025), of retained employees in the same pool, scored by the same director under the same criteria. That lets us compare how the retained 29- to 52-year-olds' reviews translated into Haldane's scores. It seeks two document types, not complete files. Names are replaced by identifiers, sensitive data is redacted, and the records are designated Confidential.

**Special Interrogatory 5 and Request 8 (replacement).** Varnell says her position was "eliminated" and her duties "absorbed" (position statement). Ms. Marchand reports that Tyler Voss, 34, became "Quality Lead" on October 1 and performs her audits. Replacement by a much younger employee undercuts the legitimate-reason defense and supplies a comparator. **Special Interrogatories 6–7 (knowledge).** Retaliation requires that a decision-maker knew of the complaint (*Halberd*). Varnell asserts that Haldane "was not aware" of the complaint when he scored her on August 21. But the investigator asked Ms. Marchand about Haldane's Teams message, and Ruth Abara expected Haldane and Lisowski to be interviewed before the August 12 close. A reasonable inference is that Haldane knew before he scored her. When someone learned a fact is itself a fact, and *Halberd* holds that facts are not privileged.

**Special Interrogatory 8 and Request 7 (the investigation).** These target the Second Affirmative Defense.
- *Interrogatory 8.* Under *Halberd*, who was interviewed is discoverable regardless of privilege.
- *Request 7.* Varnell pleads that counsel conducted "a thorough and impartial investigation" and that Varnell "acted reasonably and in good faith on the results," which is nearly the answer in *Halberd*. By relying on the investigation, Varnell has placed it at issue and waived privilege and work product for the notes, interview summaries, report, documents reviewed, and communications transmitting findings to decision-makers. It seeks no files.

**Request 5 (decision-makers' statements).** This targets bias evidence ("digital natives," "old guard") and communications about the complaint. It follows the model *Ellery* ordered: three named custodians (the scorer, the approver and HR), a five-month window and specific search terms. **Request 6 (the June 3 recording).** This is a specifically described item (§ 2103(b)), and it is direct evidence of Lisowski's statement.

#### III. Expected objections and our responses

1. **Privilege and work product** (Whitaker ¶ 1). We can concede the investigation was initially privileged under *Halberd*'s dominant-purpose test. The Second Affirmative Defense waives it, and offering only the "conclusion" is the sword-and-shield use *Halberd* forbids. Facts and identities are not privileged in any event. A blanket refusal without a log violates § 2103(d).
2. **Third-party privacy** (¶ 2). *Ellery* rejects a categorical bar. Comparator and pool data bear directly on the claim. The intrusion is limited by identifiers, redaction, narrow document types, and the Confidential designation and protective order (§ 2104(2)–(3)). Varnell itself disclosed the pool's ages in the severance attachment.
3. **Proportionality** (¶ 3). Each request is limited by custodians, dates and terms. The § 2101(a) factors favor us: the civil-rights issues are important; Varnell alone holds the information; and a 1,600-employee company has far greater resources than a laid-off employee whose lost salary alone is $121,000 a year.
4. **Other plants** (¶ 4). Lisowski's approval of every list, using a common template, brings Request 4 within *Ellery*'s exception, and we seek only anonymous age-and-selection data, with no names or key.
5. **Form objections.** Each interrogatory is a single, self-contained question that complies with § 2102.

We will serve the proposed stipulated protective order with the requests and meet and confer promptly on any objection.

**Common mistakes**
1. Compound or conjunctive interrogatories ("IDENTIFY each person who scored, reviewed or approved…"; "State all facts and identify all documents…"), or an instructions paragraph in the interrogatory set. § 2102(b)–(c) forbids both.
2. "All documents" or "all emails" with no custodians, dates or terms. *Ellery* and § 2103(b) treat these as unparticularized and disproportionate.
3. Demanding complete personnel files or the age of every employee, or giving up on comparator data because Varnell cited privacy. *Ellery* allows narrower, anonymized discovery.
4. Accepting the privilege objection to the investigation, or overreaching to litigation counsel's files. *Halberd* gives the waiver and its limits.
5. Asking for Lisowski's arrest and divorce records because the client wants them (§ 2105).
6. Missing the 180-day deletion of the June 3 recording.
