**RE:** Kiln & Clay Studio, LLC: advice letter on the Marigold Commons lease

Joanna Pak owns Kiln & Clay Studio, LLC, a pottery studio and shop that leases Unit 4 at Marigold Commons, a small retail center in Westbrook. She is the LLC's only member, and she personally guaranteed the lease. The roof over her studio has leaked repeatedly since last November. The landlord's construction of a restaurant next door has cut into her parking and, for a time, hid her sign. She has found a larger space at Harbor Row and wants to know whether she can get out of this lease. I met with her on Friday. The transcript and the documents she brought are in the File.

She asked three questions. In her words:

1. "Can I stop paying rent, or at least take my kiln repair out of the rent, until they fix the roof?"
2. "Can I just fix the roof myself and make them pay for it, and for what I've already lost?"
3. "Can I move to the Harbor Row space in January and walk away from this lease without owing rent here?"

Please draft a **letter for my signature to Ms. Pak** that:

- answers each of her three questions, **in that order**, with your best judgment of the likely outcome and an honest statement of the risks;
- then sets out her **realistic options**, measured against the goals she described in the interview, and gives **my recommendation** (she asked for one), making clear that the decision is hers;
- explains **in dollars** what Kiln & Clay, and Ms. Pak personally under her guaranty, could owe if the company leaves early and a court later decides it was not entitled to. Use only figures in the File and show the arithmetic; and
- ends with a short list of **next steps with dates**, including the written notices that should be sent. Describe the notices; do not draft them.

**Format and tone:**
- Ms. Pak is a ceramic artist with no legal training ("I don't speak lawyer"). Write in plain English, with short paragraphs and headings phrased as questions. Explain any legal term you must use.
- Mention a Columbia statute or case only where it helps her understand or trust a conclusion. No case-by-case discussion; a handful of citations at most.
- **Do not include a separate statement of facts.** You may briefly confirm the key facts you relied on.
- Address the letter to Ms. Pak only.
- Ms. Pak asked me for "a letter I can show Curtis so he knows I'm serious." **Do not write that letter.** This is a confidential advice letter, and it must be candid about weaknesses. Our advice is confidential and privileged; sharing it with the landlord could waive that protection and would hand the landlord a list of our weak points. Include one or two sentences telling her why she should not share it.

**Do not address:**
- the dispute over the common-area (CAM) charge reconciliation;
- the noise complaints about Still Point Yoga;
- any claim against Ember & Oak or Kestrel Builders;
- the student's fall on February 9 (no claim has been made);
- the business terms of the Harbor Row letter of intent (rent, free rent, allowances). You may use its dates; or
- taxes or bankruptcy.

Assume that the lease and the guaranty are valid and enforceable as written, and that Kiln & Clay has paid all rent due through September 2026.

### Columbia Civil Code (selected sections)

**§ 1962. Breach and abandonment by tenant of nonresidential property; landlord's remedies.**
(a) If a tenant of nonresidential property breaches the lease and abandons the property before the end of the term, or if the landlord terminates the lease because of the tenant's breach, the landlord may recover from the tenant:
(1) the unpaid rent that had been earned at the time of termination;
(2) the amount by which the unpaid rent for the balance of the term after termination exceeds the amount of the rental loss for the same period that the tenant proves could have been reasonably avoided; and
(3) any other amount necessary to compensate the landlord for the detriment proximately caused by the tenant's failure to perform its obligations under the lease, including the reasonable cost of reletting.
(b) Efforts by the landlord to relet the property do not by themselves constitute an acceptance of the tenant's surrender of the property.
(c) A lease may be terminated before the end of its term by the landlord's acceptance of the tenant's surrender of the property. A surrender agreed in writing by the landlord and the tenant ends the tenant's obligation to pay rent accruing after the date of surrender, unless the agreement provides otherwise.

**§ 1963. Nonresidential leases; implied covenants.**
(a) In a lease of nonresidential property, no warranty that the property is habitable, or fit for the tenant's intended use, is implied. The landlord's duties to repair and maintain the property are those stated in the lease.
(b) Every lease, including a lease of nonresidential property, contains an implied covenant that the landlord, and persons claiming under the landlord, will not disturb the tenant's quiet enjoyment of the property during the term. This covenant may not be waived.

**§ 1964. Guarantors of leases.** The guarantor of a tenant's obligations under a lease is liable, within the limits of the guaranty, for each obligation that the tenant owes under the lease. The guarantor is exonerated to the extent that the tenant's obligation is excused, discharged or satisfied.

### *Harrow Mercantile Co. v. Vandermeer Properties, Inc.* (Columbia Supreme Court, 1999)

Harrow Mercantile Co. operated a hardware store under a ten-year lease from Vandermeer Properties, Inc. The lease required Vandermeer to maintain the roof and the store's air-conditioning system. It required Harrow to pay rent "without deduction or offset." In June of the fourth year the air-conditioning failed. Vandermeer did not repair it for four months. Harrow kept the store open but stopped paying rent, writing to Vandermeer that rent would resume "when the repairs are made." After two months of nonpayment, Vandermeer gave notice of default, terminated the lease and sued for possession and damages. Harrow argued that its duty to pay rent depended on Vandermeer's performance of the repair covenant, so that Vandermeer's breach excused the rent. The trial court ruled for Vandermeer, and the Court of Appeal affirmed. We affirm the termination and remand for trial of Harrow's counterclaim.

**Independent covenants.** Under the long-standing rule, the covenants in a lease are independent: the tenant's promise to pay rent does not depend on the landlord's performance of its other promises. A landlord's breach gives the tenant a claim for damages, but it does not by itself suspend the rent. Some courts have relaxed this rule for residential housing. We decline to extend that relaxation to commercial leases. Business tenants can bargain for rent abatement, offset rights or a right to terminate, and the lease is where they must find them. Harrow's lease said the opposite: rent was payable "without deduction or offset." Harrow's withholding was therefore a default, and Vandermeer's termination was valid. A tenant that withholds rent, even in good faith, bears the risk that it is wrong.

**The tenant's remedies.** This does not leave a commercial tenant without a remedy. A tenant whose landlord breaches a repair covenant may: (1) remain in possession, pay the rent, and recover damages by suit or counterclaim; (2) if the breach is serious enough to amount to a constructive eviction, vacate the premises and be relieved of further rent; and (3) exercise any self-help or offset right that the lease provides, on the lease's terms. Damages for breach of a repair covenant include the difference between the rental value of the premises as promised and as they actually were, and other losses proximately caused by the breach, including lost profits proved with reasonable certainty. A tenant that pays rent "under protest," or with a written reservation of its rights, does not waive its claim for damages.

**Constructive eviction requires abandonment.** Harrow asked us, in the alternative, to hold that it had been "partially" constructively evicted and owed reduced rent while it stayed. We decline. A tenant cannot both keep the premises and claim that it has been evicted from them. Columbia does not recognize a partial constructive eviction for a tenant that remains in possession of the whole premises.

**Interpretation.** A commercial lease is a contract and is read according to the plain meaning of its terms. Ambiguous terms are construed against the party that drafted them. Clauses limiting a landlord's liability are enforceable between commercial parties. But they are construed strictly against the landlord and do not reach losses they do not clearly cover.

**Disposition.** Harrow's termination was proper, and Harrow is liable for the rent due under the lease. But Harrow's counterclaim for damages caused by the four-month air-conditioning failure survived termination, and the trial court erred in dismissing it. Judgment affirmed in part and reversed in part, and the case is remanded for trial of the counterclaim, any recovery on which shall be offset against the amount Harrow owes.

### *Sorensen Dental Group v. Pinecrest Plaza, LLC* (Columbia Court of Appeal, 2014)

Sorensen Dental Group leased a ground-floor suite in Pinecrest Plaza for a dental practice. The lease required Pinecrest to keep the roof and exterior walls "watertight." It also contained a general clause requiring notices to be "in writing," but it prescribed no method of delivery and did not make any form of notice a condition of the tenant's remedies.

Beginning in October 2010, water came through the ceiling over two of the practice's five treatment rooms in heavy rain. Sorensen's office manager called and emailed Pinecrest's building manager after each leak. Pinecrest's maintenance staff patched the roof four times, and the leaks returned each time. Over the winter Sorensen had to close the affected rooms on eleven days and cancel 140 appointments, though the practice stayed open in its other rooms. In January 2011 the building manager wrote that a "permanent roof solution" would follow "after the rainy season." By May nothing had been done. In June 2011, eight months after the first leak, Sorensen gave written notice that it would vacate. It moved out in August 2011, after building out a new suite elsewhere, and stopped paying rent. Pinecrest sued for the rent for the rest of the term. The trial court found that Sorensen had been constructively evicted and entered judgment for Sorensen. We affirm.

**Elements.** A tenant is constructively evicted, and excused from further rent, when (1) the landlord's act or omission breaches a duty the landlord owes under the lease or under the covenant of quiet enjoyment; (2) the breach substantially interferes with the tenant's beneficial use of the premises for the purposes for which they were leased; (3) the tenant gives the landlord notice, and the landlord fails to cure within a reasonable time; and (4) the tenant vacates within a reasonable time after the landlord's failure.

**Substantial interference.** Whether interference is substantial is measured against the use the lease permits. Treatment rooms are the heart of a dental practice. Recurrent, unpredictable interruptions of a tenant's core use can be substantial even though the tenant stays open between them. Not every leak is an eviction, however. An occasional leak into a storage area, or a condition that inconveniences the tenant without impairing its core use, is not enough.

**Notice and cure.** Pinecrest argued that Sorensen's calls and emails were not proper notice. Where the landlord actually knew of the condition and responded to the tenant's complaints, and the lease did not prescribe a method of notice or make formal notice a condition of the tenant's remedies, informal notice is sufficient. The purpose of notice is to give the landlord a chance to repair, and Pinecrest had every chance. Patches that predictably fail are not a cure. A landlord's ongoing, good-faith efforts that are reasonably calculated to correct the problem may lengthen the time that is reasonable for a cure. Promises without action do not.

**Time to vacate.** Pinecrest argued that Sorensen waived any eviction by staying eight months. A tenant does not lose its claim by remaining while the landlord promises repairs and the tenant reasonably waits for them. The reasonable time to vacate runs from when the tenant knew or should have known that the landlord would not cure. It includes the time reasonably needed to locate and prepare substitute premises. Sorensen gave notice soon after the promised spring repair failed to materialize, and moved within two months after building out its new suite. That was reasonable. By contrast, a tenant that stays on for its own convenience after it is clear that the landlord will not cure, and takes no step toward leaving, waives the constructive eviction, although it keeps its claim for damages.

Affirmed.

### *Quan v. Bellhaven Center Associates* (Columbia Court of Appeal, 2021)

Lily Quan's company, Lotus Nail Studio, Inc., leased a store in the Bellhaven shopping center for five years. Quan personally guaranteed the lease. The lease allowed Bellhaven to "temporarily close portions of the common areas as reasonably necessary for repairs, alterations or construction, using reasonable efforts to minimize interference with Tenant's business." It also required each party to give notice of any claimed default by certified mail and allowed the defaulting party thirty days to cure.

In the third year Bellhaven repaved the parking lot and renovated the center's storefronts over five months. It closed about half of the parking at a time, in phases, and kept scaffolding in front of Lotus's store for six weeks. Lotus's revenue fell about 30 percent. Quan complained by telephone and then sent an email to the property manager. Nine days after the email, Lotus moved out and stopped paying rent, claiming constructive eviction. Bellhaven relet the store seven months later at the same rent and sued Lotus and Quan under Civil Code § 1962. The trial court entered judgment against both. We affirm.

**Interference the lease permits.** The covenant of quiet enjoyment cannot be waived (§ 1963(b)), but the parties may define what the landlord is permitted to do. Interference that the lease expressly permits is not a breach of the lease or of the covenant, because the tenant bargained for it. Bellhaven's closures were temporary, phased and tied to construction that the clause contemplated. A decline in the tenant's revenue does not by itself show that the landlord exceeded the clause.

We do not hold that a reserved-rights clause licenses any interference. A landlord that closes more of the common areas, or for longer, than the work reasonably requires, or that refuses feasible and inexpensive measures to reduce the interference that the clause obliges it to consider, acts outside the clause. Its interference may then support a claim for damages or, if substantial, a constructive eviction. Quan offered no evidence that Bellhaven refused any such measure.

**Notice before vacating.** Where the lease prescribes how notice of a default must be given and allows a period to cure, a tenant that relies on the landlord's breach to end its own obligations must give notice in that manner and allow the cure period, or at least a reasonable time to cure, before vacating. Lotus did neither: it sent an email rather than certified mail, and it left nine days later. *Sorensen Dental Group v. Pinecrest Plaza, LLC* (2014) is not to the contrary. There, the lease prescribed no method of notice, and the landlord had actual knowledge for months and repeatedly promised repairs.

**Damages and the guaranty.** Lotus was not constructively evicted, so it abandoned the premises in breach of the lease. Bellhaven was entitled to the rent earned before termination and to the rent for the balance of the term, less the rental loss that Lotus proved could reasonably have been avoided (§ 1962(a)). The burden of proving avoidable loss is on the tenant. Lotus's broker testified that comparable stores leased within three months. Bellhaven's evidence showed a soft market and steady marketing efforts. The trial court was entitled to find that seven months was a reasonable time to relet. Because Lotus's obligation was not excused, Quan is liable as guarantor within the limits of her guaranty (§ 1964).

Affirmed.

**Length note.** This model runs to about 3,700 words, more than anyone can type in 50 minutes; it is fuller than an exam answer so that it shows every refinement. An answer scoring 70 or more can be about 2,200–2,600 words. Keep each answer short, and spend the words on Part 3 (the four elements and the exposure table) and on the options.

**Common mistakes**
1. Advising the client to withhold rent, or to put rent in escrow, because the landlord breached first. *Harrow* and § 4 make the covenants independent, and § 12(c) bars offset.
2. Applying an implied warranty of habitability or "repair and deduct" from residential law. § 1963(a) rules out implied warranties in commercial leases; only the lease's own § 12 gives a self-help right.
3. Treating the construction as an easy constructive eviction. Section 8 and *Quan* make it weak, and no formal notice has yet been given.
4. Missing the timing trap: staying through the holidays for the business's convenience can waive constructive eviction (*Sorensen*).
5. Giving only the company's exposure and ignoring the personal guaranty, or the reverse; or not showing the arithmetic.
