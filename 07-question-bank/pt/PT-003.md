### Columbia Probate Code (selected sections)

**Part A. Restraints on beneficial interests**

**§ 7101. Spendthrift restraint.** If the trust instrument provides that a beneficiary's interest in income or principal is not subject to voluntary or involuntary transfer, the beneficiary's interest may not be transferred and is not subject to enforcement of a money judgment until it is paid to the beneficiary, except as provided in §§ 7103 and 7104.

**§ 7102. Discretionary trusts.** If the trust instrument provides that the trustee shall pay to or for the benefit of a beneficiary so much of the income or principal as the trustee in the trustee's discretion sees fit, a transferee or creditor of the beneficiary may not compel the trustee to pay any amount that may be paid only in the exercise of the trustee's discretion.

**§ 7103. Judgments for support.**
(a) Notwithstanding §§ 7101 and 7102, a judgment for the support of the beneficiary's child, spouse or former spouse may be enforced against (1) payments the trustee is required to make to or for the benefit of the beneficiary, and (2) payments the trustee, in the exercise of its discretion, determines to make to or for the benefit of the beneficiary.
(b) The court may order the trustee to satisfy all or part of the support judgment out of those payments, as the court determines is equitable and reasonable in the circumstances.
(c) This section does not authorize a court to compel a trustee to exercise its discretion to make a payment.

**§ 7104. Other judgment creditors.** Notwithstanding § 7101, on petition of a judgment creditor of the beneficiary, the court may order the trustee to pay the creditor up to 25 percent of each payment that the trustee is required to make, or determines in its discretion to make, to or for the benefit of the beneficiary. The court may not order payment of any amount it finds necessary for the support of the beneficiary and of the persons the beneficiary is required to support.

**Part B. Donative transfers to care custodians**

**§ 8401. Definitions.**
(a) "Care custodian" means a person who provides health or social services to a dependent adult for compensation.
(b) "Dependent adult" means a person who, when the instrument was executed, was 65 years of age or older and either (1) was unable to provide properly for his or her personal needs for physical health, food, clothing or shelter, or (2) because of deficits in mental function, had substantial difficulty managing his or her own financial resources or resisting fraud or undue influence.
(c) "Independent attorney" means an attorney who (1) did not draft the instrument and is not a partner, associate or employee of the law firm that drafted it, and (2) has no legal, business, financial, professional or personal relationship with the transferee.

**§ 8402. Presumption of fraud or undue influence.** A provision of an instrument making a donative transfer to a care custodian of a dependent adult is presumed to be the product of fraud or undue influence if the instrument was executed while the care custodian was providing services to the transferor, or within 90 days before or after that period. The presumption may be rebutted only by clear and convincing evidence.

**§ 8403. Exceptions.** Section 8402 does not apply to:
(a) a transfer to a person related to the transferor by blood or marriage within the fourth degree;
(b) a transfer of property worth not more than $5,000, if the transferor's estate is worth more than $150,000; or
(c) an instrument reviewed by an independent attorney who (1) counsels the transferor, outside the presence of the transferee and of any heir or other beneficiary, about the nature and consequences of the intended transfer, including its effect on the transferor's heirs and other beneficiaries; (2) attempts to determine whether the transfer is the product of fraud or undue influence; and (3) signs and delivers to the transferor a certificate stating that the attorney has done so and has concluded that the transfer is not the product of fraud or undue influence.

**§ 8404. Effect of failure.** If a donative transfer fails under this Part, the instrument operates as if the transferee had predeceased the transferor without spouse or issue.

**Part C. No-contest clauses**

**§ 8501. Definitions.**
(a) "No-contest clause" means a provision in an instrument that, if enforced, would penalize a beneficiary who files a pleading in any court.
(b) "Direct contest" means a pleading that alleges the invalidity of a protected instrument, or of one or more of its terms, on any of these grounds: forgery; lack of due execution; lack of capacity; menace, duress, fraud or undue influence; revocation; or disqualification of a beneficiary under § 8402.
(c) "Protected instrument" means the instrument that contains the no-contest clause, and any other instrument in existence when the clause was executed that the clause expressly identifies.
(d) "Pleading" includes a petition, complaint, cross-complaint, objection, answer or claim.

**§ 8502. When enforced.** A no-contest clause shall be enforced only against:
(a) a direct contest brought without probable cause;
(b) a pleading challenging a transfer of property on the ground that it was not the transferor's property at the time of the transfer, but only if the no-contest clause expressly provides for that application; and
(c) the filing of a creditor's claim, or the prosecution of an action based on it, but only if the no-contest clause expressly provides for that application.

**§ 8503. Probable cause.** Probable cause exists if, when the contestant filed the contest, the facts known to the contestant would cause a reasonable person to believe there is a reasonable likelihood that the requested relief will be granted after an opportunity for further investigation or discovery.

**§ 8504. Construction.** A no-contest clause shall be strictly construed. It shall not be enforced against a pleading that seeks only to interpret the instrument, to challenge the conduct of a fiduciary, or to obtain an accounting.

### *Estate of Thornbury* (Columbia Court of Appeal, 2016)

Walter Thornbury's revocable trust directed that, on his death, the trust estate be distributed "to my children in equal shares." He had two daughters with his wife, June. He also raised June's son from an earlier marriage, Benjamin, from the age of four. When Benjamin was ten, Walter tried to adopt him, but Benjamin's biological father refused to consent. Benjamin turned eighteen in 1991. Walter never sought to adopt him as an adult, although he lived until 2014. Benjamin petitioned to be treated as one of Walter's "children." The probate court denied the petition. We affirm.

Probate Code § 1215 provides that terms of relationship in an instrument, such as "children," "issue" or "descendants," include the persons who would be treated as such for intestate succession, unless the instrument shows a contrary intent. Under § 6455, a stepchild is treated as the stepparent's child for intestate succession only if (1) the relationship began during the stepchild's minority and continued throughout their joint lifetimes, and (2) clear and convincing evidence shows that the stepparent would have adopted the stepchild but for a legal barrier. The legal barrier must have continued until the stepparent's death. A barrier that ends, such as a parent's refusal to consent that loses its force when the child becomes an adult, does not satisfy the statute.

The first requirement is met: the relationship began when Benjamin was four and lasted until Walter's death. But the father's refusal stopped being a barrier in 1991, when Benjamin became an adult and could have been adopted without that consent. Walter lived 23 more years and did not adopt him. Benjamin therefore is not Walter's child for intestate succession.

Benjamin offered testimony that Walter often called him "my son" and said that Benjamin was his son "as much as the girls are" his daughters. We do not doubt the affection. But the trust used a word whose meaning the statute supplies, and nothing in the instrument suggested a broader meaning. Statements of affection do not show that the word "children" in the trust meant something other than what the Legislature has said it means. A settlor who wishes to include a stepchild can easily do so, by naming the stepchild or by defining the class to include him. The same construction applies to gifts to "descendants" or "issue": a stepchild's children are the settlor's descendants for this purpose only if the stepchild is the settlor's child.

Affirmed.

### *In re Marchetti Family Trust* (Columbia Supreme Court, 2019)

Elena Marchetti's trust left one-half of her estate in a separate trust for her son, Leo, with his sister, Carla, as trustee. The trustee was to pay to or for Leo's benefit "so much of the income and principal as the trustee, in her sole and absolute discretion, may determine." The trust contained a spendthrift provision. It stated no purpose, and it made no provision for Leo's children.

Leo was an alcoholic. For four years after Elena's death, Carla made no distributions at all, although Leo was unemployed, was twice evicted and sought residential treatment he could not afford. Carla testified that she wanted to "teach him a lesson" and that "Mom would have wanted him to hit bottom." Leo petitioned for review of the trustee's conduct. Leo's former wife, Teresa, who held a judgment for child support with arrears of $48,000, petitioned for an order under § 7103 directing the trustee to pay the support from any distributions. The probate court found that Carla had abused her discretion, directed her to pay Leo's rent and treatment costs directly to the providers, and ordered one-third of those payments applied to the support arrears. The Court of Appeal affirmed. So do we.

**"Sole and absolute discretion."** Words such as "sole," "absolute" or "uncontrolled" enlarge a trustee's discretion. They do not release the trustee from fiduciary duty. A trustee with absolute discretion must still act honestly, in good faith and for the purposes of the trust. A court will not substitute its judgment for the trustee's good-faith judgment. It will, however, intervene when the trustee acts in bad faith, for a purpose foreign to the trust, or without exercising judgment at all. A settlor cannot place a trustee entirely beyond review, but a settlor can greatly strengthen the trustee's hand by stating the trust's purposes and the factors the trustee should weigh. A trustee who considers those factors in good faith will rarely be second-guessed. Elena's instrument stated no purposes, which left the trustee with little guidance and the court with little to measure her conduct against. Carla's refusal to make any distribution, for the stated purpose of punishing a destitute beneficiary, was not an exercise of judgment for the trust's purposes. It was an abuse of discretion.

**The support claim.** Section 7103(c) means that a support claimant cannot force a trustee to make a distribution. But once the trustee makes a payment "to or for the benefit of" the beneficiary, § 7103(a) allows the court to reach it. Payments made directly to a landlord or a treatment program for the beneficiary's housing or care are payments "for the benefit of" the beneficiary, just as much as cash handed to him. The spendthrift provision does not change this, because § 7101 is expressly subject to § 7103. In deciding what is "equitable and reasonable," the probate court properly considered Leo's own need for housing and treatment, and it ordered only one-third of the payments applied to the arrears.

The former wife also argued that, because Elena loved her grandchildren, the trust should be read as one for their support. We disagree. The only beneficiary of the trust during Leo's lifetime was Leo. A settlor who wishes to provide for a beneficiary's children may do so directly, by naming them as beneficiaries. What the settlor gives the children is theirs, not the parent's, and the parent's creditors have no claim to it.

Affirmed.

### *Estate of Delacroix* (Columbia Court of Appeal, 2021)

Martin Delacroix, a widower aged 81, amended his trust to leave $100,000 to Rosa Imbert, a housekeeper he had paid for six years to clean and cook two days a week. He executed the amendment while she was working for him. His nephews challenged the gift under § 8402. The probate court found that Delacroix was not a "dependent adult," so the presumption did not apply, and it found that the nephews had not proved undue influence. We affirm.

A person aged 65 or older is a dependent adult under § 8401(b) only if he was unable to provide properly for his personal needs for physical health, food, clothing or shelter, or, because of deficits in mental function, had substantial difficulty managing his finances or resisting undue influence. Age alone is not enough, and neither is the fact that the transferor pays someone to help around the house. Delacroix drove himself, shopped, managed his own investments, paid his own bills and walked two miles a day. He hired Imbert for convenience, not because he could not care for himself. A transferor who depends on a paid helper to manage his medications, prepare his meals, provide his transportation or handle his financial affairs presents a different case.

Imbert argues in the alternative that a certificate of independent review signed by attorney Paul Grady takes the gift outside § 8402 under § 8403(c). Because the presumption does not apply, we need not decide that question. We note, however, that Grady was a partner in the firm that drafted the amendment, and that Imbert was in the room for part of his meeting with Delacroix. Section 8403(c) requires counseling by an independent attorney, as § 8401(c) defines that term, outside the presence of the transferee. A certificate obtained in those circumstances would not satisfy the statute.

Affirmed.

Dear Mr. Nygaard:

Enclosed is a draft of Article Five of your restated trust, the part that says who receives what after your death. **In short, the draft does almost everything you asked.** Megan, Peter and Dana share equally. Peter's third stays in a protected trust run by Megan, which can also pay for Ellie's college. Aurelia receives $60,000. If none of your family survives you, the Calloway Public Library inherits. Columbia law limits three of your wishes, and for each one this letter explains the closest arrangement the law allows. Before you sign, you will also need **one private meeting with a separate lawyer** about Aurelia's gift. The steps are listed at the end of this letter.

**1. Will Dana be treated exactly like Megan and Peter?**

Yes, but only because the draft names her. Under Columbia law, "children" in a trust means children as the law defines them. A stepchild counts only if the stepparent would have adopted her but for a legal obstacle that lasted until the stepparent's death. You tried to adopt Dana when she was 12, and Gary Whitlock refused. But his refusal stopped being an obstacle once Dana turned 18 (and he died in 2010), so the law would not treat her as your child, even though she calls you Dad. A Columbia court left a stepson out of a gift to "my children" for exactly that reason, even though his stepfather always called him his son. Your 2004 trust left everything to "the Settlors' children," meaning yours and Ruth's, wording that could be read to include Dana as Ruth's daughter. Now that you are the only settlor, that wording would no longer protect her. The draft therefore names Megan, Peter and Dana as your "Children" and says that Dana and her sons are to be treated as if she had been born to you.

**2. How does the draft handle Megan, Dana, Aurelia and the Library?**

- **Megan and Dana** receive their thirds outright. If either dies before you, her share goes to her children.
- **Aurelia** receives $60,000, whether or not she still works for you when you die. If she dies before you, or if the gift fails for the reason explained in section 4, the money goes into your children's shares, not to her family.
- **The Calloway Public Library** inherits only if none of your children or their descendants survives you.
- A person must outlive you by **30 days** to inherit. Anything for a grandchild under 25 is held in trust until that age under another part of your trust.

**3. Peter's share: what the trust can and cannot protect**

Peter's third will be held in "Peter's Trust," with Megan as trustee. Megan may pay for Peter's housing, health care and support, and for Ellie's education. No one can force her to make any particular payment, although she must use her judgment honestly (see "Can Megan's decisions be 'final'?" below). She can pay landlords, doctors and schools directly, so that large sums never pass through Peter's hands. Peter's own email to you says he would rather Megan handled the money, so this should not come as a surprise to him. When Peter dies, whatever is left goes to Ellie (and to any other children or grandchildren Peter may have); if Peter's line has ended, it goes to Megan and Dana in equal shares, or to their children. If Peter dies before you, his third goes to Ellie instead, held in trust until she is 25.

*Creditors.* The draft includes the "spendthrift" protection Columbia law allows. No creditor can force Megan to pay anything, and Peter cannot sign away his share. Two limits cannot be drafted away:
- **Child support.** If Megan decides to pay something to Peter or for his benefit, including rent paid straight to his landlord, a court may order a fair part of that payment applied to his unpaid child support (about $31,400 at the end of September).
- **Other creditors, such as Quickline (its judgment is $22,640).** A court may order up to 25 percent of each such payment paid to the creditor, but never money Peter needs for his basic support.

So Corinne and Quickline can reach only part of what Megan chooses to pay for Peter, never the trust itself. You may also find it some comfort that the support Corinne is owed is support for Ellie.

*Ellie's college.* Here the protection is strongest. The draft makes Ellie a beneficiary of Peter's Trust and lets Megan pay her school costs directly, without waiting until Ellie is 25. Money spent on Ellie belongs to Ellie, not to Peter, so Peter's creditors have no claim to it.

*If Peter relapses.* Peter has been in recovery since February 2024, and I hope this part is never used. You wrote that you want him "cut off," but not "on the street." Under the draft, if Megan concludes in good faith that Peter is gambling again, no money goes to Peter himself until he has stayed in recovery for 12 months in a row. In the meantime Megan may pay only his rent, food, medical care and treatment, directly to the providers, and Ellie's school payments continue. I recommend this over a permanent "gambled once, loses everything" rule, which would invite a court fight over whether he gambled and could leave him with nothing for treatment. If you prefer the stricter rule, I will draft it. That choice is yours.

*Can Megan's decisions be "final"?* Not entirely. Columbia's highest court has held that even a trustee with "sole and absolute" discretion must act honestly and for the trust's purposes. But the same court said that a settlor greatly strengthens the trustee's hand by writing down the purposes and the factors to weigh. The draft does that, and it makes Megan's good-faith decisions binding on everyone. As long as she follows your instructions honestly, a judge is very unlikely to overrule her.

**4. The gift to Aurelia: an important step before you sign**

Columbia law presumes that a gift to a paid caregiver resulted from improper pressure when the giver is a "dependent adult": someone 65 or older who cannot properly meet basic needs such as health care and meals on his own, or who has trouble managing money because of a mental decline. Your mind is sharp; Dr. Okoye's letter confirms it. But it also confirms that you cannot sort your own medications, drive, or safely shop and cook, and Aurelia does those things for you. A Columbia court held that the rule did not apply to a man who drove himself and ran his own affairs, but it said that someone who relies on a caregiver for medications, meals and driving "presents a different case." That describes you, so a court would very likely apply the rule.

If the rule applies, Aurelia could keep the gift only by proving to a judge, by "clear and convincing" evidence, that no one pressured you. That would mean an expensive court fight, and some facts would be used against her: she made your appointment with us, drove you to it and came into the meeting with you; she once said that people should remember those who look after them (even though, as you told me, she was speaking about her aunt); and she has since called our office to ask whether "the gift for me" is in the papers. If she lost, the $60,000 would go to your children.

**There is a simple way to avoid all of this.** If you meet privately with an independent lawyer who explains the gift and its effect on your children, and that lawyer signs a certificate that the gift is your own free choice, the rule does not apply. Two points matter:
- **The lawyer must be truly independent.** That rules out anyone from our firm and anyone with a family, business or personal tie to Aurelia. Her nephew, Daniel Santos, cannot do it, however kind the offer. We will send you three names.
- **Aurelia should not drive you to the meeting, sit in on it, or attend the signing.** Please ask someone else to bring you, or we will arrange a taxi.

The only other way around the rule is to limit the gift to $5,000 or less, which would not carry out your wish.

**5. "Anyone who fights this trust gets nothing"**

The draft includes this, as far as Columbia law allows. Anyone who receives something under your trust will lose it if he or she:
- challenges the trust in court (for example, claiming that you were pressured) **without a reasonable basis**;
- claims that property in the trust, such as your house, was not yours to give; or
- files a claim against your estate as a creditor, **including a claim for wages from the hardware store**. The law allows this only if the clause says so expressly, and the draft does. Peter has written that he will not fight the family over money, but the clause covers it anyway.

Three limits apply:
- **Reasonable grounds.** The law does not allow someone who has reasonable grounds to be punished for challenging whether the trust is valid. This is one more reason to complete the independent-lawyer step: with the certificate in place, it is much harder for anyone to claim reasonable grounds to attack Aurelia's gift.
- **Ordinary requests.** Asking a court to explain unclear wording, or to review how the trustee is managing the trust, is never penalized.
- **Joan.** The clause cannot affect Ruth's sister, because she receives nothing under your trust and so has nothing to lose.

If someone does lose a share, the draft treats that person as if he or she had died before you, so the share goes to that person's children (Aurelia's gift would instead go into your children's shares). For example, Ellie would still be provided for even if Peter brought a groundless challenge. If you would rather the share go to your other children, please tell me.
