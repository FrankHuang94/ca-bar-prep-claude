## Library

### Columbia Civil Code, Division 4, Part 6: Prompt Payment on Private Works (selected sections)

**§ 3260. Definitions.** In this Part: (a) "Contractor" means a contractor that has a direct contract with the owner of a work of improvement. (b) "Subcontractor" means a person that has a contract with the contractor to perform part of the work. (c) "Retention" means an amount withheld from progress payments to secure performance.

**§ 3261. Progress payments to subcontractors.**
(a) Within ten days after receiving a progress payment from the owner, a contractor shall pay each subcontractor the amount the owner paid on account of that subcontractor's work, less any retention the subcontract permits.
(b) This section applies notwithstanding any contrary term of a subcontract.

**§ 3262. Retention.**
(a) Within ten days after receiving all or any part of the retention from the owner, a contractor shall pay each subcontractor its share of the retention received on account of that subcontractor's work.
(b) This section does not determine whether a contractor's duty to pay retention to a subcontractor depends on the owner's payment. That question is governed by the subcontract.

**§ 3263. Good-faith disputes.**
(a) If a good-faith dispute exists between a contractor and a subcontractor about any amount due, the contractor may withhold from a payment required by this Part an amount not exceeding 150 percent of the estimated reasonable cost of the disputed work or claim.
(b) The contractor shall pay the balance as required by § 3261 or § 3262.

**§ 3264. Penalty; attorney's fees.**
(a) A contractor that violates § 3261 or § 3262 shall pay the subcontractor, in addition to the amount due, a penalty of 2 percent of the amount wrongfully withheld for each month that payment is withheld, in lieu of interest.
(b) In any action to collect amounts withheld in violation of this Part, the prevailing party is entitled to reasonable attorney's fees and costs.
(c) A term of a subcontract that waives this section is void.

### *Kittredge Plumbing, Inc. v. Halvorsen General Contractors, Inc.* (Columbia Supreme Court, 2012)

Halvorsen General Contractors built a forty-unit condominium project for a developer. Halvorsen subcontracted the plumbing to Kittredge Plumbing, Inc. and the drywall to Ostrander Drywall Co., each on Halvorsen's own form. Both subcontractors finished their work in March 2009. The developer became insolvent and never made final payment to Halvorsen, which then refused to release either subcontractor's retention. Kittredge and Ostrander sued. The trial court entered judgment for Halvorsen against both, and the Court of Appeal affirmed. We reverse as to Kittredge and affirm as to Ostrander.

Kittredge's subcontract provided: "Contractor shall pay Subcontractor within seven days after Contractor receives payment from Owner for Subcontractor's work. Receipt of payment from Owner is a condition of payment to Subcontractor." Ostrander's provided: "Contractor's receipt of payment from Owner is a condition precedent to Contractor's obligation to pay Subcontractor. Subcontractor expressly assumes the risk that Owner will not pay, including by reason of Owner's insolvency, and agrees to look solely to Owner's payment for its compensation."

**"Pay-if-paid" and "pay-when-paid."** A clause that makes the owner's payment a true condition precedent to the contractor's duty to pay ("pay-if-paid") shifts to the subcontractor the risk that the owner will never pay. Freedom of contract allows such a clause, but the law disfavors it because it works a forfeiture of payment for work already performed. Where it is doubtful whether language creates a condition or merely fixes the time for performance, Columbia courts read it as fixing the time. We therefore hold that a subcontract clause will be enforced as pay-if-paid only if it (1) expressly makes the owner's payment a condition precedent to the contractor's duty to pay, and (2) clearly shows that the subcontractor assumes the risk of the owner's nonpayment. A clause that fails either requirement is a "pay-when-paid" clause. It postpones payment for a reasonable time to allow the owner's payment to arrive, but it does not excuse the contractor if the owner never pays.

**Kittredge.** The first sentence of Kittredge's clause is a timing provision. The second uses the word "condition," but it does not say "condition precedent," and it says nothing about who bears the risk if the owner never pays. Read with the timing sentence that precedes it, the clause is at best ambiguous. Ambiguity in a form contract is construed against the party that drafted it, here Halvorsen. Kittredge's clause is pay-when-paid.

**A reasonable time.** Under a pay-when-paid clause, the contractor must pay within a reasonable time. A reasonable time is measured by when the owner's payment would ordinarily have been expected under the project's payment terms, taking into account the reasons for any delay. A contractor may not postpone payment indefinitely. Halvorsen's prime contract made final payment due forty-five days after completion. Seven months had passed when Kittredge sued. A reasonable time had expired, and Halvorsen owed Kittredge its retention.

**Ostrander.** Ostrander's clause satisfies both requirements: it expressly creates a condition precedent, and it expressly places the risk of the owner's nonpayment, including insolvency, on Ostrander. It is enforceable as written, and Ostrander bears the loss.

**Prevention.** Ostrander argued that Halvorsen could not rely on the condition. Even a valid pay-if-paid clause does not protect a contractor whose own breach or fault causes the owner to withhold payment. A party may not rely on the failure of a condition that its own wrongful conduct brought about. But the developer here withheld nothing because of any fault of Halvorsen; it simply became insolvent. The prevention rule does not help Ostrander.

Reversed as to Kittredge; affirmed as to Ostrander.

*Amari, J., dissenting in part.* I would affirm as to Kittredge as well. In the law of contracts, the word "condition" already means an event that must occur before a duty to perform arises. Kittredge, an experienced commercial subcontractor, agreed in writing that the owner's payment was "a condition of payment." The majority's second requirement, an express assumption of the risk of nonpayment, adds words the parties did not use. Subcontractors who do not wish to bear that risk can refuse to sign. I respectfully dissent from Part II of the Court's opinion.

### *Bramwell Electric Co. v. Hightower Builders, Inc.* (Columbia Court of Appeal, 2018)

Bramwell Electric Co. was the electrical subcontractor on Hightower Builders' six-story office project. Hightower received the owner's payments for two of Bramwell's progress applications but withheld $68,000 from them. It cited two reasons. (1) A **conduit-quantity dispute.** Hightower's field measurements showed about $27,000 less conduit than Bramwell had billed. Hightower raised the discrepancy in writing within a week of each application and withheld $40,000. (2) A **"clean-up and delay" backcharge** of $28,000. Bramwell's subcontract required written notice of any backcharge "within ten days after the event, with documentation, failing which the claim is waived." Hightower first asserted the backcharge in a letter four months after the events, two weeks after Bramwell demanded payment.

At the end of the job, the owner's tenants moved in and Hightower barred Bramwell's crews from the building. It then withheld $15,000 of Bramwell's retention for punch-list items, which it had its own electrician complete. Hightower documented a cost of $4,100 and, at trial, offered an undocumented estimate of $9,000.

The trial court found that Bramwell had installed the conduit it billed. It awarded the statutory penalty on all amounts withheld. We affirm in part and reverse in part.

**Good-faith dispute (§ 3263).** A good-faith dispute exists when the contractor honestly believes, on reasonable grounds in fact and under the subcontract, that the amount is not owed. The test has both a subjective and an objective part. The contractor need not ultimately prevail. Hightower's conduit dispute rested on its own field measurements and was raised promptly and in writing. It was a good-faith dispute, even though Hightower lost it at trial. Hightower's $40,000 withholding did not exceed 150 percent of the $27,000 disputed (that is, $40,500). The penalty on that amount is reversed.

The 150 percent cap is measured against the *estimated reasonable cost* of the disputed work or claim. A contractor cannot enlarge the cap by inflating its estimate. The estimate must itself be reasonable and, if challenged, supported. Where the subcontractor has given an itemized estimate and the contractor offers no competing support, the subcontractor's estimate is strong evidence of the reasonable cost.

The backcharge is different. A claim that the subcontract itself declares waived for lack of timely notice is not a reasonable ground for withholding. And a claim first raised months after the events, and only after the subcontractor demanded payment, supports an inference that it was a pretext for delay. A contractor may not manufacture a dispute to postpone payment. The $28,000 was wrongfully withheld.

**Penalty.** Once a wrongful withholding is shown, the 2 percent monthly penalty under § 3264(a) is mandatory. It is computed on the amount wrongfully withheld, for each month the payment is withheld. A contractor's good faith as to one item does not reduce the penalty on another.

**Punch list and retention.** A subcontractor has substantially performed when it has completed its work except for minor items that do not prevent the owner from using the work for its intended purpose. Bramwell's subcontract made completion of punch-list work a condition of the final payment of retention. Hightower argued that the condition never occurred. But Hightower itself barred Bramwell from the building. A party that prevents a condition from occurring may not rely on its non-occurrence. Where the subcontractor has substantially performed and the contractor has prevented it from completing minor corrective work, the contractor must pay the retention, less the reasonable cost of the corrections. The contractor must prove that cost with documentation. Hightower may deduct its documented $4,100. Its undocumented $9,000 estimate does not justify withholding more.

**Fees.** Bramwell obtained a net monetary recovery and is the prevailing party under § 3264(b).

Affirmed in part, reversed in part, and remanded for recalculation of the penalty.

### Columbia Rules of Professional Conduct (selected)

**Rule 3.10. Threatening criminal, administrative or disciplinary charges.** A lawyer shall not threaten to present criminal, administrative or disciplinary charges to obtain an advantage in a civil dispute. "Administrative charges" include a complaint to a state licensing board. *Comment:* This rule does not prohibit a lawyer from stating that the client will file a civil action or pursue other civil remedies, including claims for statutory penalties and attorney's fees.

**Rule 4.1. Truthfulness in statements to others.** While representing a client, a lawyer shall not knowingly make a false statement of material fact or law to anyone other than the client. *Comment:* A statement that is literally true but misleading because it omits a fact necessary to make it accurate, can be a false statement.

**Rule 4.2. Communication with a represented person.** (a) While representing a client, a lawyer shall not communicate, directly or through another person, about the subject of the representation with a person whom the lawyer knows to have a lawyer in that matter, unless that lawyer consents. (b) Where the represented person is an organization, this rule applies to its officers, directors and managing agents. *Comment:* Sending a copy of a letter to a represented person is a communication with that person.

#### II. Corbel must pay Pay Application No. 5 now, with the statutory penalty

**A. The prompt-payment statute required payment by June 7.** Columbia Civil Code § 3261(a) requires a contractor, within ten days after receiving a progress payment from the owner, to pay each subcontractor "the amount the owner paid on account of that subcontractor's work," less permitted retention. That duty applies "notwithstanding any contrary term of a subcontract" (§ 3261(b)). Article 5.2 of the Subcontract says the same thing. Corbel received the Owner's payment for ACM's April work on May 28, so ACM's $38,070 was due on **June 7, 2026**. It is now more than four months overdue.

**B. The $24,500 delay backcharge is not a good-faith dispute.** A contractor may withhold money from a subcontractor only in a "good-faith dispute," and even then only up to 150% of the estimated reasonable cost of the disputed claim (§ 3263(a)). A dispute is in good faith only when the contractor honestly believes, "on reasonable grounds in fact and under the subcontract," that the amount is not owed (*Bramwell Electric Co. v. Hightower Builders, Inc.* (Columbia Ct. App. 2018)). Corbel's backcharge fails that test for three independent reasons.

*First, the Subcontract bars it.* Article 9.2 required Corbel to give written notice of "any claim for a backcharge, including any claim for delay, within seven (7) days after the event giving rise to the claim, with supporting documentation." It adds: "Claims not noticed as required by this Section are waived." The delays Corbel relies on ended on April 3 (the desk) and May 15 (the guest rooms). Corbel gave no notice of any kind until your letter of July 15, two months after the later of those dates, and it has never provided documentation. Corbel wrote this waiver clause into its own form. *Bramwell* holds that "a claim that the subcontract itself declares waived for lack of timely notice is not a reasonable ground for withholding." It affirmed the statutory penalty on a backcharge raised in just this way.

*Second, the timing shows a pretext.* Corbel first raised the backcharge a month after ACM asked to be paid, and seven weeks after Corbel had received the Owner's money for ACM's work. Under *Bramwell*, a claim "first raised months after the events, and only after the subcontractor demanded payment, supports an inference that it was a pretext for delay," and "[a] contractor may not manufacture a dispute to postpone payment."

*Third, the claim has no reasonable basis in fact.* Corbel claims fourteen days of delay. But the reception desk date moved because Corbel transmitted the architect's ASI-07 redesign on March 6, two weeks before the scheduled installation. ACM asked for a schedule adjustment under Article 10 three days later. Corbel did not respond, and it later approved and paid for the change. Article 10 entitles ACM to an adjustment of the schedule for a change directed by the architect. ACM does not dispute that its guest-room work finished on May 15, about two weeks after the April 30 date. Part of that delay was ACM's, and part was due to flooring on floors 3 and 4 that was not ready until April 24. But the hotel's opening did not turn on ACM's work. According to the Owner, the hotel opened on June 1 instead of May 15 because the fire marshal would not sign off until the rooftop units' smoke-control dampers were fixed on May 27. That HVAC work is the subject of the Owner's own claims against Corbel. Corbel has not identified any extended general-conditions cost that ACM, rather than the HVAC problem, caused.

Because the backcharge is not a good-faith dispute, Corbel may not withhold any part of Pay Application No. 5 on account of it.

**C. The punch list supports a holdback of $3,450 at most.** The punch list is a legitimate item, but a small one, and Corbel has enlarged it by its own conduct. ACM priced all eleven items at $2,300 and had a crew scheduled for June 22. On June 20 Corbel refused ACM access, and it never answered ACM's June 21 offer to work at night, on weekends or room by room. Corbel's $6,000 figure is an undocumented estimate, and Corbel "cannot enlarge the cap by inflating its estimate"; where, as here, the subcontractor has given an estimate broken down by item and the contractor offers no support of its own, the subcontractor's figure "is strong evidence of the reasonable cost" (*Bramwell*). And a contractor that bars a subcontractor from completing minor corrective work may deduct only the reasonable cost of the corrections, proven "with documentation." An "undocumented ... estimate does not justify withholding more." Even if the punch list is treated as a good-faith dispute, § 3263(a) caps the holdback at 150% of its estimated reasonable cost: 150% × $2,300 = **$3,450**.

Corbel already holds $18,600 in retention, more than five times that cap, so the punch list gives Corbel no reason to hold back the progress payment. ACM will nevertheless allow Corbel to keep $3,450 of the retention until the punch list is finished. To avoid any argument, ACM has also computed the penalty below as though that holdback had come out of the progress payment.

**D. The statutory penalty.** A contractor that violates § 3261 must pay "a penalty of 2 percent of the amount wrongfully withheld for each month that payment is withheld" (§ 3264(a)). Once a wrongful withholding is shown, the penalty is mandatory. It is computed on the amount wrongfully withheld, and good faith on one item does not reduce the penalty on another (*Bramwell*). On the most conservative view, the amount wrongfully withheld is $38,070 − $3,450 = **$34,620**:

- 2% × $34,620 = $692.40 per month.
- June 7 to October 7, 2026 = 4 months: 4 × $692.40 = **$2,769.60**.
- The penalty continues to accrue at $692.40 for each further month that payment is withheld.

#### III. The retention is due, less the $3,450 holdback

Your letter states that Sections 5.3 and 5.4 make the Owner's final payment a "condition precedent" to the release of retention, and that ACM "accepted that risk." Neither the Subcontract nor Columbia law supports that position.

**A. Section 5.4 is a pay-when-paid clause, and a reasonable time for payment has passed.** The Columbia Supreme Court will enforce a clause as "pay-if-paid" only if it "(1) expressly makes the owner's payment a condition precedent to the contractor's duty to pay, and (2) clearly shows that the subcontractor assumes the risk of the owner's nonpayment" (*Kittredge Plumbing, Inc. v. Halvorsen General Contractors, Inc.* (Columbia 2012)). Any other clause only postpones payment for a reasonable time.

Section 5.4 says that Corbel's receipt of payment is "a condition of each payment." It does not say "condition precedent," and it says nothing about who bears the risk if the Owner never pays. It is materially the same as the clause that *Kittredge* held to be pay-when-paid ("Receipt of payment from Owner is a condition of payment to Subcontractor"). It is nothing like the enforceable clause in the same case, in which the subcontractor "expressly assume[d] the risk that Owner will not pay." Section 5.3, like the first sentence of the *Kittredge* clause, fixes when payment is due. And because Corbel drafted the form, any doubt is resolved against Corbel.

Under a pay-when-paid clause, Corbel must pay within a reasonable time, measured by when the Owner's payment "would ordinarily have been expected under the project's payment terms" (*Kittredge*). Under the prime contract, final payment to Corbel was ordinarily due 35 days after substantial completion. The architect certified substantial completion on June 1, so that date was about July 6, 2026. More than three months have passed since then. The Owner is withholding payment because of its own dispute with Corbel, a dispute that, in the Owner's words, has "nothing to do with [ACM's] work." A reasonable time has expired.

**B. Even a pay-if-paid clause would not protect Corbel.** *Kittredge* holds that "[e]ven a valid pay-if-paid clause does not protect a contractor whose own breach or fault causes the owner to withhold payment." The Owner is holding Corbel's retention "because of the rooftop HVAC problems and our liquidated-damages claim for Corbel's late completion." Corbel cannot rely on a non-payment that its own disputes with the Owner caused.

**C. The other conditions in Section 5.3 are satisfied or excused.**
- *Closeout.* ACM delivered the warranty letter, the as-built shop drawings and the conditional final waiver required by Section 5.5 on June 5, and Corbel acknowledged receipt on June 8. The statement in your letter that the closeout documents are incomplete is mistaken.
- *Punch list.* Corbel prevented ACM from completing the punch list. "A party that prevents a condition from occurring may not rely on its non-occurrence" (*Bramwell*). Where the subcontractor has substantially performed, meaning it has completed its work "except for minor items that do not prevent the owner from using the work for its intended purpose," the contractor must pay the retention less the reasonable, documented cost of the corrections (*Bramwell*). ACM has. Its work has been in use since the hotel opened on June 1, the open items are adjustments and a chipped edge, and the Owner describes the millwork as "the best thing in the building."

The retention is therefore due now, less the punch-list holdback: $18,600 − $3,450 = **$15,150**.
