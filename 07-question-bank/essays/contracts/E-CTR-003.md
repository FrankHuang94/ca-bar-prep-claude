# E-CTR-003 — The benches and the clogged nozzles

## Facts

Sierra Grow Supply, Inc. ("Sierra") sells greenhouse equipment to commercial growers. Valley Bloom Nursery, LLC ("Valley Bloom") grows ornamental plants for garden centers. Rita Chen, Valley Bloom's owner, handles all of its purchasing.

On January 8, 2026, Sierra's sales representative, Tom Akers, visited Valley Bloom. Rita told him that the nursery draws all its water from its own well, that "our water is very hard—about 280 ppm," and that its last misting system "clogged within months." Tom recommended Sierra's MistPro 300 automated misting system over the more expensive MistPro 500, saying: "The 300's nozzles are rated for hardness up to 300 ppm. You won't have a clogging problem." Sierra's catalog describes the MistPro 300 as "built for commercial nurseries." Well water with a hardness of 250 to 300 ppm is common among nurseries in the region.

On January 15, Rita and Sierra's sales director signed Sierra's two-page "Sales Agreement" for 12 MistPro 300 systems at $4,500 each, installed, for a total of $54,000. Paragraph 7, printed in bold capital letters, states: "LIMITED WARRANTY. SELLER WARRANTS THE EQUIPMENT TO BE FREE FROM DEFECTS IN MATERIALS AND WORKMANSHIP FOR 12 MONTHS. THIS WARRANTY IS EXCLUSIVE AND IN LIEU OF ALL OTHER WARRANTIES, EXPRESS OR IMPLIED." Paragraph 12, in ordinary type, states: "This Agreement is the complete and exclusive statement of the parties' agreement and supersedes all prior discussions." The Agreement says nothing about water hardness.

Sierra installed the systems on February 12. By early April the nozzles were clogged with mineral scale, and about a third of Valley Bloom's seedlings died for lack of water, a loss of $18,000. Sierra's technician told Rita that the MistPro 300's nozzles are rated for hardness up to 150 ppm and that the MistPro 500's are rated to 300 ppm. Tom later said he "must have mixed up the two models." On April 10 Rita notified Sierra in writing that the systems were defective and demanded that Sierra fix them. Sierra refused. Replacing the MistPro 300 nozzles with hard-water nozzles would cost $9,600.

Separately, on March 2, Tom sent Rita a letter on Sierra letterhead, which he signed: "Valley Bloom may order up to 40 HydroBench rolling greenhouse benches at $600 each. We will hold this price for you through June 30, 2026." On March 20, after its own supplier raised prices, Sierra's president emailed Rita: "The March 2 bench offer is withdrawn." On March 24, Rita telephoned Tom and said, "We accept the March 2 offer—all 40 benches." Tom replied, "Noted, but you saw our email."

On March 25, Rita mailed Sierra a letter on Valley Bloom letterhead, which she signed: "This confirms our order by telephone on March 24 for 40 HydroBench benches at $600 each under your March 2 letter." Sierra received it on March 27. Sierra did not respond until April 14, when its president wrote: "There is no contract for benches." The same benches now sell for $760 each.

## Call of the Question

1. Is Sierra bound to sell Valley Bloom 40 benches at $600 each? Discuss.
2. What warranty claims does Valley Bloom have against Sierra concerning the MistPro systems, and may it offer evidence of Tom's January 8 statements? Discuss.

## Model Answer

### Governing law and merchant status

UCC Article 2 governs contracts for the sale of goods, meaning things movable at the time of identification to the contract (UCC §§ 2-102, 2-105). Rolling benches are goods. The MistPro contract includes installation, but under the predominant-purpose test the equipment ($4,500 a unit) is the heart of the deal and installation is incidental, so **Article 2 governs both transactions.** A merchant deals in goods of the kind or holds itself out as having knowledge or skill about the practices involved (§ 2-104(1)). Sierra sells greenhouse equipment. Valley Bloom is a commercial nursery that buys equipment through its owner in the ordinary course of business, so it is a merchant for the "between merchants" rules. **Both are merchants.**

### 1. The benches

**A. Was the March 2 offer irrevocable? Merchant's firm offer (§ 2-205).** A merchant's signed written offer to buy or sell goods that by its terms gives assurance it will be held open is irrevocable without consideration for the time stated, but never longer than three months.

- *Merchant offeror and goods.* Sierra is a merchant and benches are goods.
- *Signed writing.* Tom signed the letter on Sierra letterhead. Any symbol adopted to authenticate a writing is a signature, and Tom, Sierra's sales representative, sent it as Sierra's offer; Sierra's own president later "withdrew" it, confirming that Sierra treated it as its offer.
- *Assurance it will be held open.* Sierra will argue that "we will hold this price" promised only a price, not an open offer, so it was a mere quotation it could withdraw. Valley Bloom will respond that the letter invited an order ("may order up to 40"), stated the price, and promised to "hold" that price for a fixed period; a reasonable merchant would read that as a promise not to revoke. Valley Bloom has the better reading.
- *Period.* The stated period, March 2 to June 30, is about four months. Section 2-205 caps irrevocability at three months, so the offer was irrevocable only until about June 2. That cap does not help Sierra, because it tried to revoke on March 20, well within three months.
- *Form supplied by the offeree.* The assurance was on Sierra's own letterhead, so no separate signature was needed.

At common law, an offer unsupported by consideration is revocable, and the March 20 email, received before acceptance, would have ended Valley Bloom's power to accept. Valley Bloom paid nothing for the promise, so only § 2-205 saves it. **The March 20 revocation was ineffective, and the offer remained open on March 24.**

**B. Acceptance.** An acceptance must be an unequivocal assent to the offer's terms, and unless the offer specifies otherwise it may be made in any reasonable manner and medium (§ 2-206). Rita's call, "We accept the March 2 offer—all 40 benches," was unequivocal and within the period. "Up to 40" gave Valley Bloom the power to fix the quantity, and it chose 40, so the terms are definite. Tom's reply ("Noted, but you saw our email") cannot undo an acceptance already made. **A contract for 40 benches at $600 each ($24,000) was formed on March 24.**

**C. Statute of frauds (§ 2-201(1)).** A contract for the sale of goods for $500 or more is unenforceable unless a writing sufficient to indicate a contract, stating a quantity, is signed by the party against whom enforcement is sought. At $24,000, the contract is within the statute, and Sierra is the party to be charged.

Tom's signed March 2 letter states the goods, the price and a quantity ("up to 40"). Valley Bloom may argue that a signed written offer that the other party then accepts shows a real transaction. Sierra will respond that an offer does not "indicate that a contract for sale has been made," and that "up to 40" states only a maximum. Because courts are divided on whether a signed offer suffices, Valley Bloom should rely on the merchant's confirmation rule.

**D. Merchant's confirmation (§ 2-201(2)).** Between merchants, a written confirmation sent within a reasonable time that is sufficient against the sender satisfies the statute against the recipient, if the recipient has reason to know its contents and does not give written notice of objection within 10 days after receiving it.

1. *Between merchants.* Yes, as shown above.
2. *Sent within a reasonable time.* Rita mailed the confirmation on March 25, one day after the call.
3. *Sufficient against the sender.* It is signed by Rita, refers to the contract and states the quantity (40).
4. *Received; reason to know contents.* Sierra received it on March 27. It was addressed to Sierra and concerned an offer Sierra itself had made, and Sierra's president answered it, so Sierra had reason to know its contents.
5. *No timely written objection.* Sierra's written objection came on April 14, 18 days after receipt. Tom's "Noted, but you saw our email" was oral and came before the confirmation. Sierra will argue that its March 20 email was a written objection already on file. But the statute requires notice of objection to the confirmation's contents within 10 days after it is received; an email withdrawing an offer, sent before any acceptance or confirmation existed, is not an objection to that confirmation.

**Effect.** The merchant's confirmation removes only the statute-of-frauds defense; Valley Bloom must still prove that a contract was made. It can do so through the firm-offer analysis above.

**Conclusion on call 1.** Sierra is bound to sell 40 benches at $600 each. If it refuses, Valley Bloom may cover by buying substitutes and recover the difference: 40 × ($760 − $600) = **$6,400**, plus incidental damages (§ 2-712).

### 2. The MistPro systems

**A. May Valley Bloom prove Tom's January 8 statements? Parol evidence rule (§ 2-202).** Terms set out in a writing intended as the parties' final expression may not be contradicted by evidence of a prior agreement or a contemporaneous oral agreement. They may be supplemented by consistent additional terms unless the writing was also intended as a complete and exclusive statement. Under the official comment, a consistent additional term is excluded only if it would certainly have been included in the writing had it been agreed.

- *Final writing.* Both parties signed an agreement covering price, quantity, installation and warranty. It is at least a partial integration.
- *Complete and exclusive.* Paragraph 12 is a merger clause. Valley Bloom will argue that the clause is boilerplate in ordinary type on Sierra's form, and that the writing's silence on water hardness, the very issue the parties discussed, shows it was not complete. Sierra will respond that the parties are merchants, the clause is plain, and the Agreement has a full warranty paragraph. The writing is likely a complete integration.
- *Contradiction or supplement.* Tom's promise that the nozzles were "rated for hardness up to 300 ppm" is an express warranty. It contradicts paragraph 7, which makes the limited warranty "exclusive and in lieu of all other warranties, express or implied," so it is barred even if the writing were only partly integrated. Even treated as a consistent additional term, a warranty addressing the clogging problem Rita had specifically raised would certainly have been written in had the parties agreed to it.

**The statement is likely inadmissible to add an express warranty.**

**Exceptions.** The rule does not bar extrinsic evidence offered to prove a ground for avoiding the contract, such as misrepresentation. Tom asserted a specific false fact (300 ppm, when the rating was 150), and it induced Rita to choose the 300 over the 500. A material misrepresentation on which the other party justifiably relies makes a contract voidable even if made innocently, and "I must have mixed up the two models" shows at least carelessness. Valley Bloom may therefore use the statement to seek rescission. In California, *Riverisland* allows evidence of fraud even when it contradicts the writing, and *Pacific Gas & Electric* takes a liberal view of extrinsic evidence. The conversation is also admissible to prove the circumstances that create an implied warranty of fitness (below), because implied warranties are not "terms" contradicted by the writing; whether they survive is a disclaimer question under § 2-316.

**B. Express warranties (§ 2-313).** An affirmation of fact relating to the goods that becomes part of the basis of the bargain is an express warranty, and puffery is not. If admitted, Tom's statement was a specific, factual rating that caused Rita to pick the 300, so it was an express warranty, and it was breached. Words of disclaimer inconsistent with an express warranty are inoperative, but that rule is expressly subject to the parol evidence rule (§ 2-316(1)). Because § 2-202 likely excludes the oral statement, **the oral express-warranty claim probably fails.** The catalog's "built for commercial nurseries" is general and close to puffery, though it helps define the goods' ordinary purpose.

The written limited warranty covers "defects in materials and workmanship." Valley Bloom will argue that a nozzle that clogs within two months is defective. Sierra will respond that the nozzles worked exactly as designed and rated (150 ppm); clogging in harder water is a design limitation, not faulty materials or assembly. **The written warranty likely does not cover the loss.**

**C. Implied warranty of merchantability (§ 2-314).** Unless excluded, a merchant seller warrants that the goods are fit for the ordinary purposes for which such goods are used. Sierra is a merchant in misting equipment.

- *Disclaimer.* To exclude merchantability, the language must mention "merchantability" and, if written, be conspicuous (§ 2-316(2)), unless the seller uses "as is" or similar language. Paragraph 7 is conspicuous, being in bold capitals, but it never mentions merchantability and contains no "as is" language. **The disclaimer fails as to merchantability.**
- *Breach.* Sierra will argue that the ordinary purpose of a misting system is use with ordinary water, that very hard well water is a particular use, and that it sells the 500 for that use. Valley Bloom will respond that Sierra markets the 300 as "built for commercial nurseries," and that water of 250 to 300 ppm is common among nurseries in the region. If the ordinary buyer of this product often uses such water, a system that clogs within two months is not fit for its ordinary purpose and would not pass without objection in the trade.

**Merchantability was likely breached**, although the point is close.

**D. Implied warranty of fitness for a particular purpose (§ 2-315).** Where the seller at contracting has reason to know of a particular purpose and that the buyer relies on the seller's skill or judgment to select suitable goods, the goods are impliedly warranted fit for that purpose.

- *Creation.* Rita told Tom about her 280 ppm well water and the earlier clogging, and she relied on his recommendation of the 300 over the 500; she did not insist on a model of her own choosing. The warranty arose.
- *Disclaimer.* A fitness warranty may be excluded by a conspicuous writing, and general language is enough ("there are no warranties which extend beyond the description on the face hereof"). Paragraph 7 is in writing, in bold capitals, and states that the limited warranty is "in lieu of all other warranties, express or implied." Valley Bloom will argue that the sentence sits under a heading that announces a warranty, not a disclaimer. But the typeface makes it conspicuous, and the parties are merchants who signed a two-page agreement.

**The fitness warranty was likely effectively disclaimed.**

**E. Notice and damages.** A buyer who has accepted goods must notify the seller of breach within a reasonable time after it discovers or should have discovered the breach (§ 2-607(3)(a)). Valley Bloom used the systems, so it accepted them, and it notified Sierra in writing on April 10, within days of discovering the clogging. Notice was timely.

For accepted goods, the buyer recovers the difference between the value of the goods as warranted and as accepted, often measured by the cost of making them conform (§ 2-714(2)): here **$9,600** for hard-water nozzles. It also recovers consequential damages resulting from needs the seller had reason to know of at contracting that could not reasonably have been prevented (§ 2-715(2)(a)). Rita told Tom that clogging had ruined her last system, so dead seedlings were a foreseeable result of nozzles that clog. Sierra may argue that Valley Bloom should have noticed the clogging sooner, which bears on mitigation. Valley Bloom's likely recovery on the merchantability claim is **about $27,600** ($9,600 + $18,000). As an alternative, it may seek rescission for misrepresentation, return the systems and recover the price, plus damages (rescission for fraud does not bar damages under § 2-721).

**Scoring on the 40–100 scale** (`00-exam-intel/grader-expectations.md` §4):
- **75+:** applies § 2-205 element by element, including the three-month cap; runs all five § 2-201(2) elements with the dates; separates the parol evidence rule from the warranty-disclaimer rules and treats merchantability and fitness differently.
- **65:** reaches the right results on the firm offer, the confirmation and merchantability, but treats the parol evidence question in one sentence or misses the misrepresentation exception.
- **55:** applies common-law revocation (no consideration, so revoked) and never reaches § 2-205, or holds that one conspicuous clause disclaims every warranty.
- **45 or below:** no UCC framework; discusses the warranties without addressing disclaimers or the parol evidence rule.

**Common mistakes**
1. Calling the March 20 revocation effective because Valley Bloom gave no consideration. Section 2-205 makes a merchant's signed firm offer irrevocable without consideration.
2. Treating the offer as irrevocable through June 30. Irrevocability without consideration cannot exceed three months.
3. Saying the merchant's confirmation proves the contract. It only removes the statute-of-frauds defense.
4. Using one disclaimer analysis for both implied warranties. Merchantability must be mentioned by name; fitness can be excluded by conspicuous general language.
5. Admitting the oral warranty without addressing § 2-202 and the merger clause, or excluding the statement for every purpose, which ignores the misrepresentation exception.

**Accuracy notes.** Rules follow R-CTR-009, -047, -051, -052, -058 to -060 and -103. The text of § 2-205 (three-month cap) and § 2-316(2) (merchantability must be mentioned; fitness by conspicuous writing) is settled official text, as adopted in Cal. Com. Code §§ 2205 and 2316. Whether a signed offer alone satisfies § 2-201(1) is stated as a split, not a settled rule. The current statute may say "record" rather than "writing" (rulebook V-101 concerns wording only).
