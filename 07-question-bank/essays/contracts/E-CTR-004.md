# E-CTR-004 — The theater lights

## Facts

The Harbor Theater Foundation (the "Foundation") owns a historic 1920s theater. In January 2026 it signed a written contract with Lumen Stage Lighting Co. ("Lumen") to design and install a new lighting system for $300,000: $100,000 on signing and $200,000 "within 10 days after completion." Lumen is owned by Iris Cole, a nationally known lighting designer. The Foundation chose Lumen over two lower bidders because of Iris's reputation. The contract provides:

- "Iris Cole shall personally prepare the lighting design."
- "Lumen shall not assign this contract without the Foundation's written consent."
- "Installation shall be completed by September 15, 2026. The Foundation has leased the theater to Meridian Players for a run of the play *Nightfall* beginning October 1, 2026, and the parties intend that Meridian benefit from timely completion. Lumen shall consult with Meridian's lighting director on Meridian's cue requirements."

The Foundation paid the $100,000. In February, as the contract required, Lumen's staff met with Meridian's lighting director to plan the cues. Relying on the September 15 date, Meridian sold $80,000 of advance tickets and signed contracts with its cast.

In March, Iris accepted a large project in Europe. She arranged for her former assistant, Max Hale, who now has his own design firm, to take over. In a signed agreement with Lumen, Max promised, for a fee of $60,000, "to perform all of Lumen's design obligations under the Harbor Theater contract." When the Foundation's director learned of this, she emailed Iris: "We are disappointed, but we will accept Max's design if you personally review and approve it." Iris never reviewed the design.

In April, Lumen borrowed $150,000 from Coastline Bank and, in a signed writing, assigned to the Bank "all of Lumen's right to payment under the Harbor Theater contract." On May 1 the Bank sent the Foundation written notice of the assignment and asked that all payments be made to the Bank.

In July, the Foundation and Lumen, without consulting Meridian, signed an amendment moving the completion date to November 15, in exchange for Lumen's agreement to add extra fixtures at no charge.

Max's design had serious flaws, and correcting enough of them to make the system usable delayed completion until November 20. Meridian had to cancel its entire October run. It refunded the $80,000 of advance ticket sales, and it had to pay its cast $40,000 under their contracts.

On November 25, at Iris's request, the Foundation paid Lumen $50,000 of the completion payment directly. The Foundation's experts estimate that fixing the remaining defects in Max's work will cost $40,000. Separately, Lumen owes the Foundation $10,000 for renting the theater for a private company party in June.

Coastline Bank now demands $200,000 from the Foundation, and Meridian has sued Lumen.

## Call of the Question

1. What rights does the Foundation have against Lumen and Max concerning the lighting design? Discuss.
2. How much, if anything, can Coastline Bank recover from the Foundation? Discuss.
3. Can Meridian recover from Lumen? Discuss.

## Model Answer

This is a contract for design and installation services, so the common law governs, with the Restatement (Second) of Contracts as the guide.

### 1. The Foundation's rights concerning the design

**A. Was the design duty delegable?** A party may delegate performance of its duties unless the obligee has a substantial interest in having the original promisor perform (personal skill, judgment or trust), the contract prohibits delegation, or public policy forbids it.

Three facts make the design duty nondelegable:
- *Express term.* The contract says "Iris Cole shall personally prepare the lighting design."
- *Personal skill.* The Foundation chose Lumen over two cheaper bidders because of Iris's national reputation, and a lighting design for a historic theater is an artistic, judgment-based task.
- *Anti-assignment clause.* "Lumen shall not assign this contract" is read, unless the circumstances indicate otherwise, as a bar on delegating Lumen's duties (R2d § 322).

Lumen will argue that Max, Iris's trained former assistant, could do technically equivalent work. But the express term and the reason for choosing Lumen show that the Foundation bargained for Iris's own judgment. **The delegation to Max was improper and a breach.**

**B. Did the Foundation consent or waive the term?** A condition or term can be waived by the party it protects. The director's email was conditional: "we will accept Max's design *if* you personally review and approve it." Iris never reviewed it, so the condition of the consent never occurred and there was no consent. Lumen may argue that by letting Max's installation proceed the Foundation waived the requirement by conduct. But the Foundation expressly reserved Iris's approval, and continuing with the project to meet a deadline is not a clear, intentional relinquishment of a known right. **There was no waiver.**

**C. Lumen remains liable.** Delegation never releases the delegating party; only a novation, in which the obligee agrees to substitute a new party and release the original, does that. The Foundation never agreed to release Lumen; its email insisted on Iris's continued involvement. **Lumen is still liable.**

Lumen breached in three ways: the unauthorized delegation, the defective design, and late completion. The July amendment validly moved the deadline to November 15, because Lumen's promise of extra fixtures was consideration for the Foundation's extension. But completion on November 20 was late even under the amended date. The Foundation recovers the reasonable cost of repairing the defective work, **$40,000**, which is not disproportionate to the value of a working system, plus any delay damages it can prove.

**D. Is Max liable to the Foundation?** A delegate becomes liable to the obligee only if it assumes the duty, that is, promises for consideration to perform it. That promise makes the obligee an intended beneficiary of the assumption agreement (R-CTR-116).

Max promised Lumen, for $60,000, "to perform all of Lumen's design obligations under the Harbor Theater contract." That is an express assumption. Performance was to run directly to the Foundation, and its purpose was to discharge Lumen's duty to the Foundation, so the Foundation is an intended (creditor-type) beneficiary of Max's promise. **The Foundation may sue Max for the defective design.** Max may raise against the Foundation any defense arising from his contract with Lumen, for example Lumen's failure to pay his fee. The Foundation may sue both Lumen and Max, but it can recover the $40,000 only once.

### 2. Coastline Bank's claim against the Foundation

**A. Was the assignment valid?** A party may assign its contract rights unless the assignment would materially change the obligor's duty or increase its burden or risk, is forbidden by law, or is validly precluded by the contract. An assignment needs a present intent to transfer the right.

- *Present transfer.* Lumen, in a signed writing, assigned "all of Lumen's right to payment," a present transfer.
- *Assignable right.* The right to be paid $200,000 is a right to money. Paying the Bank instead of Lumen does not change the Foundation's burden at all.
- *The anti-assignment clause.* The Foundation will argue that "Lumen shall not assign this contract without the Foundation's written consent" bars the assignment. But a prohibition on assigning "the contract" is read, unless the circumstances show otherwise, as barring only delegation of duties (R2d § 322). Here the circumstances confirm that reading, because the clause protected the Foundation's interest in Iris's performance, not in whom it pays. In addition, terms restricting the assignment of a right to payment are generally ineffective (UCC § 9-406(d), which applies because this is an assignment of a payment right as security for a loan).

**The assignment is effective.**

**B. Notice and the $50,000 payment.** An assignee stands in the assignor's shoes. Until the obligor receives notice of the assignment, it may pay the assignor and be discharged; after notice, payment to the assignor does not discharge it. The Bank gave written notice on May 1, and the Foundation paid Lumen $50,000 on November 25, nearly seven months later. **That payment does not reduce what the Foundation owes the Bank.** The Foundation must pay the Bank and seek the $50,000 back from Lumen.

**C. Defenses and set-offs.** The assignee takes subject to every defense and claim the obligor has against the assignor that arises from the contract itself, whenever it arises, and to other claims that accrue before the obligor receives notice (R2d § 336; UCC § 9-404(a)).

- *When the payment is due.* The $200,000 was due "within 10 days after completion." The Foundation may argue that a defective system was never "completed." But the system was installed and usable by November 20, and the remaining defects cost $40,000 to fix, about 13% of the price, so Lumen substantially performed. The payment became due on November 30, subject to a deduction for the defects.
- *Defects claim ($40,000).* This claim arises from the very contract that was assigned. The Foundation may assert it against the Bank even though the defects appeared after notice.
- *Party rental ($10,000).* This debt arises from a separate transaction, the June rental, and it accrued after the May 1 notice. The Foundation cannot set it off against the Bank.

**D. Computation.** The Bank recovers $200,000 − $40,000 = **$160,000**. The Foundation's $50,000 overpayment and the $10,000 rental debt remain claims against Lumen only. Because the Bank holds the assignment as security for a $150,000 loan, it must account to Lumen for any surplus it collects, but that does not reduce the Foundation's obligation on the assigned right.

### 3. Meridian's claim against Lumen

**A. Is Meridian an intended beneficiary?** A third party may enforce a contract only if it is an intended beneficiary: recognizing a right in it is appropriate to carry out the parties' intention, and either performance will satisfy a duty the promisee owes it or the promisee intends to give it the benefit of the performance (R2d § 302). An incidental beneficiary has no rights. California asks whether (1) the third party would in fact benefit, (2) a motivating purpose of the contracting parties was to benefit it, and (3) allowing it to sue is consistent with the contract's objectives and the parties' reasonable expectations (*Goonewardene*).

Lumen will argue that Meridian is a mere tenant. The lighting is installed in the Foundation's building and paid for by the Foundation, and tenants of a building under renovation are classic incidental beneficiaries.

Meridian has the stronger position. The contract names Meridian, states that "the parties intend that Meridian benefit from timely completion," ties the September 15 deadline to Meridian's October 1 opening, and requires Lumen to consult Meridian on its cue requirements, so part of the performance was designed around Meridian's production. Under *Goonewardene*:
1. Meridian would in fact benefit.
2. Benefiting Meridian was an express, motivating purpose, likely because the Foundation owed Meridian a usable theater under the lease.
3. Letting Meridian sue for lateness is consistent with a clause that exists precisely to protect Meridian's opening.

**Meridian is an intended beneficiary.**

**B. Vesting and the July amendment.** Until a beneficiary's rights vest, the promisor and promisee may modify the contract without its consent. Rights vest when the beneficiary (1) manifests assent at the request of the promisor or promisee, (2) sues on the promise, or (3) materially changes position in justifiable reliance.

Meridian's rights vested before July. Its director met with Lumen in February because the contract required that consultation, which is participation at the parties' request. More decisively, Meridian materially changed position by selling $80,000 of tickets and signing cast contracts in reliance on September 15. The contract reserved no power to change Meridian's benefit. **The July amendment does not bind Meridian; as to Meridian, completion was due September 15**, and Lumen finished more than two months late.

**C. Lumen's defenses and Meridian's damages.** The promisor may raise against the beneficiary any defense arising from the contract that it could raise against the promisee. Lumen has none. The Foundation paid on signing, and Lumen's delay came from its own delegation and Max's flawed design. Iris's decision to take the European project is not impossibility. The amendment is not a defense, because Meridian's rights had vested.

Meridian recovers expectation damages, limited by foreseeability, certainty and mitigation. Its loss was foreseeable when the contract was made, because the contract itself recited the October 1 run. Meridian cannot simply add the $80,000 in refunds and the $40,000 cast payments. Had the run gone forward it would also have paid the cast, so its expectation loss is the revenue it lost (at least $80,000, plus any provable additional sales) minus costs it saved. Advance sales give that figure reasonable certainty. Lumen may argue that Meridian failed to mitigate, for example by running with temporary lighting or rescheduling, but the facts show the run had to be cancelled. If lost revenue cannot be proved, Meridian may instead recover reliance damages: the expenditures wasted by the breach, including the $40,000 paid to the cast. **Meridian can recover from Lumen, most likely about $80,000 in lost revenue (more if additional sales are provable, less any costs it saved).**

**Scoring on the 40–100 scale** (`00-exam-intel/grader-expectations.md` §4):
- **75+:** separates delegation (duties) from assignment (rights) and reads the same anti-assignment clause correctly in each context; distinguishes contract-based defenses from unrelated post-notice claims; applies both the § 302 and *Goonewardene* tests and a vesting analysis.
- **65:** gets delegation, assignment and intended-beneficiary status right, but misses the post-notice payment problem or the vesting effect of reliance.
- **55:** treats the anti-assignment clause as voiding the Bank's assignment, or lets the July amendment defeat Meridian's claim without discussing vesting.
- **45 or below:** confuses assignment and delegation, or treats Meridian as able to sue simply because it was harmed.

**Common mistakes**
1. Concluding that delegation released Lumen. Only a novation does that.
2. Holding the assignment void because of the clause. "Shall not assign this contract" is read as barring delegation, and payment-right restrictions are generally ineffective.
3. Allowing the Foundation to deduct the $10,000 rental debt. It arose outside the contract and accrued after notice.
4. Letting the Foundation count its $50,000 payment to Lumen against the Bank's claim.
5. Adding the refunds and the cast pay together as Meridian's damages, which double counts.

**Accuracy notes.** Rules follow R-CTR-110 to R-CTR-116 and R-CTR-088. *Goonewardene v. ADP, LLC* (2019) 6 Cal.5th 817 supplies the California test (rulebook source S-203). UCC § 9-406(d) and § 9-404(a) govern assignments of payment rights; the answer cites them at section level only. The point that the Bank must account to Lumen for any surplus reflects the general Article 9 rule for collections by a secured party and is stated briefly; it does not affect the Foundation's liability.
