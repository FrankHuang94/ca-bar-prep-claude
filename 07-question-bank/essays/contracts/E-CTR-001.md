# E-CTR-001 — The bakery-café renovation

## Facts

Naomi Okafor owns a vacant one-story building that she planned to open as "Kettle & Crumb," a bakery-café. On September 2, 2025, she signed a written contract with Ridgeline Builders, Inc., owned by Dev Patel, to renovate the building for $240,000. Ridgeline agreed to "furnish all labor and materials to complete the renovation shown on the attached plans," including a $28,000 commercial convection oven that Ridgeline would buy and install, and to "substantially complete" the work by February 15, 2026. The plans showed new plumbing fixtures but said nothing about the sewer line under the floor slab. The contract specified "natural slate tile, Vermont Gray, in dining room (1,200 sq. ft.)." Naomi was to pay $60,000 at signing, $120,000 in monthly progress payments, and the final $60,000 "upon completion." The contract did not say that time was of the essence. During negotiations Naomi told Dev that her grand opening was set for March 1 and that she had hired staff to start that week.

Naomi paid the $60,000 deposit and every progress payment.

On November 10, Ridgeline's crew opened the slab and found that the building's old clay sewer line had collapsed. Replacing it would cost $18,000. Neither party had known of the problem; city records showed the line had been replaced in 1998, but the records turned out to be wrong. Dev emailed Naomi: "The sewer line isn't in our scope. I need another $18,000 to replace it or we will have to stop work until you find someone else." Naomi, worried about delay, emailed back: "Fine—just keep going." Ridgeline replaced the line.

In January, Ridgeline's supplier told Dev that Vermont Gray slate was back-ordered for eight weeks. Without telling Naomi, Dev installed a high-grade porcelain tile made to look like slate. Replacing it with slate now would cost $26,000 and close the dining room for two weeks. An appraiser says the porcelain floor lowers the building's market value by about $1,500 at most. Naomi chose slate because her late mother's farmhouse kitchen had a slate floor, and she is deeply upset.

On February 15 the work was unfinished, partly because of the tile change and partly because the city's electrical inspector postponed two inspections. On March 1, Dev offered to let Naomi open the finished take-out counter while the crew completed the dining room. Naomi declined, saying a "half-open" café would hurt her brand. Ridgeline finished on March 22, and Naomi opened the next day.

Naomi has refused to pay the final $60,000 or the $18,000. She says she lost $21,000 in profits during the three weeks after March 1, based on her business plan and on two years of sales from a similar stand she ran at farmers' markets. She also says she lost a $7,000 profit on a wedding-catering job that she booked on February 20 for March 14, because she had no working kitchen. Dev never knew about the catering job.

Ridgeline has sued Naomi for $78,000. Naomi has counterclaimed for damages.

## Call of the Question

1. Is Ridgeline entitled to the additional $18,000? Discuss.
2. Did either party materially breach the contract? Discuss.
3. What damages, if any, may each party recover? Discuss.

## Model Answer

### Governing law

Article 2 of the UCC governs sales of goods; the common law governs services, and a mixed contract is governed by the law of its predominant purpose (*Bonebrake*). Here Ridgeline promised to "furnish all labor and materials" to renovate a building. The $28,000 oven is a good, but it is under 12% of the $240,000 price and is installed as part of a construction job. The predominant purpose is construction services, so **the common law (Restatement (Second) of Contracts) governs**. No dispute concerns the oven in any event.

### 1. Is Ridgeline entitled to the additional $18,000?

**Consideration and the pre-existing duty rule.** A modification needs consideration. Performing, or promising to perform, a duty already owed to the promisor is not consideration (R2d § 73).

Naomi will argue that Ridgeline was already bound to "furnish all labor and materials to complete the renovation," which includes making the plumbing work; replacing a sewer line that serves the new fixtures is part of that duty. If so, Ridgeline gave nothing new for her promise of $18,000.

Ridgeline will respond that the plans showed only "new plumbing fixtures" and "said nothing about the sewer line under the floor slab." Work outside the agreed scope is a new performance, which is consideration. Because the plans defined the scope and were silent on the sewer line, and the defect was in pre-existing building infrastructure rather than in Ridgeline's own work, Ridgeline has the better argument that replacing the line was new work. **The promise is likely supported by consideration.**

**Enforceable even without consideration (R2d § 89).** Even if the work was within scope, a modification is binding without consideration if it is fair and equitable in view of circumstances not anticipated when the contract was made. Both parties relied on city records showing a 1998 replacement, so a collapsed clay line was not anticipated. The increase ($18,000) matches the actual cost of the work and is under 8% of the price, so it is fair and equitable. Ridgeline also performed in reliance on the modification (§ 89(c)). **The modification is likely enforceable under § 89 as well.** (California's Civil Code § 1698 would not change this: the modification was made in a writing, the exchanged emails, and was fully executed.)

**Economic duress.** A contract is voidable if assent was induced by an improper threat that left no reasonable alternative; a bad-faith threat to breach an existing contract can be improper (R2d §§ 175–176).

Naomi will argue Dev threatened to "stop work until you find someone else" when she faced a March 1 opening, leaving her no practical alternative. However, a threat is not improper if the party honestly and reasonably believes it is not obligated. Dev's position that the sewer line was outside the scope was made in good faith and was probably correct. Naomi also had alternatives: she could have paid under protest or sued. **Duress likely fails**, and Ridgeline is entitled to the $18,000.

### 2. Did either party materially breach?

**Ridgeline: the tile substitution.** A breach is material if it deprives the injured party of a substantial part of the benefit she reasonably expected, weighing the extent of the lost benefit, whether damages can compensate, the forfeiture to the breaching party, the likelihood of cure and good faith (R2d § 241). A builder who has substantially performed may enforce the contract, less damages for defects; willful deviation weighs heavily against substantial performance (*Jacob & Youngs v. Kent*).

Installing porcelain instead of "natural slate tile, Vermont Gray" is clearly a breach of an express specification.

- *Loss of benefit.* Naomi will stress that she bargained for natural slate for personal reasons tied to her mother's kitchen, so the benefit she expected was specific and aesthetic, not just a durable floor. Ridgeline will answer that the floor is one item in a $240,000 renovation, the porcelain is "high-grade," and the market-value loss is at most $1,500.
- *Adequacy of damages and forfeiture.* Money can compensate for the floor, and declaring a material breach would let Naomi withhold $60,000 for a $1,500 defect, a large forfeiture.
- *Good faith.* This factor favors Naomi. Dev knew of the shortage and substituted "without telling Naomi." A builder that consciously departs from a specification without consent looks willful, and willfulness weighs heavily against substantial performance; some courts deny the doctrine to a willful breacher altogether. Ridgeline will argue the substitution was a good-faith response to an eight-week back-order made to keep the project on schedule, not to save money.

Because the floor is a small part of the whole and is fully compensable in money, a court will likely find substantial performance despite the troubling lack of notice. **The tile substitution is likely a minor breach**, although the willfulness argument makes this the closest issue in the case.

**Ridgeline: late completion.** Delay is usually a minor breach unless the contract makes time of the essence or the circumstances show timing was vital. The contract had no time-of-the-essence clause and required only "substantial" completion by February 15. Naomi will argue that Dev knew of her March 1 opening and hired staff, so time mattered. Still, the work was finished five weeks after the deadline and three weeks after the opening date, and the loss is measurable in money. Ridgeline cannot excuse the delay by pointing to the city inspector: the risk of ordinary inspection delays falls on the builder that promised a date, and inspection postponements are neither unforeseeable nor a supervening impossibility. **The delay is a breach, but likely a minor one** that gives Naomi damages only.

**Naomi: refusal to pay.** Where one party's work takes time and the other's payment does not, the work must come first, and the builder's substantial performance is a constructive condition of the owner's duty to pay (R2d §§ 234, 237). The final $60,000 was due "upon completion." Ridgeline completed the work on March 22 and substantially performed, so the condition was satisfied, and Naomi's duty to pay matured, subject to her right to deduct her damages. By withholding the entire $60,000 and the $18,000, far more than any defensible offset, **Naomi breached**. Had the tile substitution been material, her withholding would have been justified; on these facts it likely was not.

**Conclusion on call 2.** Ridgeline committed two minor breaches (tile and delay); Naomi materially breached by refusing to pay.

### 3. Damages

The injured party recovers expectation damages: the loss in value of the promised performance plus incidental and consequential losses, minus costs avoided, limited by foreseeability, certainty and mitigation (R2d §§ 347, 350–352).

#### Naomi's damages

**The tile: cost of completion or diminution in value.** An owner normally recovers the reasonable cost of completing or correcting the work, but if that cost is clearly disproportionate to the value it adds (economic waste), she recovers the diminution in market value (R2d § 348(2)). Replacement would cost $26,000 and close the dining room for two weeks; the market-value loss is at most $1,500. Under *Jacob & Youngs*, that disproportion points to $1,500.

Naomi will argue that diminution in value undercompensates her because her loss is personal: she wanted slate for its meaning, not its resale value. Courts sometimes award cost of completion when the owner's purpose was aesthetic or personal, or when the breach was willful (*Groves*). The counter-argument is that ripping out a sound floor at seventeen times the market loss is the waste the rule targets. **A court will likely award diminution in value ($1,500)**, though the willful substitution gives Naomi a real argument for a larger sum.

**Delay: lost profits ($21,000).** Damages for a loss are recoverable only if foreseeable when the contract was made as a probable result of breach, either in the ordinary course or because of special circumstances the breaching party had reason to know (*Hadley v. Baxendale*; R2d § 351). Naomi told Dev during negotiations that she would open March 1 and had hired staff. Lost profits from a delayed opening are therefore foreseeable. **This category passes the foreseeability test.**

**The catering job ($7,000).** Naomi booked the wedding on February 20, five months after contracting, and Dev never knew of it. Foreseeability is tested at formation, and a specific catering contract is a special circumstance Dev had no reason to know of. **The $7,000 is not recoverable.**

**Certainty.** Damages must be proved with reasonable certainty; lost profits of a new business were traditionally barred and today are recoverable only on reliable evidence (R2d § 352). Ridgeline will argue Kettle & Crumb had no operating history, so a business plan is speculation. Naomi will respond with two years of sales from a similar farmers'-market stand, which gives a rational basis for estimating demand, and the opening-week staffing and schedule. Courts increasingly accept such comparable-business evidence. **Some lost-profit recovery is likely**, but a court may discount the $21,000 because a café differs from a market stand. If profits are too uncertain, Naomi may instead recover reliance losses, such as wages paid to staff who had no work.

**Mitigation.** An injured party cannot recover losses she could have avoided by reasonable effort without undue risk or burden (R2d § 350). On March 1, Dev offered to let Naomi open the finished take-out counter. Ridgeline will argue that opening would have earned a substantial part of the lost profit, so her refusal bars recovery of that part. Naomi will respond that the duty requires only reasonable steps, and a "half-open" debut with construction under way could damage a new brand's reputation. Because counter sales were a real, low-risk way to earn revenue during three weeks of delay, a court will likely find her refusal unreasonable in part and **reduce her lost-profit award by the profit the counter would have made**.

**Emotional distress.** Damages for emotional distress are not recoverable for breach of contract unless the breach also caused bodily harm or the contract made serious distress particularly likely (R2d § 353). A renovation contract is commercial; **Naomi's distress over the floor is not compensable.**

#### Ridgeline's recovery

A builder who has substantially performed recovers the contract price less the owner's damages. Ridgeline is owed $240,000 + $18,000 = $258,000 and has been paid $180,000, so the unpaid balance is **$78,000**. From that Naomi may offset $1,500 for the tile and her proven, mitigated delay profits (at most $21,000, likely less). If a court instead found the tile substitution a willful material breach, Ridgeline could not sue on the contract. It would still recover in restitution the value of the benefit it conferred beyond Naomi's damages (R2d § 374), which on these facts would yield a similar figure.

**Overall:** Ridgeline likely recovers about $78,000 minus $1,500 and Naomi's mitigated lost profits; Naomi recovers nothing for the catering job or her distress.

**Common mistakes**
1. Applying UCC perfect tender because of the oven. The predominant purpose is construction services.
2. Awarding the $7,000 catering profit. Foreseeability is measured when the contract was made (September 2), not when the loss arose.
3. Treating any delay as material. With no time-of-the-essence clause and full completion, delay is ordinarily a minor breach.
4. Forgetting that Naomi's refusal to pay the whole balance can make *her* the materially breaching party.
5. Mixing up economic duress with the pre-existing duty rule. They are separate headings: one asks whether there was consideration, the other whether assent was coerced.
