# E-CTR-005 — The amphitheater and the fireworks

## Facts

Crescent Concerts, LLC ("Crescent") promotes music events. On March 1, 2026, Crescent and Dunes Venue, LLC ("Dunes"), which owns the Lakeside Amphitheater, signed a written agreement under which Dunes rented the amphitheater to Crescent for a three-night festival, August 14–16, 2026, for $90,000. Crescent paid $30,000 on signing; the balance was due August 1. The agreement contemplated shows running from 7 p.m. to midnight. Paragraph 6 provides: "Dunes's obligations are conditioned on Crescent delivering, no later than July 1, 2026, a certificate of liability insurance naming Dunes as an additional insured."

Crescent's insurance broker was slow, and Crescent delivered a proper certificate on July 6. Ola Reyes, the Dunes manager who had negotiated the agreement and handled all dealings with Crescent, emailed back the same day: "Got it, thanks—see you in August." Over the next two weeks Crescent spent $40,000 on advertising and paid deposits to its performers.

On July 15, after neighbors complained about noise at a different venue, the county board adopted an emergency order requiring all amplified outdoor music in the county to end by 10 p.m. On July 18, another promoter offered Dunes $130,000 for the same August dates.

On July 20 Ola emailed Crescent: "Our lawyers are reviewing whether your late insurance certificate ended our agreement, and the new curfew may make the event unworkable for us. We are also considering other offers for those dates." On July 21 Crescent replied in writing: "We are performing and expect you to do the same. Please confirm in writing by July 28 that Dunes will make the amphitheater available on August 14–16." Dunes did not respond.

On July 29, Crescent rented the Riverside Pavilion, the only comparable venue available, for $115,000 for the same dates, and it notified Dunes that it had done so "because of your repudiation." On August 1, Dunes wrote that it "will honor the agreement" and demanded the $60,000 balance. Crescent refused to pay and demanded the return of its $30,000 deposit. The festival was held at Riverside, ending each night at 10 p.m.

Separately, on June 1, Crescent had hired PyroWorks, Inc. to stage a fireworks finale on each night of the festival for $45,000. Crescent paid a $15,000 deposit, and the balance was due after the last show. The PyroWorks contract states: "The deposit is nonrefundable if Crescent cancels any show." By early August PyroWorks had spent $12,000 building custom shells for the shows; they cannot be used for other customers. On August 5, citing extreme fire danger, the state fire marshal banned all fireworks displays in the county through September 30. Fire-danger bans had been issued in the county in two of the previous ten summers. PyroWorks demands the $30,000 balance, and Crescent demands the return of its $15,000 deposit.

## Call of the Question

1. Was Dunes entitled to treat the rental agreement as ended because of the late insurance certificate? Discuss.
2. Did Dunes breach the rental agreement, and can it rely on the curfew order to excuse its performance? Discuss.
3. What are the rights of Crescent and PyroWorks against each other? Discuss.

## Model Answer

**Governing law.** Both agreements are for services, a venue rental and a fireworks show, so the common law (Restatement (Second) of Contracts) governs. The custom shells are incidental to PyroWorks' service, and the result in call 3 would be the same under UCC § 2-615.

### 1. The late insurance certificate

**A. Express condition.** An express condition, created by words such as "conditioned on," must be strictly satisfied before the conditioned duty becomes due; substantial performance is not enough. Paragraph 6 makes Dunes's obligations "conditioned on" delivery of the certificate "no later than July 1." The certificate arrived on July 6, so the condition did not occur on time. Dunes's duty became due only if that failure was excused.

**B. Waiver.** The non-occurrence of a condition is excused if the party it protects waives it by words or conduct. Waiving a condition that is not a material part of the agreed exchange needs no consideration.

- *Words of waiver.* The condition protects Dunes. When the late certificate arrived, Ola replied, "Got it, thanks—see you in August." That accepts the late certificate and affirms the event.
- *Authority.* Dunes may argue that Ola could not give up its rights. But Ola negotiated the agreement and handled all dealings with Crescent, so she had at least apparent authority to accept documents under it.
- *Materiality.* Dunes will argue that insurance protection is a material part of the bargain. What was waived, however, was not the insurance but a five-day delay in proving it. Coverage was in place more than five weeks before the first show, and the delay cost Dunes nothing.

**Dunes waived the July 1 deadline.**

**C. No retraction; estoppel.** A waiver of a condition can be retracted only by notice received while there is still reasonable time for the condition to occur, and not after the other party has materially changed position in reliance (R2d § 84(2)). Here the deadline had already passed when Dunes waived it, so there was nothing left to retract. Crescent then spent $40,000 on advertising and paid performer deposits in reliance on "see you in August." **Dunes is also estopped from reviving the condition.**

**D. Forfeiture; minor breach.** Even without waiver, a court may excuse a condition where enforcing it would cause a disproportionate forfeiture and its occurrence was not a material part of the agreed exchange (R2d § 229). Enforcing a five-day lapse would cost Crescent its festival, its $30,000 deposit and its marketing spending, while giving Dunes a windfall: the chance to rent the dates for $130,000. If paragraph 6 were also read as Crescent's promise, a five-day delay that caused no harm is at most a minor breach, giving a damages claim (here nominal) but no right to terminate.

**Dunes was not entitled to treat the agreement as ended.**

### 2. Dunes's breach and the curfew

**A. Was the July 20 email a repudiation?** An anticipatory repudiation is a clear and unequivocal statement, made before performance is due, that a party will not or cannot perform.

Crescent will argue that Dunes asserted a termination right it did not have and was shopping the dates. But the email is hedged: its lawyers are "reviewing," the curfew "may" make the event unworkable, and Dunes is "considering" other offers. **Standing alone it is likely too equivocal to be a repudiation**, but it gave Crescent reasonable grounds for insecurity.

**B. Adequate assurance.** When reasonable grounds arise for insecurity about the other party's performance, a party may demand adequate assurance of due performance and, if commercially reasonable, suspend its own performance until it receives it. Failure to give adequate assurance within a reasonable time is a repudiation (R2d § 251; UCC § 2-609 states the same rule for goods). Many courts apply § 251 to non-goods contracts; a minority limit it, so Crescent should also argue that Dunes's statements together amounted to a repudiation.

- *Reasonable grounds.* Dunes questioned whether the contract survived, invoked the curfew, and said it was considering other offers for the same dates.
- *Demand.* Crescent's July 21 letter was in writing, specific, and asked only for confirmation that the venue would be available.
- *Reasonable time.* Dunes may argue that seven days was too short. But the festival was three weeks away, Crescent needed time to find another venue if Dunes would not perform, and the requested assurance required only a one-line answer.

Dunes's silence past July 28 was a failure to give adequate assurance. **Dunes repudiated the agreement.**

**C. Crescent's options.** After a repudiation the injured party may treat it as a total breach, suspend its own performance, and arrange substitute performance, and it must act reasonably to mitigate. Crescent rented the only comparable venue the next day, which was reasonable mitigation, not a breach. Payment of the $60,000 balance and Dunes's performance were exchanged performances, so Dunes's repudiation discharged Crescent's duty to pay the balance due August 1.

**D. Retraction.** A repudiating party may retract until the injured party materially changes position in reliance or indicates that it considers the repudiation final. On July 29 Crescent committed $115,000 to Riverside and told Dunes it was acting "because of your repudiation." **Dunes's August 1 retraction came too late.**

**E. Impracticability (the curfew).** A party's duty is discharged if, after contracting, its performance is made impracticable by an event whose non-occurrence was a basic assumption of the contract, without its fault, and it did not assume the risk. A government order can be such an event (R2d § 264). But the curfew does not make Dunes's performance, making the amphitheater available on August 14–16, any harder. It limits how late music can play, which affects Crescent's shows, not the venue. Even those shows remained feasible from 7 to 10 p.m., as the Riverside festival proved. **There is no impracticability.**

**F. Frustration of purpose.** A party's remaining duties are discharged where, after contracting, its principal purpose, known to both parties, is substantially frustrated without its fault by an event whose non-occurrence was a basic assumption. Dunes's purpose was to earn $90,000 in rent, and the curfew does not touch that. The $130,000 offer shows only that the deal became less attractive to Dunes, which is not frustration. Crescent's own purpose was reduced, not destroyed, because three-hour shows remained possible. Noise regulation was also foreseeable after neighbor complaints. **Frustration does not excuse Dunes.**

**G. Crescent's damages.** Expectation damages put Crescent where performance would have left it. Crescent would have paid $90,000 for the venue. Instead it paid Riverside $115,000, so its damages are **$25,000**, plus restitution of the **$30,000** deposit for which it received nothing, a total of **$55,000** plus any incidental costs. Dunes's claim for the $60,000 balance fails.

### 3. Crescent and PyroWorks

**A. Discharge by supervening illegality.** A party's duty is discharged when performance becomes objectively impossible, including through supervening illegality, by an event whose non-occurrence was a basic assumption of the contract, without that party's fault, provided it did not assume the risk.

- *Supervening event; impossibility.* The August 5 ban, issued after the June 1 contract, made every fireworks display in the county illegal through September 30. The festival's fixed dates fall within the ban, so this is not merely a temporary suspension; performance on the agreed dates became impossible.
- *Basic assumption; no fault.* Both parties assumed displays would be lawful. Neither caused the ban.
- *Risk allocation.* PyroWorks will argue that the risk was foreseeable, since bans were issued in two of the last ten summers, and that the deposit clause put the risk of cancellation on Crescent. Crescent will respond that foreseeability does not by itself show that a party assumed the risk; it is only one factor, and neither party allocated the risk of a government ban. The clause applies only if "Crescent cancels any show," and Crescent cancelled nothing; the state made performance illegal. Courts read such clauses by their terms and avoid stretching them to cause forfeiture.

**Both parties' remaining duties were discharged. PyroWorks cannot recover the $30,000 balance.**

**B. Restitution.** After discharge for impossibility, either party may recover restitution for benefits it conferred by part performance (R2d § 377). Crescent conferred a $15,000 payment and received nothing, so it may recover the deposit. PyroWorks' $12,000 in custom shells conferred no benefit on Crescent, which never received them, so PyroWorks has no restitution claim. It may ask the court to protect its reliance interest under R2d § 272(2), which allows relief "as justice requires" where the ordinary rules would leave injustice, especially because the shells were built for Crescent and are useless to others. **Crescent likely recovers its $15,000 deposit, though a court applying § 272(2) could reduce that by part of PyroWorks' wasted costs.**

- **75+:** analyzes call 1 through waiver, retraction and forfeiture; treats the July 20 email as equivocal and moves to adequate assurance; addresses retraction; separates impracticability from frustration; computes $55,000; discharges the fireworks contract and returns the deposit.
- **65:** reaches the right results but treats the July 20 email as an outright repudiation without discussing adequate assurance, or runs impracticability and frustration together.
- **55:** holds that strict compliance with paragraph 6 let Dunes terminate, without discussing waiver.
- **45 or below:** discusses performance with no reference to conditions, repudiation or discharge doctrines.

**Common mistakes**
1. Stopping at "express conditions require strict compliance." The excuse doctrines (waiver, estoppel, forfeiture) are where the points are.
2. Treating hedged statements ("may," "reviewing") as an unequivocal repudiation without considering a demand for assurance.
3. Letting Dunes retract after Crescent rented Riverside and said it treated the repudiation as final.
4. Using the curfew to excuse the venue owner. Neither doctrine fits a party whose own performance is unaffected.
5. Treating the "nonrefundable if Crescent cancels" clause as covering a government ban, or treating foreseeability alone as conclusive assumption of the risk.

**Accuracy notes.** Rules follow R-CTR-062 to -070 and R-CTR-082 to -084. Adequate assurance for non-goods contracts is stated as the Restatement position (R2d § 251) that many but not all courts follow. R2d §§ 84(2), 229, 264, 272(2) and 377 are cited for their well-known content; R-CTR-064 carries a verification flag only on §§ 245 and 271 (section numbers), which this answer does not rely on.
