# E-CTR-002 — The pouches that would not reseal

## Facts

Tidewater Snacks, Inc. makes seaweed chips. It planned to launch a new line, "Sea Crisp," in all 140 stores of Coastline Markets, a regional grocery chain, on September 15, 2026, under a written launch agreement with Coastline.

On June 1, Tidewater sent FlexPak Supply Co., a packaging manufacturer, a purchase order for 40,000 custom-printed, resealable foil pouches at $0.50 each ($20,000), to be delivered to Tidewater's plant "no later than September 1, 2026." The purchase order stated: "These pouches are for our Sea Crisp launch at Coastline Markets on September 15. Pouches must reseal; the product goes stale within 48 hours if exposed to air." It said nothing about remedies.

On June 4, FlexPak returned its standard "Order Acknowledgment," which confirmed the quantity, price and delivery date. On the back, in the same small print as its other terms, it stated: "Seller's sole liability for defective goods is replacement or refund of the price. Seller shall not be liable for incidental or consequential damages, including lost profits." Tidewater's purchasing manager filed the acknowledgment without reading the back. The companies had dealt with each other once before, in 2024, on a small order of plain pouches, using the same two forms; no dispute arose.

FlexPak delivered the pouches on August 25. On August 27, Tidewater's quality team tested a random sample of 400 pouches and found that 22% of the zipper seals did not close completely. Buyers and sellers of food pouches generally treat a seal-failure rate under 2% as acceptable. On August 28, Tidewater emailed FlexPak: "We reject the entire shipment of pouches—your zipper seals fail at a rate of over 20%. The pouches are in our warehouse awaiting your instructions."

On August 29, FlexPak replied that seal performance "varies with how the pouch is filled" and offered either to send a technician or to replace the entire order with pouches from a new production run, which it could deliver by September 12.

Tidewater decided it could not wait. On August 30 it ordered 40,000 pouches from PrintLine, another supplier, at a rush price of $0.65 each ($26,000), plus $1,200 for expedited freight. They arrived on September 6. A third supplier had quoted $0.56 per pouch but could not deliver until September 22. Because filling and shipping took several days, Tidewater delivered Sea Crisp to Coastline on September 18, three days late. Citing the launch agreement, Coastline cut its initial order by 25%, which cost Tidewater $15,000 in profit. Tidewater also paid $300 to store the rejected pouches until FlexPak collected them on September 10.

Tidewater has paid nothing. FlexPak demands the $20,000 price, claiming that Tidewater wrongfully rejected the pouches. Tidewater wants to recover its losses from FlexPak.

## Call of the Question

1. What are the terms of the contract between Tidewater and FlexPak, and do they include the limitation in FlexPak's acknowledgment? Discuss.
2. Did Tidewater properly reject the pouches, and was FlexPak entitled to cure? Discuss.
3. What damages, if any, can Tidewater recover from FlexPak? Discuss.

## Model Answer

### Governing law and merchant status

Article 2 of the UCC governs contracts for the sale of goods, meaning things movable when identified to the contract (UCC §§ 2-102, 2-105). Custom-printed foil pouches are movable goods, and specially manufactured goods are still goods, so **Article 2 applies**. A merchant is one who deals in goods of the kind or holds itself out as having special knowledge of them (§ 2-104(1)). FlexPak manufactures packaging, and Tidewater is a food manufacturer that buys packaging in its business; for § 2-207 purposes any business buyer acting through a purchasing department qualifies. **Both are merchants.**

### 1. The terms of the contract

**Formation: the acknowledgment as an acceptance (§ 2-207(1)).** In a sale of goods, a definite and seasonable expression of acceptance operates as an acceptance even though it states additional or different terms, unless acceptance is expressly made conditional on assent to them. Tidewater's purchase order was the offer. FlexPak's acknowledgment, returned three days later, matched every dickered term: quantity, price and delivery date. It did not say that FlexPak's acceptance was conditional on Tidewater's assent to its printed terms. So **a contract was formed on June 4 on the terms of Tidewater's purchase order**, and the printed limitation is at most a proposal for an addition.

**Is the limitation part of the contract (§ 2-207(2))?** The purchase order was silent on remedies, so the clause is an *additional* term. Between merchants an additional term becomes part of the contract unless (a) the offer expressly limits acceptance to its terms, (b) the term materially alters the contract, or (c) the offeror objects within a reasonable time.

- *(a) and (c).* The purchase order contained no "acceptance limited to these terms" language, and Tidewater never objected; its manager filed the form unread. Neither exception applies.
- *(b) Material alteration.* A term materially alters the contract if incorporating it without express awareness would cause surprise or hardship. The Code's comments treat a clause negating standard warranties as material, but list a clause "otherwise limiting remedy in a reasonable manner" as an example of a term that is not material. Courts are split on clauses that exclude consequential damages.

FlexPak will argue no surprise: the clause is a common commercial term, it limits remedies rather than disclaiming warranties, and the parties used the very same forms in 2024, so Tidewater was on notice of FlexPak's standard terms.

Tidewater has the stronger argument. Its own purchase order told FlexPak that the pouches were for a dated retail launch and that the product "goes stale within 48 hours" without a working seal. In that context, a term eliminating all liability for lost profits shifts the main foreseeable risk of the deal to Tidewater, which is real hardship. The 2024 deal is one small order of plain pouches with no dispute, which is weak evidence of a course of dealing (UCC § 1-303(b)) and gave Tidewater no reason to focus on the clause. A buyer that disclosed exactly why delay would be costly would be surprised to learn it had silently waived those losses. **The exclusion likely materially alters the contract and drops out.** The contract therefore consists of the purchase-order terms plus the Code's gap fillers, including the implied warranty of merchantability and the buyer's full Article 2 remedies.

**If the clause were included (§ 2-719).** The clause would make "replacement or refund" the exclusive remedy, because it says "sole liability." But an exclusive remedy that fails of its essential purpose gives way to the general Code remedies. Replacement delivered on September 12, eleven days after the delivery date and three days before a launch that needed several days of filling, would not give Tidewater the substantial value of its bargain, so the replacement option fails of its essential purpose. FlexPak will answer that the clause also offers a refund, which it can always give, and some courts hold that a remedy including a refund of the price does not fail of its essential purpose. Tidewater will reply that refunding a price it never paid leaves it with no remedy at all for losses the seller knew a dated launch would cause, so the clause does not leave it the minimum adequate remedy the Code assumes. The point is unsettled. The separate exclusion of consequential damages is enforceable unless unconscionable, and limiting commercial losses between merchants is not prima facie unconscionable. Courts disagree on whether that exclusion survives when the exclusive remedy fails. **Call 1 therefore matters most for the $15,000 lost profit**: if the clause is in, that loss is likely barred; if, as is more likely, it is out, the loss is recoverable if the ordinary limits are met.

### 2. Rejection and cure

**Nonconformity: perfect tender.** If the goods or the tender fail in any respect to conform to the contract, the buyer may reject the whole, accept the whole, or accept any commercial units and reject the rest (§ 2-601). The purchase order's description, "resealable" pouches that "must reseal," was part of the basis of the bargain and is an express term and warranty (§ 2-313). Merchantable goods must be fit for their ordinary purpose and pass without objection in the trade (§ 2-314). A 22% seal-failure rate against a trade expectation of under 2% fails both standards.

FlexPak will argue that seal performance "varies with how the pouch is filled," so Tidewater's sample test proves nothing. But Tidewater tested a random sample of 400 pouches, and FlexPak offers no evidence that the pouches meet the standard when filled properly; the failure rate is ten times the trade's tolerance. **The tender was nonconforming**, and because this was a single-delivery contract, not an installment contract, perfect tender applies.

**Was the rejection effective and rightful?** Rejection must occur within a reasonable time after delivery, with seasonable notice to the seller (§ 2-602); the notice must state defects that the seller could cure if told (§ 2-605); and the rejecting buyer must hold the goods with reasonable care at the seller's disposition and not act as owner (§§ 2-602, 2-604).

- *Timing.* Tidewater inspected two days after delivery and rejected the next day, well within a reasonable time.
- *Notice and particularity.* The email stated the specific defect ("zipper seals fail at a rate of over 20%"), giving FlexPak what it needed to cure.
- *Care of goods.* Tidewater kept the pouches in its warehouse "awaiting your instructions," stored them at a cost of $300 and released them to FlexPak. It never used them, so it did not accept them by an act inconsistent with FlexPak's ownership.

**The rejection was rightful and effective.**

**Seller's right to cure (§ 2-508).** Under § 2-508(1), when the time for performance has not expired, the seller may cure by seasonably notifying the buyer of its intent and then making a conforming delivery *within the contract time*. The deadline was September 1, and FlexPak offered replacements by September 12. That is outside the contract time, so § 2-508(1) gives FlexPak no right to cure. A technician visit is not a conforming tender either.

Under § 2-508(2), a seller that had reasonable grounds to believe the tender would be acceptable may have a further reasonable time to substitute a conforming tender. FlexPak will argue that it reasonably believed its pouches were acceptable: it shipped its standard product, and the 2024 order caused no complaints. Tidewater will respond that FlexPak had no reasonable ground to think a 22% failure rate acceptable when the purchase order said the pouches "must reseal" and the trade tolerates under 2%. Even if § 2-508(2) applied, a "further reasonable time" is measured by the buyer's known needs: FlexPak knew of the September 15 launch, and a September 12 delivery left no time to fill and ship. **FlexPak had no enforceable right to cure.**

**FlexPak's claim for the price.** A seller may recover the price only for goods the buyer accepted, goods lost after risk passed, or identified goods that cannot reasonably be resold (§ 2-709). Tidewater rightfully rejected and FlexPak took the pouches back. **FlexPak cannot recover the $20,000.**

### 3. Tidewater's damages

After a rightful rejection the buyer may cancel and either cover and recover the cover price minus the contract price, or recover market price minus contract price, plus incidental and consequential damages, less expenses saved (§§ 2-711, 2-712, 2-713, 2-715).

**Cover.** Cover must be a reasonable purchase of substitute goods, made in good faith and without unreasonable delay (§ 2-712). Tidewater ordered substitutes from PrintLine on August 30, two days after rejecting.

FlexPak will argue that paying a "rush price" of $0.65 was unreasonable when another supplier quoted $0.56. But that supplier could not deliver until September 22, a week after the launch, so it was not a reasonable substitute for a buyer with a known date. The cover test asks whether the buyer acted reasonably at the time, not whether the cheapest possible deal was found. **The cover was reasonable**: $26,000 − $20,000 = **$6,000**.

**Incidental damages.** Incidental damages include expenses reasonably incurred in the inspection, receipt, transportation, care and custody of rightfully rejected goods, and commercially reasonable expenses incident to effecting cover (§ 2-715(1)). The $300 storage charge and the $1,200 expedited freight on the substitute pouches qualify: **$1,500**.

**Consequential damages ($15,000).** A buyer recovers consequential losses resulting from general or particular requirements and needs of which the seller *at the time of contracting had reason to know* and which could not reasonably be prevented by cover or otherwise (§ 2-715(2)(a); *Hadley v. Baxendale*).

- *Reason to know.* The purchase order told FlexPak the pouches were for a September 15 launch at Coastline Markets and that the product goes stale without a seal. Lost launch profit was squarely within FlexPak's contemplation.
- *Prevention by cover.* FlexPak will argue that Tidewater could have avoided the loss. But Tidewater covered within two days, chose the fastest available supplier, and still received pouches only on September 6; it could not reasonably have done more.
- *Causation and certainty.* FlexPak may argue that Tidewater's own filling time caused the three-day delay. But for FlexPak's nonconforming tender, Tidewater would have had pouches on August 25, three weeks before the launch. The loss is proved with reasonable certainty because Coastline cut its order by a stated 25% under the launch agreement, producing a calculable $15,000 profit loss, not a speculative estimate.

**The $15,000 is recoverable**, assuming, as concluded in call 1, that the exclusion is not part of the contract.

**Net recovery.** Tidewater paid nothing and owes nothing. It recovers $6,000 (cover) + $1,500 (incidental) + $15,000 (consequential) = **$22,500**. If a court instead held the clause part of the contract, the result turns on § 2-719: if the exclusive replacement-or-refund remedy is enforced, Tidewater owes nothing for the rejected pouches but recovers no damages; if that remedy failed of its essential purpose but the exclusion of "incidental or consequential damages" is enforced, it recovers only the $6,000 cover differential; and a court that treats the exclusion as falling with the failed remedy would still award the full $22,500.

**Common mistakes**
1. Applying the mirror-image or "last shot" rule. Under § 2-207 the acknowledgment is an acceptance, and the printed term is only a proposal.
2. Treating every remedy limitation as automatically material (or automatically immaterial). The comments point both ways; the facts (disclosed launch, staleness) decide it.
3. Allowing cure after September 1 under § 2-508(1). The first subsection is limited to the contract time.
4. Calling the cover unreasonable because a cheaper supplier existed. A supplier that delivers after the launch is not reasonable cover.
5. Forgetting § 2-715(2)(a): consequential losses must be ones the seller had reason to know of *when contracting* and that cover could not prevent.
