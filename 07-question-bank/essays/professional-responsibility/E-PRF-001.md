# E-PRF-001 — Priya Shah (conflicts, confidentiality, fees)

## Facts

Priya Shah is a solo practitioner licensed in California. In 2024 LiftCo Rentals, Inc., which leases forklifts to warehouses, hired Priya to draft its standard equipment lease. Priya spent several weeks with LiftCo's operations manager learning how LiftCo inspects and services its forklifts and drafted the lease's clauses allocating brake maintenance between LiftCo and its customers. LiftCo paid her $8,000 and has not used her since.

In February 2026, Tomas Reyes, an independent delivery driver, was picking up freight at a warehouse run by Harbor Warehousing when a Harbor employee lost control of a LiftCo forklift leased to Harbor. A pallet fell on Tomas, breaking his leg. Tomas met with Priya, who agreed to sue LiftCo for a defective brake system. Priya sent Tomas an email: "I'll take your case for 40% of whatever we recover. Costs come out of your share." Tomas replied by email, "OK, thanks!" There was no other fee agreement.

A week later, Harbor's human-resources director, Gwen, called Priya. Gwen said Harbor "wants LiftCo to take responsibility," offered to advance up to $25,000 in litigation costs, and asked Priya to "keep Harbor in the loop." Priya agreed. She did not tell Tomas about the arrangement. Each month she emailed Gwen a summary of her litigation strategy, and she once attached Tomas's hospital records. A Harbor forklift operator later told Priya privately that Harbor had skipped the forklift's scheduled brake inspection. Priya did not name Harbor as a defendant and did not pursue that information.

Tomas, unable to work, told Priya he might be evicted. Priya lent him $3,000 for rent. Tomas signed a one-line note: "I will repay Priya $3,000 out of any settlement." Priya gave him no other document.

At a bar-association lunch in June, Priya told a table of lawyers, "My forklift client tested positive for marijuana in the ER. LiftCo doesn't know yet, so let's hope it stays that way." None of the lawyers represented LiftCo.

In September, LiftCo's counsel moved to disqualify Priya because of her 2024 work. The motion was pending when, in October, LiftCo settled for $300,000. Priya deposited the check in her client trust account. The same day, she transferred $120,000 to her operating account as her fee and paid Harbor $25,000 to reimburse the costs it had advanced. When Priya sent Tomas the balance, he objected that a 40% fee was excessive for a case that settled before any depositions, and that he had never agreed to repay Harbor. Priya replied that the money had already been disbursed.

## Call of the Question

What ethical violations, if any, has Priya committed? Answer according to California and ABA authorities.

## Model Answer

### Governing law

California lawyers are bound by the California Rules of Professional Conduct (CRPC) and the State Bar Act (Bus. & Prof. Code § 6000 et seq.). The ABA Model Rules (MR) are not binding in California but are persuasive, and the call asks for both. Each duty below states the ABA rule, then the California rule, then the application.

### 1. Duty of loyalty — former client LiftCo

**ABA:** Under MR 1.9(a), a lawyer who formerly represented a client may not represent another person in the same or a substantially related matter in which that person's interests are materially adverse to the former client unless the former client gives informed consent, confirmed in writing. Matters are substantially related if they involve the same transaction or legal dispute, or if there is a substantial risk that confidential information normally obtained in the prior representation would materially advance the new client's position.

**California:** Rule 1.9(a) is the same, except that consent must be informed *written* consent (rule 1.0.1(e-1)).

**Application.**
- *Former client and adversity.* LiftCo was Priya's client in 2024. Suing LiftCo for Tomas is directly and materially adverse.
- *Substantial relationship.* Priya will argue that the 2024 work was transactional and finished, and that a form lease is not the same matter as a personal-injury suit. But the suit turns on who was responsible for the forklift's brakes, and Priya drafted the very clauses allocating brake maintenance between LiftCo and its customers. She also spent weeks learning LiftCo's internal inspection and service practices, confidential information that would materially help Tomas prove that LiftCo's maintenance was deficient. There is a substantial risk that this information would advance Tomas's case.
- *Consent.* LiftCo did not consent, as its motion to disqualify shows.

**Conclusion:** Priya violated MR 1.9(a) and CRPC rule 1.9(a). The court would likely have granted the disqualification motion, because California courts disqualify a lawyer whose former representation is substantially related to the current one (*H.F. Ahmanson*; *SpeeDee Oil*), and Priya may face fee consequences as well.

### 2. Duty of loyalty — Harbor's payment and influence

**(a) Third-party payment.**
*ABA:* MR 1.8(f) permits a lawyer to accept compensation for representing a client from someone other than the client only if the client gives informed consent, there is no interference with the lawyer's independent professional judgment or with the lawyer-client relationship, and the client's information is protected under MR 1.6.
*California:* Rule 1.8.6 is the same but requires the client's informed *written* consent.
*Application:* Harbor advanced the litigation costs. Priya never told Tomas, so there was no consent of any kind. Worse, Harbor asked to be "kept in the loop," and Priya treated Harbor as entitled to her strategy, which is exactly the interference with the relationship and with confidentiality that the rule forbids.

**(b) Material-limitation conflict.**
*ABA:* MR 1.7(a)(2) prohibits representation if there is a significant risk that it will be materially limited by the lawyer's responsibilities to a third person or by the lawyer's own interests, unless the lawyer reasonably believes she can represent the client competently and diligently and the client gives informed consent, confirmed in writing.
*California:* Rule 1.7(b) is the same, with informed written consent.
*Application:* Harbor is not a neutral benefactor. A Harbor employee was driving the forklift, and Harbor apparently skipped the scheduled brake inspection. Harbor is a potential defendant, and its interest in "LiftCo taking responsibility" is directly opposed to Tomas's interest in recovering from every responsible party. Priya's decisions not to name Harbor and not to pursue the skipped-inspection evidence suggest her judgment was in fact limited by her arrangement with the payor. Priya may argue that suing only the deep-pocketed lessor was a reasonable tactical choice, but she made it without telling Tomas of the conflict, so he could not evaluate it. The conflict was likely consentable only with full disclosure; there was none. Ignoring the operator's report of a skipped brake inspection also raises competence and diligence concerns (MR 1.1, 1.3; CRPC rules 1.1, 1.3).

**Conclusion:** Priya violated MR 1.8(f) and 1.7(a)(2), and CRPC rules 1.8.6 and 1.7(b). She also violated the duty to communicate significant developments (MR 1.4; rule 1.4; B&P § 6068(m)) by concealing the arrangement.

### 3. Duty of confidentiality

**ABA:** MR 1.6(a) prohibits revealing information relating to the representation unless the client gives informed consent, the disclosure is impliedly authorized to carry out the representation, or an exception in MR 1.6(b) applies (for example, preventing reasonably certain death or substantial bodily harm, or certain client crimes or frauds).

**California:** B&P § 6068(e)(1) requires a lawyer "to maintain inviolate the confidence, and at every peril to himself or herself to preserve the secrets, of his or her client," and rule 1.6 permits disclosure only with informed consent or to prevent a criminal act likely to cause death or substantial bodily harm (§ 6068(e)(2)). Protected information includes embarrassing or detrimental information, whether or not privileged.

**Application.**
- *Monthly reports and medical records to Harbor.* Tomas never consented. Sharing strategy and hospital records with a third party, and a potential adverse party at that, was not impliedly authorized to carry out the representation; it served Harbor. No exception applies. Because Tomas, the privilege holder, never consented, Priya's unauthorized disclosures probably do not waive his attorney-client privilege (Evid. Code §§ 912, 954), but they handed a potential adversary his strategy and may forfeit work-product protection for her summaries.
- *The bar lunch.* Tomas's positive marijuana test is information relating to the representation and is both embarrassing and harmful, because it could support a comparative-fault defense. Priya revealed it to other lawyers for gossip. It does not matter that no LiftCo lawyer was present; the duty bars any unauthorized disclosure. No exception applies (no threat of death or bodily harm, no client crime or fraud).

**Conclusion:** Priya violated MR 1.6(a), CRPC rule 1.6 and B&P § 6068(e)(1) twice. The result is the same under both systems, although California's statutory duty is stricter.

### 4. Fees — the contingent-fee agreement and its amount

**(a) Form of the agreement.**
*ABA:* MR 1.5(c) requires a contingent-fee agreement in a writing signed by the client stating the method of calculating the fee, including the percentages for settlement, trial or appeal, the litigation expenses to be deducted and whether they are deducted before or after the fee is calculated, and any expenses the client must pay whether or not he prevails.
*California:* B&P § 6147 requires a contingency agreement signed by both lawyer and client, with a copy to the client, stating the contingency rate, how costs will affect the fee and the client's recovery, how the lawyer will be paid for related matters not covered, and that the fee is not set by law and is negotiable. If the agreement does not comply, it is voidable at the client's option, and the lawyer is then entitled only to a reasonable fee.
*Application:* Priya's email states a 40% rate and that "costs come out of your share," but it does not distinguish settlement, trial and appeal, does not say whether costs are deducted before or after the fee is computed, and was not signed by both parties in any formal sense (Tomas's "OK, thanks!" reply is at most an informal electronic assent). It omits the § 6147 negotiability statement and the related-matters term entirely.
*Conclusion:* The agreement violates MR 1.5(c) and does not comply with § 6147. Tomas may void it, limiting Priya to a reasonable fee (quantum meruit).

**(b) Amount.**
*ABA:* MR 1.5(a) prohibits an unreasonable fee, judged by factors including the time and labor required, the difficulty of the questions, the customary fee, the amount involved and results obtained, and whether the fee is contingent.
*California:* Rule 1.5(a) prohibits an *unconscionable* or illegal fee, judged as of the time of the agreement, considering factors including the amount of the fee in proportion to the value of the services, the relative sophistication of lawyer and client, the client's informed consent, and any failure to disclose material facts.
*Application:* Priya will argue that 40% is within the customary range for personal-injury work and that she bore the risk of recovering nothing. Tomas will respond that the case settled before any depositions, so Priya's work was modest, and that he is an unsophisticated client who received no explanation. Because California judges unconscionability at formation, and a 40% contingency is not shocking when made, the fee is likely not unconscionable; under the ABA's lower "unreasonable" standard it is closer, given the early settlement. Because the agreement is voidable under § 6147, the practical result is that Priya can keep only a reasonable fee.
*Conclusion:* Likely no violation of CRPC rule 1.5(a); an arguable violation of MR 1.5(a).

### 5. Loan for Tomas's rent — financial assistance

**ABA:** MR 1.8(e) prohibits a lawyer from providing financial assistance to a client in connection with pending or contemplated litigation, except advancing court costs and litigation expenses, paying them for an indigent client, and modest gifts for basic living expenses to an indigent client the lawyer represents pro bono.

**California:** Rule 1.8.5 bars paying a client's personal expenses, but permits a lawyer, after being retained, to lend money to the client on the client's written promise to repay, provided the lawyer first complies with rules 1.7(b)–(c) and 1.8.1. A loan to be repaid from the settlement also gives the lawyer a pecuniary interest adverse to the client, which under rule 1.8.1 requires fair terms disclosed in writing, written advice to seek independent counsel, and the client's informed written consent.

**Application.** A rent loan is a personal living expense, not a litigation cost, and Priya is not acting pro bono, so it violates MR 1.8(e) regardless of documentation. In California, the loan was made after retention, and Tomas signed a written promise to repay, so the loan is permitted in principle. But Priya gave no written disclosure, no advice to consult independent counsel, and obtained no informed written consent to the repayment-from-settlement term.

**Conclusion:** Priya violated MR 1.8(e). In California the loan itself was allowed, but she likely violated rule 1.8.5's conditions by not complying with rule 1.8.1.

### 6. Settlement funds — the disputed fee and the payment to Harbor

**ABA:** MR 1.15 requires client funds to be held in trust, separate from the lawyer's funds; the lawyer must promptly deliver funds the client is entitled to, and when the lawyer and client (or a third person) claim interests in funds, the disputed portion must be kept separate until the dispute is resolved.

**California:** Rule 1.15 is the same in substance: the lawyer may withdraw her share only when it is fixed, a disputed portion must stay in the trust account until the dispute is resolved, and California adds specific timing standards (notice of receipt within 14 days; a presumption of violation if undisputed funds are not distributed within 45 days).

**Application.**
- *The fee.* Priya deposited the settlement in trust, which was proper. But Tomas disputed the fee, and the § 6147 defect means Priya's entitlement to 40% was genuinely uncertain. Priya moved the full $120,000 to her operating account the same day, before Tomas had seen an accounting. Once Tomas objected, the disputed portion had to be in trust; by disbursing it unilaterally and then refusing to restore it, Priya kept disputed funds in her own account.
- *The payment to Harbor.* Harbor had no agreement with Tomas, and Tomas never agreed to reimburse Harbor. Harbor's claim was at least disputed. Priya will argue that Tomas agreed that "costs come out of your share," but that term let her recover costs she advanced, not repay a third party whose involvement Tomas never knew of. Paying $25,000 of Tomas's settlement to Harbor without his consent was a disbursement of client money to a third party without authority.

**Conclusion:** Priya violated MR 1.15(d)–(e) and CRPC rule 1.15. She must restore the disputed amounts to trust (or pay them to Tomas) pending resolution; Tomas may also invoke fee arbitration.

### Summary

Priya violated the former-client conflict rule (both), the third-party-payment and material-limitation rules (both; California also required written consent), the duty of confidentiality (both; § 6068(e)), the contingent-fee formalities (both; voidable under § 6147), the financial-assistance rule (ABA outright; California because she skipped rule 1.8.1's steps), and the trust-account rules (both). The fee amount is likely not unconscionable in California but arguably unreasonable under the ABA.

**Common mistakes**
1. Saying "California is the same" on the rent loan. The ABA forbids it; California permits a post-retention loan with a written promise to repay, subject to rules 1.7 and 1.8.1.
2. Treating Harbor only as a fee payor. Harbor is a potential defendant, which creates a material-limitation conflict.
3. Arguing confidentiality was not breached because the marijuana result was not privileged. Confidentiality covers all information relating to the representation, privileged or not.
4. Forgetting that a non-compliant § 6147 agreement is voidable, leaving only a reasonable fee.
5. Treating the fee as "earned" once the case settles. Once the client disputes it, the disputed portion stays in trust.
