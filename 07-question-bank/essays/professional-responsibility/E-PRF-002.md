# E-PRF-002 — Marco Bell (candor, fairness, communication, withdrawal)

## Facts

Marco Bell is a second-year associate at Hart & Lowe LLP in Los Angeles. His supervising partner is Diane Hart. In 2025 the firm's client, Ivy Chen, sold her three-store bakery chain to Felix Ortega for $2.4 million. In January 2026 Felix sued Ivy in Los Angeles County Superior Court for fraud, alleging that Ivy overstated the chain's 2024 revenue. Felix is represented by Nina Park. Diane assigned the day-to-day work to Marco.

At her deposition in March 2026, Ivy testified that the 2024 revenue figures she gave Felix were "accurate to the dollar." Marco then filed a motion for summary judgment supported by excerpts of Ivy's deposition testimony and by a declaration from Ivy repeating that the figures were accurate.

In April, while preparing for the hearing, Ivy told Marco privately: "Between us, my bookkeeper added about $400,000 of catering orders that never happened. Nobody can prove it." Ivy then handed Marco a folder of emails between herself and the bookkeeper discussing "year-end adjustments" and said, "Make sure these never see the light of day." The emails were responsive to a document request Felix had served in February. Marco moved them into an electronic folder labeled "Do not produce," did not list them on any privilege log, and served a supplemental response, which he signed, stating that "all responsive, non-privileged documents have been produced." Marco did not discuss Ivy's admission with her and did not withdraw the summary-judgment motion.

In its opposition, Felix's counsel did not cite a recent published decision of the California Court of Appeal holding that a seller's revenue representations remain actionable even when the buyer conducts its own due diligence. Marco knew of the decision, which squarely rejected the main argument in his motion. His reply brief did not mention it.

On May 1, Nina sent Marco a letter: "Mr. Ortega will dismiss with prejudice if Ms. Chen pays $300,000 by May 15." Marco showed it to Diane, who said, "Ivy will never pay that. Don't bother her with it." Marco did not tell Ivy, and the offer lapsed.

On May 20, Marco ran into Felix at a coffee shop. Marco said, "This case could go away for a lot less than you think. Call me directly if you want to talk without Nina."

Ivy then stopped paying the firm's monthly invoices. After three unpaid months and two written warnings, Marco filed a motion to withdraw as counsel. The motion stated: "Counsel cannot continue because the client has admitted to counsel that her deposition testimony was false, and she has not paid our fees." The court has not ruled.

## Call of the Question

What ethical violations, if any, has Marco committed? Answer according to California and ABA authorities.

## Model Answer

### Governing law; Marco's status as an associate

California lawyers are governed by the California Rules of Professional Conduct (CRPC) and the State Bar Act (Bus. & Prof. Code); the ABA Model Rules (MR) are persuasive. Marco is a subordinate lawyer. Under both MR 5.2 and CRPC rule 5.2, a lawyer is bound by the rules even when acting at another person's direction; he is protected only when he follows a supervisor's *reasonable resolution of an arguable question* of professional duty. Diane, as supervising partner, is separately responsible if she orders or ratifies a violation (MR 5.1; rule 5.1).

### 1. Candor toward the tribunal — the false deposition testimony

**ABA:** MR 3.3(a)(3) forbids offering evidence the lawyer knows is false. If the lawyer's client has offered material evidence and the lawyer comes to know of its falsity, the lawyer must take reasonable remedial measures, including, if necessary, disclosure to the tribunal. The comments describe the sequence: remonstrate with the client confidentially and seek her cooperation in correcting the evidence; if that fails, seek to withdraw; if withdrawal will not undo the effect of the false evidence, disclose to the tribunal. Under MR 3.3(c), these duties continue to the end of the proceeding and apply even if compliance requires disclosure of information otherwise protected by MR 1.6. MR 3.3 applies to evidence given in proceedings ancillary to the litigation, such as depositions.

**California:** Rule 3.3(a)(3) likewise forbids offering evidence the lawyer knows is false and requires reasonable remedial measures when material false evidence has been offered, but disclosure to the tribunal is permitted only if it is not prohibited by B&P § 6068(e) and rule 1.6. Because California's only confidentiality exception concerns preventing a criminal act likely to cause death or substantial bodily harm, California remedial measures stop short of revealing the client's confidences: remonstrate, refuse to use the false evidence, and seek to withdraw. Separately, rule 1.2.1 (MR 1.2(d)) forbids assisting a client's fraud, and B&P § 6068(d) requires a lawyer never to mislead a judge by artifice or false statement.

**Application.**
- *Knowledge and materiality.* Before April, Marco did not know the testimony was false, so filing the motion was proper. Ivy's admission that $400,000 of orders "never happened" gave him actual knowledge that her deposition testimony and declaration were false. Revenue accuracy is the central fact in a fraud case, so the evidence is material.
- *Evidence "offered."* The deposition testimony was given in an ancillary proceeding and, more importantly, Marco himself put it and Ivy's declaration before the court in support of the pending motion.
- *What Marco did.* Nothing. He did not remonstrate with Ivy, did not withdraw the motion or the declaration, and continued toward a hearing at which the court would rely on evidence he knew to be false. Continuing to press the motion also assists Ivy's fraud on the court.
- *What he had to do.* Under both systems, he had to talk to Ivy and urge her to correct the testimony, and he could not continue to rely on the false evidence (for example, he should withdraw the motion or the false declaration). If Ivy refused, he had to seek to withdraw. Under the ABA, if withdrawal would not remedy the false evidence, he would ultimately have to disclose it to the court. In California he could not disclose Ivy's confidential admission; his duty would end with remonstration, refusing to use the evidence, and withdrawal.

**Conclusion:** Marco violated MR 3.3(a)(3) and 1.2(d), and CRPC rule 3.3(a)(3), rule 1.2.1 and B&P § 6068(d), by continuing to rely on testimony he knew was false and by taking no remedial measures. The systems differ only on the last step: the ABA may require disclosure; California forbids it.

### 2. Fairness to the opposing party — the bookkeeper emails

**ABA:** MR 3.4(a) forbids unlawfully obstructing another party's access to evidence or unlawfully altering, destroying or concealing a document having potential evidentiary value, or counseling or assisting another to do so; MR 3.4(d) forbids failing to make reasonably diligent efforts to comply with a proper discovery request. MR 4.1 and 8.4(c) forbid knowingly false statements and dishonesty.

**California:** Rule 3.4(a) is the same, and rule 3.4(b) adds an express prohibition on suppressing evidence the lawyer or client has a legal obligation to reveal or produce. Rule 1.2.1 forbids assisting a violation of any law or rule, and rule 8.4(c) and B&P § 6106 reach dishonesty and intentional misrepresentation.

**Application.** The emails were responsive to Felix's February document request, so Ivy had a legal obligation to produce them unless a privilege applied. Communications between Ivy and her bookkeeper are not attorney-client communications, and Marco did not even list them on a privilege log; there was no good-faith basis to withhold them. Ivy's instruction ("make sure these never see the light of day") was a request to conceal evidence, and Marco carried it out by segregating the emails. He then signed a supplemental response falsely stating that all responsive documents had been produced. Marco may argue that he only "moved" the files and did not destroy them, but concealment and suppression are enough. Acting at Ivy's direction is no defense, and nothing here was an "arguable question" that would protect a subordinate.

**Conclusion:** Marco violated MR 3.4(a) and (d), 4.1 and 8.4(c), and CRPC rules 3.4(a)–(b), 1.2.1 and 8.4(c) (and B&P § 6106). He should have advised Ivy that the emails must be produced and refused to conceal them.

### 3. Communication — the $300,000 written offer

**ABA:** MR 1.4(a)(1) requires a lawyer to promptly inform the client of any decision or circumstance requiring her informed consent; comment [2] explains that a lawyer who receives a settlement offer in a civil case must promptly inform the client unless the client has previously indicated that such an offer is acceptable or unacceptable. Under MR 1.2(a), whether to settle is the client's decision.

**California:** Rule 1.4.1 and B&P § 6103.5 expressly require a lawyer to promptly communicate the amount, terms and conditions of any written settlement offer in a civil matter; rule 1.2(a) gives the settlement decision to the client.

**Application.** Nina's May 1 letter was a written offer to dismiss with prejudice for $300,000 with a deadline. Ivy had never told the firm that such an offer was unacceptable; Diane merely predicted that Ivy "will never pay that." That prediction is not authority. With the evidence of fraud mounting, a $300,000 dismissal might have been very attractive to Ivy. By not telling her, Marco took the settlement decision away from her, and the offer lapsed.

**Diane's instruction.** Marco will argue that he followed his supervisor. But the duty to transmit a written offer is clear, not an arguable question, so rule 5.2 / MR 5.2 does not protect him. Diane separately violated rule 5.1 / MR 5.1 (and rules 1.4.1 and 1.2(a)) by ordering the omission.

**Conclusion:** Marco violated CRPC rules 1.4.1 and 1.2(a), B&P § 6103.5, and MR 1.4(a)(1) and 1.2(a). The rules reach the same result; California's is more specific.

### 4. Communication with a represented person

**ABA and California (same):** Under MR 4.2 and CRPC rule 4.2, in representing a client a lawyer may not communicate about the subject of the representation with a person the lawyer knows is represented by another lawyer in the matter, unless that lawyer consents or the communication is authorized by law or court order.

**Application.** Marco knew Felix was represented by Nina. His remark that the case "could go away for a lot less than you think" concerned settlement of this very case, and his invitation to "talk without Nina" was an effort to bypass counsel. It does not matter that the meeting was by chance or that Felix spoke first, if he did; the rule protects the represented person even when he is willing to talk. Parties may speak directly to each other, but a lawyer may not.

**Conclusion:** Marco violated MR 4.2 and CRPC rule 4.2.

### 5. Candor — directly adverse controlling authority

**ABA and California (same):** A lawyer must disclose to the tribunal legal authority in the controlling jurisdiction known to the lawyer to be directly adverse to the client's position and not disclosed by opposing counsel (MR 3.3(a)(2); CRPC rule 3.3(a)(2); see also B&P § 6068(d)).

**Application.** A published California Court of Appeal decision binds California trial courts, so it is controlling authority in Los Angeles Superior Court. It "squarely rejected" the main argument in Marco's motion, so it was directly adverse, Marco knew of it, and Felix's counsel did not cite it. Marco may argue that he had no duty to do opposing counsel's research, but the rule exists precisely for authority the opponent missed. Marco could have cited it and argued that it is distinguishable.

**Conclusion:** Marco violated MR 3.3(a)(2) and CRPC rule 3.3(a)(2).

### 6. Withdrawal

**(a) Grounds.**
*ABA:* MR 1.16(a) requires withdrawal if the representation will result in a violation of the rules or other law, and (since 2023) if the client persists in using the lawyer's services to commit or further a crime or fraud. MR 1.16(b) permits withdrawal on grounds including that the client fails substantially to fulfill an obligation to the lawyer regarding fees after reasonable warning, or that the representation has been rendered unreasonably difficult by the client.
*California:* Rule 1.16(a) requires withdrawal when the lawyer knows or reasonably should know that the representation will result in a violation of the rules or the State Bar Act. Rule 1.16(b) permits withdrawal on grounds including a client's breach of a material term of an agreement regarding fees after reasonable warning, and conduct by the client that renders the representation unreasonably difficult or that seeks to pursue a fraudulent course.
*Application:* Ivy's three months of nonpayment after two written warnings is a permissive ground under both systems. If Ivy insists on using her false testimony and concealing the emails, continued representation would require Marco to violate rules 3.3 and 3.4, which makes withdrawal mandatory under both.

**(b) Court permission and protecting the client.** Under MR 1.16(c)–(d) and CRPC rule 1.16(c)–(d), when a matter is pending in court the lawyer must obtain the tribunal's permission and continue if ordered, and on withdrawal must take reasonable steps to avoid foreseeable prejudice to the client (notice, time to find new counsel, return of the file). Marco properly filed a motion rather than simply abandoning the case.

**(c) The confidential disclosure in the motion.**
*California:* B&P § 6068(e)(1) and rule 1.6 forbid revealing confidential information, and no exception covers a client's past false testimony. A lawyer seeking to withdraw should say only that professional considerations require termination of the representation. By telling the court that Ivy "admitted … her deposition testimony was false," Marco revealed a client confidence in a public filing. Revealing the fee dispute in detail was also unnecessary.
*ABA:* The comment to MR 1.16 likewise says a statement that professional considerations require termination should ordinarily suffice. Marco will argue that MR 3.3(c) required him to disclose the false testimony. But disclosure under MR 3.3 is the last remedial step, after remonstration and withdrawal fail to cure the false evidence; Marco skipped remonstration entirely, so the disclosure was premature and not shown to be necessary.

**Conclusion:** Withdrawal itself was justified and properly sought by motion. The disclosure in the motion violated B&P § 6068(e)(1) and CRPC rule 1.6 and likely violated MR 1.6 as premature; under the ABA, disclosure might eventually have become mandatory had Ivy refused to correct the record.

**Common mistakes**
1. Importing the criminal-defendant "narrative" approach. This is a civil case; the question is the general remedial-measures duty.
2. Saying California requires disclosure to the court as a last resort. California's remedial measures stop short of disclosure barred by § 6068(e).
3. Accepting "my supervisor told me to" as a defense. Rule 5.2 protects only a reasonable resolution of an *arguable* question.
4. Treating the coffee-shop remark as harmless because Felix is a party who may talk to anyone. The rule binds the lawyer, not the parties.
5. Forgetting that a motion to withdraw should state only that professional considerations require withdrawal.

**Accuracy notes.** The model answer avoids the numbering of CRPC rule 1.16(b)'s permissive grounds (repo item V-457) and the California criminal-perjury narrative approach (V-461), which is not needed in a civil case. It also avoids the ABA's 2023 duty-to-inquire language in MR 1.16(a) (V-470).
