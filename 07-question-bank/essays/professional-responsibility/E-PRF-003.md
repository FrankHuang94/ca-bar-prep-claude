## Facts

Paige Moreno is a California lawyer with a solo personal-injury practice. Her social-media ads show a photograph of Paige standing in front of a courthouse with the words: "Injured? We've recovered over $40 million for our clients! NO FEE UNLESS WE WIN." Beneath is a quotation: "Paige got me $1.2 million in six months! — Tom R." The $40 million figure is accurate, and Tom R. is a real former client whose case settled for $1.2 million. The ads contain no other text. Paige's standard retainer requires clients to repay all case costs whether or not they recover anything.

Paige pays Rick, a tow-truck driver, $300 for every accident victim who signs a retainer with her. At crash scenes Rick hands drivers Paige's card and says, "She's the best. Call her before the insurance company calls you." Paige also mails letters to people named in police accident reports, offering a free consultation. Neither the letters nor their envelopes contain the word "Advertisement."

In May 2025 Victor called Paige after Rick gave him her card at the scene of a collision. Victor signed Paige's one-page retainer, which reads in full: "Client agrees to pay Attorney 40% of any recovery. Client will reimburse all costs." Paige did not sign it or give Victor a copy.

Paige asked Leo, an experienced trial lawyer at another firm, to try Victor's case. Paige and Leo agreed orally to split the fee equally. Paige told Victor, "Leo will help at trial at no extra cost to you," and said nothing more about Leo's compensation. Paige handled the pretrial work; Leo prepared and conducted the trial, with Paige attending.

On the second day of trial the defendant settled for $300,000. Paige deposited the check in her client trust account on March 3, 2026. Inland Valley Hospital had earlier sent Paige written notice of a $40,000 lien for Victor's treatment. On March 4 Paige transferred $120,000, her 40% fee, to her operating account, and sent Leo $60,000 from it. On March 10 she transferred another $25,000 from the trust account to pay her office rent, intending to replace it "when the next settlement comes in." She told Victor about the settlement on April 20 and replaced the $25,000 on April 22. On April 25 she sent Victor $140,000, explaining that she was holding $40,000 "for the hospital" and that "costs are being worked out." Victor objected that he had never agreed that Paige and Leo would share his fee and that 40% was "too much for a case that settled before the verdict."

While working out the fee split, Leo saw Paige's trust-account ledger showing the March 10 rent transfer. He emailed Paige: "That rent transfer is a big problem for you. I'll forget I saw it if you make my share 60%." Leo has not contacted the State Bar.

## Call of the Question

Answer according to California and ABA authorities.

1. What ethical violations, if any, has Paige committed?
2. What ethical violations, if any, has Leo committed, and what must Leo do about the rent transfer?

## Model Answer

### Sources of law

California lawyers are governed by the California Rules of Professional Conduct (CRPC), the State Bar Act (Bus. & Prof. Code § 6000 et seq.) and California case law. The ABA Model Rules (MR) are not binding in California but are persuasive where California has no rule. Each issue below states the ABA rule, then the California rule, and concludes under each.

### 1. Paige

**A. The social-media ad**

*Rule.* ABA and California: a lawyer may not make a false or misleading communication about the lawyer's services, including one that omits a fact necessary to make the statement as a whole not materially misleading (MR 7.1; CRPC 7.1). Truthful statements can mislead if they create unjustified expectations. California's State Bar standards also presume misleading a testimonial without an appropriate disclaimer. Both rules require an advertisement to include the name and office address of at least one responsible lawyer or firm (MR 7.2; CRPC 7.2).

*Application.*
- *"$40 million recovered."* True and verifiable; standing alone, likely not misleading.
- *The testimonial.* "Paige got me $1.2 million in six months!" is true, but it invites viewers to expect a similar result in a similar time, and there is no disclaimer that results depend on each case. Under California's standards a testimonial without a disclaimer is presumptively misleading; under the ABA it likely creates an unjustified expectation (MR 7.1 cmt.).
- *"NO FEE UNLESS WE WIN."* Paige's retainer makes clients repay costs whatever the outcome. Telling injured people they owe nothing unless they win, while omitting that they may owe costs, omits a fact necessary to make the statement not misleading. This violates both rules.
- *Identification.* The ad shows Paige's photograph and her first name in the quotation but contains "no other text": no full name of a responsible lawyer and no office address. That violates the identification requirement under both rules.

*Conclusion.* Paige violated CRPC 7.1–7.2 and MR 7.1–7.2.

**B. Paying Rick: recommendations, runners and solicitation**

*Paying for recommendations.* ABA and California: a lawyer may not give anything of value to a person for recommending or securing the lawyer's employment, except the reasonable cost of advertising, the usual charges of a qualified referral service or legal services plan, the purchase of a practice, and limited reciprocal arrangements (MR 7.2(b); CRPC 7.2(b)). Paying $300 for each client who signs is a per-referral payment that fits no exception. Paige may argue Rick is a "marketing" vendor, but a payment per signed client is compensation for securing employment, not the cost of advertising. **Violation of both rules.**

*Runners and cappers (California).* The State Bar Act forbids using a runner or capper, a person paid to solicit business for a lawyer (B&P § 6152). Rick is one. **Paige violated § 6152**; the ABA has no statute, but MR 7.2 and 8.4 reach the same conduct.

*Solicitation through an agent.* ABA and California: a lawyer may not solicit professional employment by live person-to-person contact when a significant motive is the lawyer's pecuniary gain, unless the person is a lawyer or has a family, close personal or prior professional relationship with the lawyer (MR 7.3(b); CRPC 7.3(a)). A lawyer may not violate the rules through the acts of another (MR 8.4(a); CRPC 8.4(a)). Rick speaks in person to strangers at crash scenes, at the moment they are most vulnerable, and urges them to call Paige "before the insurance company calls you." That is the in-person solicitation the rules forbid (*Ohralik v. Ohio State Bar Ass'n*), carried out by Paige's paid agent for Paige's pecuniary gain. **Violation of both rules.**

**C. The targeted letters**

*ABA.* Targeted written solicitation of people known to need legal services is protected commercial speech and is permitted if it is truthful and not coercive (*Shapero v. Kentucky Bar Ass'n*). Since 2018 the Model Rules have not required an "advertising" label. **No ABA violation** on these facts.

*California.* CRPC 7.3 requires every written, recorded or electronic communication soliciting employment from a person known to need legal services in a particular matter to include the word "Advertisement" (on the envelope, and at the beginning and end of a recorded or electronic message), unless the recipient is a lawyer or has a close or prior relationship with the lawyer, or it is apparent from context that the communication is an advertisement. Paige's letters go to strangers named in accident reports and carry no label. **Violation of California's rule.**

**D. Victor's contingent-fee agreement**

*ABA (MR 1.5(c)).* A contingent fee agreement must be in a writing signed by the client, state the method of calculating the fee (including the percentages for settlement, trial and appeal), the litigation expenses to be deducted and whether they are deducted before or after the fee is calculated, and any expenses the client must pay whatever the outcome; at the end the lawyer must give a written statement of the outcome and the computation. Victor signed, and the agreement states 40% and that he must reimburse costs. But it does not say whether costs are deducted before or after the 40% is calculated, and Paige's April 25 letter ("costs are being worked out") is not the required closing statement. **Violation.**

*California (B&P § 6147).* The agreement must be signed by both lawyer and client, with a signed duplicate given to the client, and must state the fee rate, how costs will affect the fee and the client's recovery, what the client may have to pay for related matters, and that the fee is not set by law and is negotiable. Paige did not sign or give Victor a copy, and the agreement omits the cost, related-matter and negotiability statements. Noncompliance makes the agreement **voidable at Victor's option**, leaving Paige a reasonable fee. **Violation.**

*Amount.* ABA: a fee may not be unreasonable (MR 1.5(a)). California: a fee may not be unconscionable, judged as of when the agreement was made (CRPC 1.5(a)–(b)). The case settled only after full pretrial work and two days of trial, and a contingent fee reflects the risk assumed at the outset; 40% is high but not unusual for a litigated injury case. It is likely neither unreasonable nor unconscionable, but if Victor voids the agreement under § 6147, Paige recovers only a reasonable fee.

**E. Dividing the fee with Leo**

*ABA (MR 1.5(e)).* Lawyers not in the same firm may divide a fee only if the division is in proportion to the services each performs or each assumes joint responsibility for the representation, the client agrees to the arrangement, including the share each lawyer will receive, and the agreement is confirmed in writing, and the total fee is reasonable. A 50-50 split may roughly match the work (Paige's pretrial work, Leo's trial), and both lawyers handled the case, but Victor never agreed to any split or to the shares, and nothing was put in writing. **Violation.**

*California (CRPC 1.5.1).* Lawyers not in the same firm may divide a fee only if (1) they enter into a written agreement to divide it; (2) the client consents in writing, at the time of the agreement or as soon as reasonably practicable, after full written disclosure that the fee will be divided, of the identity of the lawyers, and of the terms of the division; and (3) the total fee is not increased because of the division. California does not require proportionality. The lawyers' agreement was oral, and Victor received only an oral statement that Leo would help "at no extra cost," which disclosed neither the division nor its terms. Only the third requirement is met. **Violation by both Paige and Leo.**

*Consequences.* A lawyer who divides a fee without the client's written consent cannot enforce the division against the other lawyer (*Chambers v. Kay*), though he may recover the reasonable value of his services in quantum meruit (*Huskinson & Brown v. Wolf*).

**F. The settlement funds**

*Rules.* ABA and California: client funds must be kept in a trust account and not commingled with the lawyer's own; the lawyer must promptly notify the client of their receipt (California: within 14 days absent good cause), account in writing and promptly distribute undisputed funds (California presumes a violation after 45 days). A disputed portion must stay in trust until resolved, and funds subject to a third person's lawful claim must be protected (MR 1.15; CRPC 1.15). In California, misappropriating client funds is ordinarily moral turpitude (B&P § 6106).

*Application.*
1. *Deposit.* Paige properly deposited the check in her trust account.
2. *Notice.* She received the funds on March 3 and told Victor on April 20, 48 days later, with no apparent good cause. That violates California's 14-day standard and the ABA's "promptly," and the duty to keep the client informed of significant developments (MR 1.4; CRPC 1.4; B&P § 6068(m)).
3. *The $25,000 rent transfer.* Using client money for office rent is misappropriation under both rules; her intent to replace it, and the April 22 repayment, are no defense. **This is the most serious violation**, and in California an act of moral turpitude.
4. *The hospital lien.* Paige had written notice of the hospital's $40,000 lien and has kept $40,000 in trust. That complies with both rules, provided she resolves the claim promptly.
5. *The fee.* Paige took her full 40% the day after deposit, before telling Victor of the settlement, under a voidable agreement, and paid Leo under a split Victor never approved. Victor now disputes both. By withdrawing the fee before he knew of the recovery, she deprived him of the chance to object; she should restore or otherwise protect the disputed amount until the dispute is resolved.
6. *Accounting.* Her April 25 letter gives no written accounting of costs or of the computation, violating both rules.

*Conclusion.* Paige violated MR 1.15 and CRPC 1.15, the communication rules, and B&P § 6106.

### 2. Leo

**A. The fee division.** Leo is equally bound by MR 1.5(e) and CRPC 1.5.1. He agreed to an oral split, took $60,000, and made no effort to obtain Victor's written consent. **He violated both rules** and cannot enforce the split.

**B. Duty to report the rent transfer**

*ABA (MR 8.3).* A lawyer who **knows** that another lawyer has committed a violation of the rules that raises a substantial question about that lawyer's honesty, trustworthiness or fitness must inform the appropriate professional authority. The rule does not require disclosure of information protected by MR 1.6.

*California (CRPC 8.3, effective August 1, 2023).* A lawyer must, without undue delay, inform the State Bar or a tribunal with jurisdiction when the lawyer knows of **credible evidence** that another lawyer has committed a criminal act or engaged in conduct involving dishonesty, fraud, deceit, reckless or intentional misrepresentation, or **misappropriation of funds or property** that raises a substantial question about the lawyer's honesty, trustworthiness or fitness. The rule does not require disclosure of information protected by B&P § 6068(e) and rule 1.6.

*Application.* Leo saw the ledger entry himself. That is credible evidence (California) and gives him knowledge (ABA) that Paige used $25,000 of client money for rent. Misappropriation of client funds is one of the categories California lists and plainly raises a substantial question about Paige's honesty and trustworthiness. Repayment does not remove the duty. Leo must report.

*Confidentiality.* Leo learned this as Victor's co-counsel, and how Victor's settlement funds were handled relates to Victor's representation; § 6068(e) protects client secrets broadly, and both rules excuse reporting only to the extent it would reveal protected information. But Victor is the victim and has every reason to want it reported. Leo should promptly obtain Victor's informed consent and then report. Confidentiality cannot justify silence that protects Paige rather than Victor.

*Conclusion.* Leo's failure to report, without undue delay, violates CRPC 8.3 and MR 8.3 unless he promptly obtains Victor's consent and reports.

**C. "I'll forget I saw it if you make my share 60%"**

*California (CRPC 3.10).* A lawyer may not threaten to present criminal, administrative or disciplinary charges to obtain an advantage in a civil dispute. Leo's email implies that he will report Paige to the State Bar unless she increases his share of the fee, which is a civil dispute between them. **Violation.** The demand may also amount to extortion, an act of moral turpitude (B&P § 6106).

*ABA.* There is no counterpart to rule 3.10. But offering to suppress a report the rules require, in exchange for money, is conduct prejudicial to the administration of justice and dishonest (MR 8.4(c), (d)), and if it amounts to extortion it is a criminal act reflecting on fitness (MR 8.4(b)). It also confirms Leo's knowing breach of MR 8.3. **Violation.**

### Conclusion

Paige violated the advertising, referral-payment, solicitation, contingent-fee, fee-division and trust-account rules under both systems, plus California's labeling and runner rules. Leo violated the fee-division rules, must report Paige's misappropriation (with Victor's consent), and violated CRPC 3.10 and MR 8.4 by offering to stay silent for a larger share.

**Common mistakes**
1. Applying the ABA proportionality requirement to California. CRPC 1.5.1 allows pure referral fees but requires a written lawyer agreement and the client's written consent after written disclosure.
2. Excusing the $25,000 transfer because Paige replaced it. Intent to repay is no defense to misappropriation.
3. Stating California's rule 8.3 as "knows of a violation." California requires reporting on **credible evidence**, but only for the listed categories.
4. Forgetting that the "Advertisement" label and rule 3.10 are California-only rules.
5. Treating the contingency agreement as void. Under § 6147 it is voidable at the client's option, leaving a reasonable fee.
