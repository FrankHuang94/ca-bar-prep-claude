## Facts

Rosalind Park, a California domiciliary, died on March 2, 2025, without a will. Her husband, Theo, had died in 2019. Her probate estate, after debts and expenses, is worth $900,000.

Rosalind's family:
- **Anna**, 52, Rosalind's daughter from her first marriage, which ended in divorce in 1984. Anna has one child, **Gwen**, 24.
- **Ben**, 40, Theo's son from Theo's first marriage to Lucia. Ben lived with Rosalind and Theo from 1991, when he was six and they married, until he left for college. In 1994 Theo asked Lucia to consent to Rosalind's adopting Ben; Lucia refused. Ben turned 18 in 2003. Rosalind called Ben "my son," paid much of his college tuition and listed him as her son in a church directory, but never took any further step to adopt him. In 2016 she told a friend, "Papers don't matter between Ben and me."
- **Carl**, 34, the son of Rosalind and Theo. Carl was unmarried and had no children.
- **Dana**, 29, whom Rosalind and Theo adopted in 1996, when she was two months old.

**Lifetime gifts.** In June 2021 Rosalind gave Dana a $60,000 check toward a house; the memo line read "Dana's house." That day Dana texted Rosalind: "Thank you Mom!! I know this comes out of what I'd get someday. Love you." In 2022 Rosalind wrote in her diary, "Dana has had her share early." In 2020 Rosalind had given Anna $20,000 for a car; nothing about it was put in writing.

**The crash.** On the evening of March 2, 2025, Carl was driving Rosalind home from dinner when the car left the road on a straight, dry stretch and struck a tree. Rosalind was pronounced dead at the scene at 9:40 p.m. Carl died in the hospital at 5:40 a.m. on March 6. His blood-alcohol level was 0.11%. A gas-station clerk saw Carl and Rosalind arguing loudly about 15 minutes before the crash and heard Carl shout, "You never help me!" A week earlier Rosalind had refused to lend Carl $50,000 to pay gambling debts. A traffic camera shows the car drifting across its lane and leaving the road without braking. The district attorney closed the case after Carl died.

**Insurance.** Rosalind's $200,000 life insurance policy names "my son, Carl Park" as sole beneficiary. It provides that if no beneficiary survives the insured, the proceeds are paid to the insured's estate. It contains no other survival requirement.

**Carl's estate.** Carl died without a will, leaving a condominium and savings worth $150,000 after debts.

**Anna's disclaimer.** Anna is financially comfortable and wants her share of her mother's estate to go to Gwen. She waited until the administrator filed the estate's inventory and appraisal in December 2025, and on January 20, 2026, she filed in the probate proceeding a signed writing disclaiming "all my interest in the estate of my mother, Rosalind Park." She has received nothing from Rosalind's estate, which has not been distributed.

## Call of the Question

Answer according to California law.

1. How should Rosalind's probate estate be distributed? Discuss the claims of Anna, Gwen, Ben, Carl's estate and Dana.
2. Who is entitled to the proceeds of Rosalind's life insurance policy?
3. How should Carl's estate be distributed?

## Model Answer

### 1. Rosalind's probate estate

**A. Framework.** Property not effectively disposed of by will passes by intestate succession (Prob. Code § 6400). Rosalind left no will and no surviving spouse (Theo died in 2019), so her entire estate passes to her issue (§ 6402(a)), divided by representation: equal shares at the first generation below her with a living member, one share for each living member and one for each deceased member who left issue (§ 240). An heir must survive the decedent by 120 hours (§ 6403). The question is who counts as Rosalind's issue.

**B. Anna and Dana.** Anna is Rosalind's natural child (§ 6450(a)). An adopted person is the child of the adopting parent for intestate succession (§ 6450(b)), so Dana, adopted by Rosalind in infancy, inherits exactly as a natural child would. Both are heirs.

**C. Ben**

*Stepchild statute (§ 6454).* A stepchild inherits from a stepparent only if (1) the relationship began during the child's minority, (2) it continued throughout their joint lifetimes, and (3) clear and convincing evidence shows that the stepparent would have adopted the child but for a legal barrier.
1. The relationship began in 1991, when Ben was six. **Met.**
2. It continued until Rosalind's death: she called him her son, paid for college and listed him as her son. **Met.**
3. *Legal barrier.* Lucia's refusal to consent in 1994 was a legal barrier, and Ben will argue that the request shows Rosalind wanted to adopt him. But the barrier must persist until the stepparent's death (*Estate of Joseph*). Once Ben turned 18 in 2003, Rosalind could have adopted him as an adult without Lucia's consent. She lived 22 more years without doing so and said, "Papers don't matter between Ben and me," which shows that her own choice, not a legal barrier, explains why there was no adoption. **Not met.**

Ben cannot inherit as a stepchild.

*Equitable adoption (§ 6455; Estate of Ford).* A child may inherit from a person who intended to adopt but never completed the adoption if clear and convincing evidence shows an intent to adopt, such as an express or implied agreement, and conduct consistent with it. A close and loving relationship alone is not enough. Ben will rely on the 1994 request and Rosalind's lifelong treatment of him as a son. But the 1994 request was Theo's request to Lucia, made while the barrier existed; after it lifted, Rosalind made no promise and took no step to adopt, and her 2016 statement shows she chose not to. **Equitable adoption likely fails.**

*Predeceased-spouse property.* Ben might argue that part of Rosalind's estate came from Theo and should pass to Theo's issue. That rule (§ 6402.5) applies only when the decedent leaves no surviving spouse **and no issue**. Rosalind left issue, so it does not apply.

**Ben takes nothing from Rosalind's estate** (but see Call 3).

**D. Carl and the 120-hour rule.** For intestate succession, a person who fails to survive the decedent by 120 hours is deemed to have predeceased the decedent; if survival by 120 hours cannot be established by clear and convincing evidence, the person is deemed not to have survived (§ 6403). The rule does not apply if it would cause escheat.

Rosalind died at 9:40 p.m. on March 2; Carl died at 5:40 a.m. on March 6. He survived her by **80 hours**, short of 120. Carl is therefore **deemed to have predeceased Rosalind**. He left no issue, so no share is set aside for his line, and his estate takes nothing from Rosalind's estate. Escheat is not a concern because Rosalind has other issue.

The stakes are significant: had Carl survived 120 hours, he would have taken one-third of Rosalind's estate, and that share would then have passed through Carl's estate in part to Ben (Call 3).

**E. Advancements**

*Rule (§ 6409).* A lifetime gift to an heir counts as an advancement against the heir's intestate share **only if** (1) the decedent declared in a contemporaneous writing that it was an advancement or was to be deducted from the heir's share, or (2) the heir acknowledged in writing that the gift was an advancement or was to be deducted. The gift is valued when the heir received it or at the decedent's death, whichever is first, unless the decedent's contemporaneous writing or the heir's written acknowledgment fixes a value.

*Dana's $60,000.*
- *Rosalind's writings.* The memo line "Dana's house" is contemporaneous but describes the purpose, not an advancement. The 2022 diary entry ("Dana has had her share early") declares an advancement but was written a year later, so it is not contemporaneous. Neither satisfies § 6409(a)(1).
- *Dana's text.* The heir's acknowledgment need not be contemporaneous, only written. Dana's text, "I know this comes out of what I'd get someday," acknowledges that the gift is to be deducted from her inheritance. Dana will argue that an affectionate text is not a legal acknowledgment and that a text message is not a "writing." But a typed message that Dana composed and sent is a written statement in her own words, and its meaning, that the money "comes out of" her inheritance, is exactly what the statute describes. **The $60,000 is likely an advancement.** No writing fixed a value, so it is valued at **$60,000**, its value when Dana received it.

*Anna's $20,000.* Nothing was written by Rosalind or Anna. Dana will argue fairness, but § 6409 requires a writing. **It is not an advancement.**

**F. Anna's disclaimer**

*Rule.* An heir may disclaim an interest by a signed writing filed within a reasonable time after learning of the interest. A disclaimer filed within nine months of the decedent's death is conclusively presumed timely; otherwise the disclaimant bears the burden of showing that the time was reasonable. No disclaimer is allowed after the heir has accepted the interest. Unless the instrument provides otherwise, the disclaimed interest passes as if the disclaimant had predeceased the decedent, and the disclaimer relates back to the date of death (§§ 275–285).

*Application.* Anna's disclaimer is a signed writing filed in the probate proceeding, and she has accepted nothing. It was filed on January 20, 2026, about ten and a half months after the death, so the conclusive presumption does not apply and Anna must show reasonableness. She knew of her interest from the day her mother died, and the statute's nine-month benchmark suggests the Legislature's view of a normal period. But she waited only until the inventory showed what she was giving up, filed six weeks later, while the estate was still open and before any distribution, and no one has relied on her delay or been prejudiced by it. **The disclaimer is likely timely and effective.**

*Effect.* Anna is treated as having predeceased Rosalind. Under § 240 the estate is still divided at the children's generation, where Dana is alive: one share for Dana and one for Anna, a deceased member with issue. **Gwen takes Anna's share by representation.**

**G. Distribution**
- Hotchpot: $900,000 + $60,000 advancement = $960,000 ÷ 2 = $480,000 per share.
- **Gwen** (Anna's share): **$480,000**.
- **Dana**: $480,000 − $60,000 = **$420,000**.
- Ben and Carl's estate: nothing.

If the slayer rule applied (Call 2), the $200,000 insurance proceeds would be added to the estate: $1,160,000 ÷ 2 = $580,000, so Gwen would take $580,000 and Dana $520,000.

### 2. The life insurance proceeds

**A. Nonprobate contract.** A life insurance beneficiary designation is a nonprobate transfer that passes under the policy, not by intestacy (§ 5000). The intestacy rules, including the 120-hour rule, do not govern it.

**B. Survival.** The 120-hour rule of § 6403 applies only to intestate succession. A beneficiary under an instrument must survive the transferor (§ 21109), and if the insured and the beneficiary both die and it cannot be established by **clear and convincing evidence** that the beneficiary survived, the proceeds are paid as if the insured survived the beneficiary (§§ 220, 224). Here the coroner's and hospital records establish clearly that Carl outlived Rosalind by 80 hours. The policy adds no other survival period. So, unless the slayer rule applies, **Carl's estate is entitled to the $200,000.** (Under the Uniform Probate Code a 120-hour requirement would also apply to the policy; California's Probate Code does not impose one.)

**C. The slayer rule**

*Rule.* A named beneficiary of a life insurance policy who feloniously and intentionally kills the insured is not entitled to any benefit under the policy, which becomes payable as though the killer had predeceased the insured (§ 252; see § 250). A final conviction of a felonious and intentional killing is conclusive; without one, the court decides whether the killing was felonious and intentional by a preponderance of the evidence, and the party seeking to establish it bears the burden (§ 254). An accidental or merely negligent or reckless killing is not intentional.

*Who argues what.* If the rule applies, there is no surviving beneficiary and the proceeds go to Rosalind's estate, where they pass to Gwen and Dana. If it does not, they pass through Carl's estate to Anna, Ben and Dana. Gwen and Dana therefore bear the burden of proving an intentional killing; Ben opposes. There was no conviction, so the probate court decides.

*Evidence of intent.* Carl had a motive: gambling debts, a refused $50,000 loan and his position as sole beneficiary. Minutes before the crash he and Rosalind were arguing and he shouted, "You never help me!" The car left a straight, dry road without braking, which Gwen and Dana will say suggests a deliberate act.

*Evidence against intent.* Carl's blood-alcohol level was 0.11%, which readily explains a car that drifts out of its lane without braking. He crashed his own car and died; a person planning to kill for insurance money does not usually choose a method that kills himself, and a dead beneficiary could not use the proceeds to pay his debts. Angry words in an argument are not a threat to kill, and no one heard him say anything about harming his mother.

*Conclusion.* Driving while intoxicated may be felonious, but the statute also requires an **intentional** killing. The evidence fits a drunk and angry driver better than a planned killing, so Gwen and Dana likely cannot prove intent by a preponderance. **The slayer rule likely does not apply, and the $200,000 goes to Carl's estate.**

### 3. Carl's estate

Carl died intestate with no spouse and no issue. Both parents predeceased him: Theo in 2019 and Rosalind before him in the crash. His estate therefore passes to the issue of his parents "or either of them," taking equally if they are of the same degree of kinship (§ 6402(c)). Relatives of the half blood inherit the same share as relatives of the whole blood (§ 6406).
- **Anna** is Rosalind's child, a half-sister through Rosalind.
- **Ben** is Theo's natural child, a half-brother through Theo. Ben's failure to qualify as Rosalind's child is irrelevant here; he takes as Theo's issue.
- **Dana**, adopted by both Rosalind and Theo, is their child for all intestacy purposes (§ 6450(b)) and therefore Carl's full sister.

All three survived Carl by more than 120 hours and are of the same degree, so each takes one-third. Anna's disclaimer covered only her mother's estate, so she takes her share of Carl's estate herself, and Gwen takes nothing here.

Carl's estate: $150,000 + $200,000 insurance = $350,000. **Anna, Ben and Dana each take about $116,667.**

**Common mistakes**
1. Applying the 120-hour rule to the life insurance. In California it governs intestate succession only; instruments use survival by clear and convincing evidence.
2. Treating Ben as an heir of Rosalind because she raised him. § 6454 requires a legal barrier lasting until death, and equitable adoption requires proof of an intent to adopt.
3. Forgetting that Ben is a full heir of Carl as Theo's issue, and that half-blood siblings take equal shares.
4. Requiring the heir's acknowledgment of an advancement to be contemporaneous; only the decedent's declaration must be.
5. Treating the slayer rule as satisfied by a drunk-driving death. The killing must be both felonious and intentional.
