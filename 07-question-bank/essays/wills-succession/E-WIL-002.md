## Facts

In 2015, Rafael Ortiz, then divorced, executed a will that was properly signed and witnessed. His only child at the time was his adult daughter, Sofia. The will provides:

> 1. I give my sailboat, the *Marigold*, to my friend Jorge Lin.
> 2. I give $50,000 to my brother, Tomás Ortiz.
> 3. I give $30,000 to my longtime housekeeper, Elena Díaz.
> 4. I give my house on Cypress Lane to my daughter, Sofia.
> 5. I give the residue of my estate to my daughter, Sofia.

In 2021, Rafael married Nina. In 2022, he bought a $200,000 life insurance policy on his life and named Nina as beneficiary. At a dinner that year, he told Jorge, "I'm not changing my will. Nina's taken care of with the insurance, and the house stays with Sofia." Nina never signed any agreement waiving her rights in Rafael's estate.

In 2023, Rafael and Nina had a son, Leo. Rafael put $25,000 into a custodial account for Leo under the California Uniform Transfers to Minors Act and told Nina, "This will get Leo started on college."

In 2024, the *Marigold* was destroyed in a fire at the marina. Rafael filed a claim with his insurer, which approved a $60,000 payment but had not paid it when Rafael died suddenly in 2026, domiciled in California. A few months after the fire, Rafael told Jorge that he would "buy another boat when the insurance money comes through, and you'll get that one instead." He never changed his will.

Elena had cleaned Rafael's house every week for twenty years, and Rafael often said she was "like family." Tomás and Rafael spoke by phone every Sunday. Sofia, who lives in the Cypress Lane house, has told the executor that she cannot afford to buy out anyone else's interest in it.

At his death, Rafael's probate estate consisted of the Cypress Lane house (worth $700,000), bank accounts holding $90,000, and the right to the $60,000 insurance payment for the *Marigold*. Debts, funeral costs and administration expenses total $70,000. The insurer paid the $200,000 life insurance proceeds to Nina. Leo's custodial account now holds $27,000. Assume that all of Rafael's property was his separate property.

Nina, Leo (through his guardian), Sofia, Jorge, Tomás and Elena all claim shares of the estate.

## Call of the Question

Answer according to California law.

1. What rights, if any, does Nina have in Rafael's estate?
2. What rights, if any, does Leo have in Rafael's estate?
3. What rights, if any, do Jorge, Tomás, Elena and Sofia have in Rafael's estate?

## Model Answer

**Preliminary: the will and the gifts.** Rafael's 2015 will was properly signed and witnessed, so it is a valid attested will (Prob. Code § 6110). Rafael died domiciled in California, and all of his property is SP, so the will and California's family-protection statutes govern the whole probate estate. Gifts are classified as specific (identified property), general (payable from general assets), demonstrative, or residuary (§ 21117):
- the *Marigold* to Jorge and the Cypress Lane house to Sofia are **specific**;
- $50,000 to Tomás and $30,000 to Elena are **general**;
- the residue to Sofia is **residuary**.

### 1. Nina

**A. Is Nina an omitted spouse?** A surviving spouse who married the decedent **after** the execution of all of the decedent's testamentary instruments, and who is **not provided for** in them, takes an omitted spouse's share unless an exception applies (§ 21610). Rafael's only testamentary instrument is the 2015 will; he married Nina in 2021 and never made a new will or codicil. The will makes no provision for Nina. She qualifies unless an exception applies.

**B. Exceptions.** The omitted spouse takes nothing if (1) the failure to provide was intentional and that intent appears in the testamentary instruments; (2) the decedent provided for the spouse by transfer outside the will, with the intent that the transfer be **in lieu of** a testamentary provision, as shown by the decedent's statements, the amount of the transfer, or other evidence; or (3) the spouse validly waived the right to share in the estate (§ 21611).
1. *Intent on the face of the will.* The 2015 will was written six years before the marriage and does not mention Nina or any future spouse. This exception does not apply.
2. *Waiver.* Nina signed no waiver, so this exception does not apply.
3. *Transfer in lieu.* This is the real dispute.
   - *Sofia's argument.* In 2022, a year after the marriage, Rafael bought a $200,000 life insurance policy naming Nina. He then explained his reason: "I'm not changing my will. Nina's taken care of with the insurance, and the house stays with Sofia." That statement ties the insurance to his decision not to provide for Nina in the will, which is precisely what "in lieu of a testamentary provision" means. The amount is substantial: about a quarter of his probate estate.
   - *Nina's argument.* Life insurance is ordinary financial planning for a new family, especially with a child on the way; "taken care of" is casual dinner conversation, not a deliberate estate plan; and a policy is not evidence that Rafael meant to cut her out of the probate estate.
   - *Likely result.* § 21611 expressly allows the court to rely on the decedent's statements and the amount of the transfer. Rafael's statement links the insurance directly to leaving his will unchanged, and the amount is significant. **The in-lieu exception likely applies, and Nina takes no omitted-spouse share.**

**C. Nina's share if her claim succeeded.** An omitted spouse receives the decedent's one-half of the CP and QCP and a share of the SP equal to the spouse's intestate share, **but not more than one-half** of the SP (§ 21610). Here all the property is SP. In intestacy, a spouse takes one-third of the SP when the decedent leaves more than one child (§ 6401(c)). Rafael left two children, Sofia and Leo, so Nina's share would be one-third of the net estate: $780,000 ÷ 3 = **$260,000**. That is under the one-half cap.

**D. The life insurance.** A beneficiary designation is a nonprobate transfer that is valid without will formalities and passes under the policy, not the will (§ 5000). Nina keeps the $200,000 regardless of the outcome of her omitted-spouse claim.

**Conclusion.** Nina likely takes nothing from the probate estate because the life insurance was a transfer in lieu of a testamentary provision. She keeps the $200,000 in insurance proceeds.

### 2. Leo

**A. Is Leo an omitted child?** A child born or adopted after the execution of all the decedent's testamentary instruments, and not provided for in them, receives the share he would have received had the decedent died without any testamentary instrument (§ 21620). Leo was born in 2023, eight years after the will, and the will does not provide for him.

**B. Exceptions (§ 21621).** The child takes nothing if:
1. *The omission was intentional and appears in the will.* The will says nothing about future children.
2. *The decedent had one or more children and left substantially all of the estate to the omitted child's other parent.* Rafael already had Sofia, but the will leaves nothing to Nina, Leo's mother.
3. *The decedent provided for the child outside the will with intent that it be in lieu of a testamentary provision.* Sofia will argue that the $25,000 custodial account was that provision. But Rafael described it only as something to "get Leo started on college," not as a substitute for an inheritance. The amount is small compared with a $780,000 net estate, and, unlike his statement about Nina, Rafael never linked the account to his will.

None of the exceptions applies. **Leo is an omitted child.**

**C. Leo's share: an intestate share.** In intestacy, the surviving spouse takes one-third of the SP when the decedent leaves more than one child (§ 6401(c)), and the rest passes to the issue equally (§ 6402(a)). So if Rafael had died without a will, Nina would take one-third and Sofia and Leo would each take one-third. The omitted-child share does not depend on whether Nina actually takes as an omitted spouse; it is the share Leo would have received in a full intestacy. The net estate is $700,000 + $90,000 + $60,000 − $70,000 = $780,000.

**Leo is entitled to one-third: $260,000.**

### 3. Jorge, Tomás, Elena and Sofia

**A. Jorge: the destroyed sailboat**

*Ademption by extinction.* A specific gift is adeemed if the specific property is not in the estate at death. Under the traditional identity theory, the gift would simply fail. The *Marigold* was destroyed in 2024, so Sofia, as residuary beneficiary, will argue that Jorge's gift is adeemed and that the insurance claim falls into the residue.

*Statutory nonademption.* California disfavors ademption. By statute, the recipient of a specific gift takes, among other things, **any unpaid fire or casualty insurance proceeds** for injury to the property (§ 21133). The insurer approved the $60,000 claim, but it had not been paid at Rafael's death, so the claim remains in the estate as an unpaid casualty recovery for the specifically given property.

*Counterargument.* Sofia may argue that Rafael meant Jorge to have *a boat*, not money: he said Jorge would get the replacement boat, and he never bought one or changed his will. But Rafael's statement confirms that he still wanted Jorge to benefit from the *Marigold*, and § 21133 gives the specific beneficiary the unpaid casualty proceeds without requiring any further proof of intent. Had the insurer paid Rafael before his death and the money been mixed into his accounts, Jorge would have had a much harder claim; on these facts the proceeds were still owed.

**Conclusion.** Jorge takes the right to the **$60,000** insurance payment (subject to funding Leo's share, below).

**B. Abatement for debts and expenses**

*Rule.* When the estate is insufficient to pay all debts, expenses and gifts, the shares abate in this order, unless the will or the testator's plan requires otherwise: (1) property not disposed of by the will; (2) residuary gifts; (3) general gifts to persons who are not relatives; (4) general gifts to relatives; (5) specific gifts to non-relatives; and (6) specific gifts to relatives. Gifts within each class abate pro rata (§ 21402).

*Application.*
- The specific gifts (the house and the boat claim) are not general assets. The only general asset is the $90,000 in bank accounts.
- The $70,000 of debts and expenses are paid first, leaving $20,000.
- The general gifts total $80,000, so there is a $60,000 shortfall.
  1. No property passes outside the will.
  2. The residue abates first: Sofia's residuary gift is reduced to **$0**.
  3. General gifts to non-relatives abate next. Elena, a housekeeper, is not a relative, so her **$30,000 gift abates entirely**, leaving a $30,000 shortfall.
  4. General gifts to relatives abate next. Tomás, Rafael's brother, absorbs the remaining $30,000 and receives **$20,000** of his $50,000.
- The specific gifts to Sofia (the house) and Jorge (the boat claim) do not abate for debts, because the general and residuary assets were enough to pay them.

Elena may argue that Rafael called her "like family" and would not want her cut out while his brother still takes. But § 21402's order yields only where the will or the testator's overall plan shows a contrary intent. The will gives her gift no priority, and an affectionate remark does not make a housekeeper a relative for abatement purposes.

Note also that a specific gift passes subject to any lien on it, and a general "pay my debts" direction does not change that (§ 21131). The facts show no mortgage on the house.

**C. Funding Leo's share; final distribution**

An omitted child's share is satisfied first from estate property not disposed of by the will (there is none here) and then from the will beneficiaries in proportion to the value each receives (§ 21623). After abatement for debts, the beneficiaries are receiving $780,000 in total (house $700,000; boat claim $60,000; Tomás $20,000). Leo's $260,000 is one-third of that, so each beneficiary's gift is reduced by about one-third, unless the court finds another allocation better preserves Rafael's plan. Because Sofia's share is the house and she cannot buy out Leo's interest, the house may have to be sold, or Leo may receive a fractional interest in it.

| Person | Under the will after abatement | After funding Leo (≈ one-third) |
|---|---|---|
| Sofia | House ($700,000); residue $0 | ≈ $466,667 interest in the house (she may need to pay or the house may be sold) |
| Jorge | $60,000 insurance claim | ≈ $40,000 |
| Tomás | $20,000 | ≈ $13,333 |
| Elena | $0 (abated) | $0 |
| Leo | — | $260,000 (omitted child) |
| Nina | — (in-lieu exception) | $0 from the estate; $200,000 insurance outside probate |

If the court rejected the in-lieu exception, Nina would also take $260,000 as an omitted spouse, and the beneficiaries' gifts would be reduced further, by about two-thirds in total.

**Common mistakes**
1. Giving Nina "all the separate property." The omitted-spouse share is the intestate share, capped at one-half.
2. Forgetting that life insurance is nonprobate and stays with Nina whatever happens in probate, and that it can also be the "transfer in lieu."
3. Computing Leo's share without first asking what a spouse would take in a hypothetical intestacy.
4. Adeeming Jorge's gift because the boat is gone, without applying § 21133.
5. Abating by the common-law order (personal property before real property, or pro rata across all general gifts) instead of California's preference for relatives within each class.
