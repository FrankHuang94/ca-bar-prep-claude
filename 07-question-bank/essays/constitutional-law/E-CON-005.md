# E-CON-005 — After the Ridge Fire

## Facts

In 2025 the Ridge Fire swept along Cedar Ridge in State X, killing 14 people and destroying 300 homes. The State legislature responded with the Wildland Safety Act, effective January 1, 2026.

**Section 10** of the Act prohibits any new residential structure in mapped "Extreme Fire Zones." Land in those zones may be used only for grazing, agriculture, passive recreation such as hiking and camping, and one non-habitable storage building of up to 400 square feet. The Act allows no variances. The State Fire Agency's report supporting Section 10 found that homes on the ridge were reachable only by one-lane roads, could not be defended without putting firefighters at serious risk, and had helped the 2025 fire spread from house to house.

**Section 20** provides that any exclusion of wildfire damage in a homeowner's insurance policy "in force on January 1, 2026, is void," and that no insurer may cancel such a policy before its expiration because of wildfire risk. Insurers may not charge additional premiums for the added coverage. State X's insurance commissioner has approved homeowner policy forms and premium rates for decades, and all such policies run for one year.

**Owen.** In 2022 Owen Price paid $1.2 million for a 40-acre parcel on Cedar Ridge, then zoned rural residential, planning to build a vacation home. He spent $150,000 on architectural plans, a well and a gravel driveway, and the County approved his preliminary plans in 2024. In 2026 the parcel was mapped into an Extreme Fire Zone, and the State Fire Agency denied his building permit under Section 10. An appraiser values the parcel at $95,000 for grazing and recreation. A neighboring rancher has offered to lease it for grazing at $4,000 a year.

**Rosa.** Rosa Delgado was a fire-safety inspector for the State Fire Agency for twelve years. Under the State Civil Service Act, permanent employees "may be dismissed only for cause." In 2024 Rosa had reviewed Owen's preliminary plans. On March 9, 2026, a newspaper reported that Owen's architect had paid Rosa $5,000 in 2024. The next morning the Agency's director called Rosa into his office and said, "We know about the architect's payment. Anything to say?" Rosa answered that the money was her fee for teaching a weekend fire-code class the architect's firm had organized, and that she had a signed contract at home. She asked for one day to bring it in. The director replied, "No need," and handed her a letter, already signed, dismissing her "for cause" effective immediately. That afternoon the Agency issued a press release stating that "Inspector Rosa Delgado was fired today for taking bribes from developers." Agency rules entitle a dismissed employee to a full evidentiary hearing before the State Personnel Board; because of a backlog, Rosa's hearing is set for January 2027, ten months after her dismissal. Rosa has applied for 30 jobs and received no offers.

**Summit.** Summit Insurance Company has 9,000 homeowner policies in force in State X with wildfire exclusions. Summit sold them at premiums about 30% below those for policies covering wildfire. It estimates that Section 20 adds $200 million of uninsured exposure.

Rosa has sued the State Fire Agency and its director; Owen has sued the State; and Summit has sued the State insurance commissioner. All the suits are in federal court.

## Call of the Question

1. Did the State Fire Agency violate Rosa's rights under the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment, and what relief may she obtain? Discuss.
2. Is Owen entitled to compensation under the Takings Clause? Discuss.
3. Does Section 20 violate the Contracts Clause? Discuss.

## Model Answer

All three challenges target state action: a state agency, a state statute and a state official.

### 1. Rosa's due-process claims

**Property interest.** The Due Process Clause protects property interests, which arise from a legitimate claim of entitlement created by an independent source such as state law (*Roth*). The Civil Service Act provides that permanent employees "may be dismissed only for cause." That for-cause protection creates an entitlement to continued employment, unlike the at-will or one-year-contract job in *Roth*. Rosa was a permanent inspector of twelve years, and the Agency's dismissal was an intentional deprivation. **Rosa had a protected property interest.**

**Pre-termination process.** Once a protected interest is at stake, the process due is set by balancing (1) the private interest, (2) the risk of erroneous deprivation and the value of added safeguards, and (3) the government's interest (*Mathews v. Eldridge*). For a tenured public employee, *Loudermill* applies that balance: before dismissal the employee is entitled to oral or written notice of the charges, an explanation of the employer's evidence, and an opportunity to present her side, followed by a full post-termination hearing.

- *Notice of the charges.* The director said, "We know about the architect's payment." The Agency will argue this identified the subject. But Rosa was not told the charge (bribery) or the rule she allegedly broke; she learned that only from the press release.
- *Explanation of the evidence.* Nothing was explained beyond the newspaper's report that a payment was made.
- *Opportunity to respond.* The Agency will stress that the pre-termination hearing "need not be elaborate" and that Rosa did respond, explaining the teaching fee. However, the opportunity must be meaningful. Rosa offered specific documentary proof and asked for one day; the director refused, and the dismissal letter was "already signed" before she spoke, showing the meeting could not have changed the outcome.
- *Mathews balance.* Rosa's interest in her livelihood is weighty, especially as she remains unpaid for ten months. The risk of error was high, because the charge rested on a news report and a one-day delay to see a signed contract would have tested it directly. The State's interest in removing a corrupt inspector quickly is real, but it could have suspended Rosa with pay for a day, and nothing suggests a single day posed any danger.

**The pre-termination process was inadequate.**

**Delay in the post-termination hearing.** Due process also requires a meaningful post-termination hearing at a meaningful time. *Loudermill* held that a nine-month administrative delay was not unconstitutional in itself, so the Agency will argue that ten months caused by a backlog is acceptable. Rosa will respond that she is unpaid and branded a bribe-taker throughout, and that the *Mathews* factors favor a prompt hearing when the pre-termination process was so thin. Because *Loudermill* tolerated a similar delay, **the delay alone probably is not a separate violation**, but it heightens the harm of the defective pre-termination process.

**Liberty interest: stigma plus.** Reputation alone is not a protected liberty interest (*Paul v. Davis*). But when the government publicly makes a stigmatizing charge in connection with a change in legal status, such as dismissal from public employment ("stigma plus"), the person is entitled to a hearing to clear her name (*Roth*; *Paul*).
- *Stigma:* "fired... for taking bribes" accuses Rosa of dishonesty and crime, the classic stigmatizing charge.
- *Publication:* the Agency issued it as a press release.
- *Plus:* it accompanied her dismissal the same day.
- *Falsity:* Rosa disputes it and has a contract showing a lawful teaching fee.
The Agency may argue the statement was true, and that a name-clearing hearing need not precede publication: the Personnel Board hearing, where Rosa can rebut the bribery charge, can serve that purpose. That is the Agency's stronger point, because the remedy for a stigmatizing discharge is a chance to clear one's name. But the hearing must come at a meaningful time. The stigma attached the day she was fired, her 30 unsuccessful applications show its effect, and she must wait ten months, publicly branded a bribe-taker, before she can answer. **Rosa has a liberty interest and is entitled to a prompt name-clearing hearing; the ten-month delay likely denies her one at a meaningful time.**

**Defendants and relief.** State sovereign immunity (the Eleventh Amendment) bars suits in federal court against the State and its agencies, whatever relief is sought, absent consent or valid abrogation, and a state agency is not a "person" under § 1983 (*Will*); Rosa's claims against the Agency itself therefore fail there. Under *Ex parte Young* she may sue the director in his official capacity for prospective relief: reinstatement pending a constitutionally adequate hearing and a prompt name-clearing hearing. Under 42 U.S.C. § 1983 she may seek damages from the director personally, subject to qualified immunity; because *Loudermill* clearly established the pre-termination requirements, that defense is weak.

### 2. Owen's takings claim

**Ripeness.** A regulatory takings claim requires a final decision by the regulator. Section 10 allows no variances, and the State denied Owen's permit, so the decision is final. Under *Knick*, a property owner need not first seek compensation in state court before bringing a federal takings claim. **The claim is ripe.** Forum is a separate problem. *Knick* involved a township, but Owen has sued the State itself. A State is not a "person" under § 1983 (*Will*), and lower federal courts have generally held that state sovereign immunity bars a takings damages suit against a State in federal court when the State's own courts are open to the claim. Unless State X waives immunity, Owen should seek compensation in state court (for example, by an inverse-condemnation action); the merits below are the same in either forum.

**Lucas: total deprivation.** A regulation that deprives land of *all* economically beneficial use is a per se taking unless background principles of nuisance and property law already prohibited the use (*Lucas*). Owen will argue that a 40-acre residential parcel that cannot hold a house has been stripped of its economic value. But the parcel is still worth $95,000, a rancher has offered $4,000 a year to lease it for grazing, and the Act permits agriculture, recreation and a storage building. A loss of about 92% is severe, but it is not total; *Lucas* itself acknowledged that an owner who loses 95% of value may not claim the categorical rule and must proceed under *Penn Central*. The State's alternative argument, that building a single home is a common-law nuisance, is weak, because building homes on rural residential land has long been lawful; but it need not be reached. **Lucas does not apply.**

**Penn Central.** The claim is decided under the ad hoc factors of *Penn Central*, applied to the parcel as a whole (*Tahoe-Sierra*; *Murr*).

- *Economic impact.* The value dropped from $1.2 million to $95,000, about 92%. That is a heavy loss, and it is the factor most favorable to Owen. But large diminutions alone have been upheld (*Euclid*), and Owen retains real, if modest, uses.
- *Distinct investment-backed expectations.* Owen bought in 2022 under residential zoning, four years before the Act; he spent $150,000 on plans, a well and a driveway; and the County approved his preliminary plans in 2024. These are concrete, distinct expectations, stronger than in *Palazzolo*, where the owner acquired title after the rules existed. The State will argue that land on a fire-prone ridge in a State that regulates wildfire risk carries a reasonable expectation that use may be restricted, and that preliminary approval was not a vested right to build.
- *Character of the government action.* Section 10 is not a physical invasion; it is a general safety regulation of all mapped zones, adopted after a fire that killed 14 people and spread from house to house. Regulations that prevent serious harm to public safety, rather than appropriating private land for a public benefit, weigh against a taking. Owen will reply that a handful of ridge owners bear the whole cost of protecting the public and firefighters, the kind of disproportionate burden the Takings Clause is meant to spread.

**Conclusion.** The economic impact and Owen's expectations favor him, but the harm-preventing, generally applicable character of the regulation and his retained uses favor the State. Courts rarely find a regulatory taking short of a total wipeout; **Owen's claim will likely fail**, though this is a closer case than most because of his pre-Act investments and preliminary approval.

**Remedy.** If a taking is found, the remedy is just compensation, the fair market value of what was taken, not an injunction against the Act (*Knick*; *First English*).

### 3. Summit and Section 20

**Rule.** No State may pass a law impairing the obligation of contracts. A state law that substantially impairs an existing contractual relationship is valid only if it is an appropriate and reasonable way to advance a significant and legitimate public purpose. When the State is not itself a party to the contract, courts defer to the legislature's judgment about necessity and reasonableness (*Energy Reserves*; *Sveen*).

**Threshold.** Section 20 is state legislation, and it applies retroactively to policies "in force on January 1, 2026," that is, to existing contracts. The Clause therefore applies.

**Substantial impairment.** Courts consider how far the law undermines the contractual bargain, interferes with a party's reasonable expectations, and prevents it from safeguarding or reinstating its rights (*Sveen*).
- *Bargain.* The wildfire exclusion was a central, priced term. Summit charged premiums 30% lower because it excluded wildfire, and Section 20 erases that term, adding about $200 million of exposure without additional premium.
- *Safeguards.* Summit cannot cancel, reprice or reinsure the risk on existing policies; it can act only at renewal.
- *Expectations.* The State will argue that insurance is a heavily regulated industry: the commissioner has approved policy forms and rates for decades, and a regulated party contracts with reason to expect further regulation (*Energy Reserves*). But regulation of rates and forms going forward does not suggest that a priced exclusion will be erased mid-term.

**Section 20 substantially impairs Summit's contracts.**

**Public purpose and means.**
- *Purpose.* Protecting thousands of homeowners from uninsured losses after a catastrophic fire, and ensuring that communities can rebuild, addresses a broad social and economic problem, not a benefit for a narrow special interest. It is significant and legitimate.
- *Means.* Summit will rely on *Allied Structural Steel*, where a sudden, retroactive and severe obligation imposed on a narrow class was struck down, and will contrast *Blaisdell*, where relief was temporary and conditioned on mortgagors paying reasonable rent. Section 20 transfers the whole wildfire risk to insurers and forbids any compensating premium. The State will respond that the impairment is temporary (no policy runs more than a year), that insurers may price wildfire risk at renewal, and that courts defer to the legislature's choice of means in an emergency affecting private contracts.

**Conclusion.** The question is close. Given the deference owed when the State is not a party, the broad public purpose and the one-year limit, a court would likely uphold Section 20; but the absence of any premium adjustment is Summit's strongest point, and a court could find that the law is not a reasonable and appropriate means.

- **75+:** separates Rosa's property and liberty claims; applies the *Loudermill* elements to the meeting with the pre-signed letter; rejects *Lucas* because value remains and applies all three *Penn Central* factors; runs the Contracts Clause in two steps with the *Sveen* factors and deference.
- **65:** correct frameworks and conclusions, but misses stigma-plus or treats *Penn Central* in a single sentence.
- **55:** applies *Lucas* to a 92% loss, or finds no property interest because public employment is a "privilege."
- **45 or below:** discusses "fairness" without the *Mathews*/*Loudermill* framework, or confuses the Contracts Clause with substantive due process.

**Common mistakes**
1. Treating any brief conversation as a sufficient *Loudermill* hearing. The employee must learn the charges and the evidence and have a meaningful chance to respond.
2. Missing the stigma-plus liberty interest when the government publicizes a charge of dishonesty with a dismissal.
3. Applying *Lucas* whenever value drops sharply. *Lucas* requires the loss of all economically beneficial use.
4. Forgetting that the Contracts Clause protects only existing contracts and applies only to state laws; Section 20 is retroactive, so the Clause applies.
5. Ignoring the Eleventh Amendment when a State agency is sued in federal court.

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: PRC law compensates land expropriation and requisition, but has no general doctrine requiring compensation when a regulation sharply reduces land value; the US *Penn Central* test asks whether such a regulation goes "too far" (approx.).
