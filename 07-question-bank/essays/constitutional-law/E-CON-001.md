# E-CON-001 — Signs at City Hall

## Facts

During 2025, tenant activists filled several meetings of the City Council of Marlow, a mid-sized city, to demand rent control. Some meetings ran past midnight. In January 2026, the Council adopted Ordinance 26-4. Its findings state that the ordinance is meant "to prevent crowding and keep the entrances to City Hall clear for persons conducting business with the City."

**Section 1** provides: "On any day the City Council meets, no person shall display any sign, banner or placard on the public sidewalk within 50 feet of any entrance to City Hall, except (a) signs identifying a City-sponsored event, and (b) signs supporting or opposing a candidate in a City election."

**Section 2** provides: "No demonstration, rally or assembly of more than 25 persons shall be held in Founders Park without a permit issued by the Parks Director. Applications must be filed at least 30 days in advance. The Director shall grant or deny a permit as the Director deems consistent with the public interest and the family character of the park."

A violation of either section is punishable by a fine of up to $500. The sidewalk in front of City Hall is 20 feet wide. Marlow already has an ordinance making it unlawful to obstruct a building entrance or block pedestrian traffic. Founders Park, across the street from City Hall, is a five-acre public park with lawns and a bandstand.

Renters United is a nonprofit corporation with 300 members, all Marlow tenants. Its bylaws state that its purpose is "to advocate for affordable housing in Marlow through education, public demonstration and participation in elections." Rent control will be on the November 2026 Marlow ballot as Measure H.

On March 10, 2026, while the Council met, Renters United member Dana Cho stood alone and silently on the City Hall sidewalk, about 30 feet from the main entrance and well away from the doorway, holding a sign reading "FREEZE RENTS NOW — YES ON MEASURE H." A police officer cited her under Section 1, and she paid a $500 fine. That evening, two volunteers stood at the same spot holding large signs for a City Council candidate; they were not cited.

On April 1, Renters United applied for a permit to hold a 150-person rally at the Founders Park bandstand on May 15. The Parks Director denied the application, writing: "Rent control is a divisive topic, and a large political rally would be inconsistent with the family character of the park." The City's own Spring Festival, with about 2,000 attendees, was held in Founders Park in April.

Renters United and Dana sued the City in federal district court under 42 U.S.C. § 1983. They seek a declaration that Sections 1 and 2 violate the First Amendment, an injunction against their enforcement, a refund of Dana's fine and nominal damages. Dana states that she intends to hold her sign outside City Hall at future Council meetings.

After the suit was filed, the City refunded Dana's $500 and sent her a letter stating that the City "will not enforce Section 1 against Ms. Cho while this litigation is pending." The City then moved to dismiss, arguing that Renters United lacks standing because it was never cited and that Dana's claims are moot.

## Call of the Question

1. Do Renters United and Dana have standing, and is Dana's challenge moot? Discuss.
2. Does Section 1 of the ordinance violate the First Amendment? Discuss.
3. Does Section 2 of the ordinance violate the First Amendment, on its face or as applied to Renters United? Discuss.

## Model Answer

**State action.** The ordinance is a City law enforced by City officials, so there is state action. The First Amendment applies to the City through the Fourteenth Amendment, and § 1983 supplies the cause of action; the City is answerable because the ordinance is its own official policy (*Monell*).

### 1. Justiciability

**Dana's standing.** A plaintiff must show (1) an injury in fact that is concrete, particularized and actual or imminent, (2) fairly traceable to the defendant, and (3) likely to be redressed by a favorable decision (*Lujan*). Standing is assessed for each form of relief.

- *Retrospective relief.* Dana was cited and paid a $500 fine under Section 1. That is a concrete, personal, completed injury caused by the City's enforcement, and a refund or nominal damages would redress it.
- *Prospective relief.* For an injunction and declaration, Dana must show a real and immediate threat of future injury. A pre-enforcement challenge is proper when the plaintiff intends to engage in arguably protected conduct that the law arguably proscribes and faces a credible threat of enforcement (*Susan B. Anthony List*). Dana states she will hold her sign at future Council meetings, which Section 1 prohibits, and the City has already enforced it against her. The threat is credible.

**Dana has standing** for all the relief she seeks.

**Renters United's standing.** An organization may sue on behalf of its members if (1) its members would have standing in their own right, (2) the interests at stake are germane to its purpose, and (3) neither the claim nor the relief requires individual members' participation (*Hunt*).

- *Members' standing.* Dana, a member, has standing, as shown above.
- *Germaneness.* The bylaws commit the organization to advocacy through "public demonstration and participation in elections," and Section 1 limits exactly that kind of advocacy outside City Hall.
- *Participation.* Declaratory and injunctive relief benefit all members without individualized proof. But the **refund of Dana's fine** is personal to her and would require her participation, so Renters United cannot seek it; Dana can.

The City's argument that the organization was "never cited" misses the point of associational standing. As to Section 2, Renters United also has standing **in its own right**: it applied for a permit and was denied, a direct injury caused by the Director and redressable by an injunction. **Renters United has standing** to seek declaratory and injunctive relief on both sections.

**Mootness.** A case becomes moot when events after filing eliminate any live controversy. Voluntary cessation does not moot a case unless the defendant shows it is "absolutely clear" that the challenged conduct cannot reasonably be expected to recur (*Laidlaw*).

The City refunded the fine and promised not to enforce Section 1 against Dana "while this litigation is pending." That promise is expressly temporary: once the case ends, the City is free to cite her again, and Section 1 remains on the books against everyone else. The City cannot carry its heavy burden. The refund claim itself is moot, because the City has repaid the $500 and there is nothing left to order. In addition, Dana seeks **nominal damages** for the completed violation, and a claim for nominal damages keeps a case alive even after the challenged conduct stops (*Uzuegbunam*). **Dana's challenge is not moot**, except for the refund claim.

### 2. Section 1: the sidewalk sign ban

**Protected speech in a traditional public forum.** Holding a sign about a ballot measure is core political speech. Public sidewalks are traditional public forums, where content-based restrictions get strict scrutiny and content-neutral restrictions must satisfy the time, place and manner test (*Perry*; *McCullen*). The City Hall sidewalk is an ordinary 20-foot public sidewalk.

**Content-based or content-neutral?** A law is content-based if, on its face, it applies to particular speech because of the topic discussed or the idea or message expressed (*Reed v. Town of Gilbert*). A law that draws lines by location, such as on-premises versus off-premises signs, is content-neutral even if an official must read the sign to apply it (*City of Austin*).

The City will argue that Section 1 is a location-and-time rule: it applies only within 50 feet of the entrances on meeting days, and its stated purpose, preventing crowding, has nothing to do with message. Plaintiffs will respond that the exceptions make the law content-based. Whether a sign is allowed depends on what it says: a sign announcing a City-sponsored event or supporting a candidate is permitted, but Dana's sign about a ballot measure is banned in the same place at the same time. Unlike the location distinction in *City of Austin*, these exceptions turn on the sign's subject matter and function, exactly the distinctions *Reed* held content-based. The facts show the effect: Dana was cited for a ballot-measure sign while volunteers holding candidate signs at the same spot were not. A benign purpose does not save a facially content-based law. **Section 1 is content-based and triggers strict scrutiny.**

**Strict scrutiny.** The City must prove that Section 1 is narrowly tailored, meaning the least restrictive means, to serve a compelling interest (*Reed*; *Brown*).

- *Interest.* Keeping the entrances to a seat of government clear and safe is important, and the City may argue it is compelling given past crowded meetings. Even if it is, the law fails on tailoring.
- *Underinclusive.* Candidate signs and City-event signs crowd the sidewalk exactly as much as ballot-measure signs. Exempting them shows that the City does not regard crowding by signs as a serious problem when it favors the message.
- *Overinclusive.* Section 1 bans even one silent person holding a sign 30 feet from the doorway on a 20-foot sidewalk, as Dana did, causing no obstruction at all.
- *Less restrictive means.* Marlow already prohibits obstructing entrances and blocking pedestrian traffic. That law addresses the actual harm without banning a category of speech.

**Section 1 fails strict scrutiny.**

**Alternatively, as a time, place and manner rule.** Even if Section 1 were content-neutral, the City would have to show that it is narrowly tailored to a significant government interest and leaves ample alternative channels; narrow tailoring forbids burdening substantially more speech than necessary (*Ward*; *McCullen*). The interest in clear entrances is significant, and speakers may stand 50 feet away or speak without signs, which gives the City an argument on alternatives. But *McCullen* struck down a 35-foot buffer zone because the government had not shown that available tools, such as anti-obstruction laws, were inadequate. Marlow has such a law and bans signs within 50 feet on a wide sidewalk regardless of any obstruction. Signs carried 50 feet away also may not reach Council members and residents entering City Hall, the audience that matters for a message about a local ballot measure. **Section 1 likely fails even intermediate scrutiny.**

**Conclusion:** Section 1 violates the First Amendment.

### 3. Section 2: the Founders Park permit requirement

**Facial challenge: prior restraint and unbridled discretion.** A permit requirement for speech is a prior restraint and bears a heavy presumption against validity. A government may require permits for large gatherings in a public park to coordinate competing uses and protect safety, but only if the scheme is content-neutral and uses narrow, objective and definite standards that leave the licensing official no unbridled discretion (*Thomas v. Chicago Park District*; *Forsyth County*; *Shuttlesworth*). A speaker subject to such a scheme may challenge it on its face, whether or not it has applied (*Lakewood*).

Founders Park is a traditional public forum, and requiring permits only for gatherings over 25 persons is a reasonable trigger. But the Director grants or denies permits "as the Director deems consistent with the public interest and the family character of the park." Those phrases contain no objective criteria, such as size limits, scheduling conflicts or safety capacity, and allow the Director to approve speech he likes and deny speech he dislikes, which is what happened here. The City may argue that "family character" reflects a legitimate interest in keeping the park suitable for children. But a standard this open-ended invites content-based decisions that cannot be reviewed effectively. **Section 2 is facially invalid** because of the Director's unbridled discretion.

**The 30-day advance-notice requirement.** A content-neutral permit rule must also be narrowly tailored. The City's interest in planning for large events justifies some advance notice, but a 30-day minimum with no exception sweeps broadly: it prevents a timely large rally in response to fast-moving events such as a Council vote, and the City has not shown why a shorter period would not suffice. Courts vary on how much lead time is acceptable, so this is a secondary argument; it is **likely excessive** as applied to political rallies, but the discretion problem is decisive.

**As applied to Renters United.** The Director denied the permit because "rent control is a divisive topic" and a "large political rally" was inconsistent with the park's character.

- *Content and viewpoint.* The denial was based expressly on the rally's subject (rent control) and its political nature, which is content discrimination in a traditional public forum. It requires strict scrutiny, and the City offers no compelling interest.
- *Heckler's veto.* Treating a message as too "divisive" to permit is a listener-reaction rationale, which the First Amendment forbids; *Forsyth County* rejected letting the anticipated hostility of listeners determine a speaker's access.
- *Pretext and underinclusiveness.* The City hosted a 2,000-person Spring Festival in the same park the same spring, more than thirteen times the size of the proposed 150-person rally. The "family character" of the park was evidently not threatened by large crowds, only by a political message about rent control.

The City might argue the denial served neutral concerns about park use, but its own written reasons and the Festival comparison defeat that claim. **Section 2 is unconstitutional as applied to Renters United.**

**Conclusion:** Plaintiffs have standing, and Dana's claims other than the refund are live. Sections 1 and 2 violate the First Amendment; the court should enter declaratory and injunctive relief and award Dana nominal damages. The refund claim is moot because the City has already repaid the fine.

**Common mistakes**
1. Treating the City's anti-crowding purpose as making Section 1 content-neutral. Under *Reed*, a facially content-based law is content-based regardless of motive.
2. Saying Renters United lacks standing because it was not cited. Associational standing rests on members' injuries; the organization also suffered its own injury on the permit.
3. Declaring Dana's case moot after the refund. The non-enforcement promise is temporary and nominal damages remain.
4. Analyzing only the as-applied denial of the permit. A standardless permit scheme is facially invalid.
5. Using the nonpublic-forum "reasonableness" test for a sidewalk or a park. Both are traditional public forums.
