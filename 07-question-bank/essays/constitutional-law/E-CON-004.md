# E-CON-004 — Winter Haven

## Facts

Fairhaven is a city of 40,000. Grace Fellowship Church owns an 1891 brick sanctuary and an attached fellowship hall in a residential zone. Since 2019, from December through February, the church has run "Winter Haven," hosting up to 12 unhoused people overnight in the fellowship hall, with cots, a hot meal and two volunteers who stay awake all night. The pastor testified that the ministry carries out the congregation's belief that "Scripture commands us to shelter the stranger." The fire marshal inspected the hall in 2025 and found it compliant with the fire code.

After neighbors complained of noise and litter, the City Council adopted Ordinance 26-14 in September 2026: "No building in a residential zone may be used for overnight lodging of persons who do not reside there, except (a) licensed hotels, motels and bed-and-breakfast inns; (b) hospitals and licensed care facilities; and (c) any other use for which the Planning Director grants a waiver for good cause." The ordinance recites the City's interests in "noise control, sanitation, fire safety and neighborhood character." At the hearing, Councilmember Burke said, "Some churches act as if Scripture puts them above the zoning code. This ordinance will remind them it does not." Fairhaven has nine bed-and-breakfasts in residential zones, each hosting up to ten guests a night. In October 2026, the Planning Director granted a waiver to a high-school booster club for an all-night "lock-in" fundraiser for 40 students at a residential community center. She denied Grace Fellowship's waiver request, writing that "homeless shelters are not appropriate in residential neighborhoods."

Since 2025, the City's Historic Facade Program has reimbursed up to $50,000 for exterior restoration of any building on the City's historic register. Program rules exclude "any building owned by a religious organization or used primarily for worship." The City Attorney explained, "Taxpayers should not pay to maintain houses of worship." Grace Fellowship's sanctuary is on the register. Its brick is crumbling, and its stained-glass rose window, which depicts a dove, is cracked. The City rejected its application solely because of the exclusion.

Since 2015, the Mayor has opened each City Council meeting by inviting a local clergy member to give an invocation. All eleven congregations in Fairhaven are Christian, and every invocation has been Christian. Amira Haddad, a Fairhaven resident who leads a weekly Muslim prayer group in a rented room, asked in August 2026 to give an invocation. The Mayor refused in a letter stating, "Our invocation reflects the faith this town was founded on." At the October 2026 meeting, the agenda included Amira's application for a permit to convert a vacant store into a community center for her prayer group. Before the invocation, the Mayor said, "Everyone please rise and bow your heads." When Amira and a few others stayed seated, the Mayor said, "I see some folks don't want to join us. We'll remember who our friends are." Later that evening, the Council tabled Amira's application without discussion.

Grace Fellowship and Amira have sued the City in federal court.

## Call of the Question

1. Is Grace Fellowship likely to succeed on a Free Exercise challenge to Ordinance 26-14 and the denial of its waiver? Discuss.
2. Does the Facade Program's exclusion of Grace Fellowship violate the First Amendment? Discuss.
3. Does the City's invocation practice violate the Establishment Clause? Discuss.

## Model Answer

The City is a state actor, and the Religion Clauses apply to it through the Fourteenth Amendment.

### 1. Grace Fellowship v. Ordinance 26-14

**Standing and ripeness.** A plaintiff needs an injury in fact, traceable to the defendant and redressable by the court (*Lujan*). The ordinance and the waiver denial force Grace Fellowship to end Winter Haven, a ministry it has run every winter since 2019. The City has made a final decision on the waiver, so the claim is ripe, and an injunction would redress the injury. **The church has standing.**

**Sincere belief and burden.** The Free Exercise Clause protects sincere religious practice; courts examine sincerity, not truth (*Ballard*). The pastor testified that sheltering strangers is commanded by Scripture, and the church has done so for seven winters at its own expense, which supports sincerity. The ordinance flatly prohibits the practice in its building. **There is a sincere practice and a substantial burden.**

**Framework.** A neutral law of general applicability that incidentally burdens religion needs only a rational basis (*Smith*). A law that is not neutral, or not generally applicable, must satisfy strict scrutiny (*Lukumi*; *Fulton*; *Tandon*).

**Neutrality.** On its face the ordinance does not mention religion; it regulates "overnight lodging" by anyone. The City will argue it was adopted to address neutral complaints of noise and litter.

Grace Fellowship will point to Councilmember Burke's statement that "some churches act as if Scripture puts them above the zoning code" and that the ordinance "will remind them it does not." Government hostility toward religion violates neutrality (*Masterpiece Cakeshop*), and a law whose object is to suppress a religious practice is not neutral even if facially neutral (*Lukumi*). The City will respond that one member's remark does not show the purpose of the whole Council, and only part of the *Lukumi* Court relied on legislative motive. The statement nonetheless reveals that at least one decisionmaker saw the ordinance as a way to rein in churches, and the ordinance was adopted right after neighbors complained of noise and litter, apparently from Winter Haven. **Neutrality is contestable; the stronger ground is general applicability.**

**General applicability.** A law is not generally applicable if (1) it creates a mechanism for individualized exemptions, or (2) it treats comparable secular activity more favorably than religious exercise; comparability is judged against the risks the government says it is addressing (*Fulton*; *Tandon*).

- *Individualized exemptions.* Exception (c) lets the Planning Director grant a waiver "for good cause," a standard that invites case-by-case judgment about which reasons are worthy. Under *Fulton*, the existence of such a discretionary system makes the law not generally applicable, whether or not waivers are often granted. Here the Director used it in exactly that way: she granted a waiver for an all-night lock-in for 40 students but denied the church's request for 12 guests.
- *Comparable secular activity.* The City's interests are noise, sanitation, fire safety and neighborhood character. Nine bed-and-breakfasts host up to ten strangers each night in residential zones, and the booster-club lock-in put 40 teenagers in a residential building overnight. Those uses create at least the same noise, sanitation and fire risks as 12 sleeping guests supervised by two awake volunteers in a hall that passed fire inspection. The City will argue that bed-and-breakfasts are licensed and inspected, and that unhoused guests present distinct sanitation and security concerns. But the church's hall has been inspected too, and the Director's stated reason ("homeless shelters are not appropriate") rests on the identity of the guests, not on any risk the ordinance names.

**The ordinance, as applied through the waiver system, is not generally applicable, so strict scrutiny applies.**

**Strict scrutiny.** The City must prove that its action is narrowly tailored to a compelling interest. The interest must be shown as applied to this claimant: the question is not whether noise control matters in general, but whether the City has a compelling reason to deny *this* church an exception (*Fulton*).
- *Interest.* Fire safety and sanitation can be compelling in principle, but the hall passed inspection, and neighborhood quiet and character are ordinary zoning interests, not compelling ones.
- *Tailoring.* The ordinance is underinclusive: the City tolerates bed-and-breakfasts and granted a 40-person lock-in. Less restrictive means are obvious: an occupancy cap, required supervision, quiet hours, litter patrols, or annual fire inspection.

**The City cannot satisfy strict scrutiny; the Free Exercise claim is likely to succeed.**

**RLUIPA.** Because Ordinance 26-14 is a land-use regulation applied through individualized assessments, the church may also sue under RLUIPA, which bars a substantial burden on a religious institution's exercise unless the government shows a compelling interest pursued by the least restrictive means (42 U.S.C. § 2000cc). The same facts make that claim strong.

### 2. The Facade Program

**Rule.** A government that offers a generally available public benefit may not exclude otherwise eligible recipients because of their religious character or because they will use the benefit for religious purposes, unless the exclusion survives strict scrutiny (*Trinity Lutheran*; *Espinoza*; *Carson*).

**Application.** The program pays for exterior restoration of any registered historic building, but excludes buildings "owned by a religious organization" (a status exclusion) or "used primarily for worship" (a use exclusion). Grace Fellowship's sanctuary is on the register and otherwise qualifies; it was rejected "solely because of the exclusion." As in *Trinity Lutheran*, where a church was barred from a playground-resurfacing grant, the church must disavow its religious character to compete for a public benefit. *Carson* rejected the argument that excluding religious *use* is more acceptable than excluding religious *status*. **Strict scrutiny applies.**

**The City's justification.** The City Attorney's reason, "Taxpayers should not pay to maintain houses of worship," is an interest in separating church and state more strictly than the Establishment Clause requires. *Trinity Lutheran*, *Espinoza* and *Carson* hold that such an interest is not compelling when the Establishment Clause itself permits the aid.

The City's best argument is *Locke v. Davey*, which allowed a State to refuse scholarships for training clergy because of a historic and substantial tradition against funding ministers. The City will argue that tax support for building and maintaining churches lies at the core of historical establishments, so a comparable tradition supports this exclusion. Grace Fellowship will answer that *Locke* concerned a narrow refusal to fund an essentially religious vocation, that *Carson* and *Espinoza* declined to extend it, and that this program pays for brick and glass on a historic facade for the secular purpose of preserving the City's architecture.

**Establishment Clause.** Would funding the facade *violate* the Establishment Clause? The Clause is read by reference to history and tradition and forbids coercion; *Lemon* has been abandoned (*Kennedy*). A neutral program open to all registered buildings, chosen by objective historic criteria, coerces no one to support or practice religion. Repairing a window that depicts a dove is the closest point, because public money would maintain a religious image. But the grant restores the building's historic exterior on the same terms as every other landmark, without regard to its message. **The Establishment Clause does not require the exclusion, so it cannot supply a compelling interest.**

**Conclusion.** The exclusion likely violates the Free Exercise Clause.

### 3. The invocation practice

**Amira's standing.** Amira attended the October meeting, was personally refused the chance to give an invocation because of her faith, and was singled out at a meeting where her own application was pending. Those are personal, concrete injuries, not a generalized grievance. **She has standing.**

**Legislative prayer: history and tradition.** The Establishment Clause is interpreted by reference to historical practices and understandings (*Kennedy*). Opening legislative sessions with prayer is part of the Nation's tradition and is permissible, even if the prayers are sectarian, as long as the practice is not exploited to proselytize or disparage other faiths and is not coercive (*Marsh*; *Town of Greece*).
- The City's practice of inviting local clergy fits *Town of Greece*, where a town's prayer-givers were almost all Christian because nearly all local congregations were. The City need not search outside Fairhaven for non-Christian clergy, and Christian content alone is not a violation.
- **The practice is not unconstitutional merely because every invocation has been Christian.**

**Denominational discrimination.** The government may not prefer one religious denomination over another; such discrimination triggers strict scrutiny (*Larson v. Valente*). *Town of Greece* stressed that the town maintained a policy of nondiscrimination and would accept any minister or layperson of any faith who asked.

The Mayor refused Amira because "our invocation reflects the faith this town was founded on." That is an explicit preference for Christianity. The City may argue that the policy invites only "clergy" and Amira leads a lay prayer group. But the Mayor's letter gave a religious reason, not a clergy-status reason, and no compelling interest supports excluding a resident's faith from a public practice. **The refusal is unconstitutional denominational discrimination.**

**Coercion.** Government may not coerce anyone to support or participate in religion (*Lee*; *Kennedy*). In *Town of Greece*, the plurality found no coercion because the requests to stand came from guest ministers, adults were free to leave, and nothing suggested that decisions depended on participation. It noted that a different case would arise if officials directed the public to participate, singled out dissidents for opprobrium, or indicated that their decisions might be influenced by a person's acquiescence.

Here all three occurred. The *Mayor*, not a guest, told "everyone" to "rise and bow your heads." The Mayor then singled out those who stayed seated ("I see some folks don't want to join us") and warned, "We'll remember who our friends are." Amira's permit application was on the agenda that same night and was tabled without discussion. The City will argue that the Mayor's remark was offhand and that adults at a city meeting are not as impressionable as students. But a resident with business before the Council could reasonably understand that refusing to join the prayer might cost her the permit. **The practice, as conducted on these facts, is coercive.**

**Conclusion.** Legislative prayer is permissible in principle, but the City violated the Establishment Clause by excluding Amira because of her faith and by pressuring attendees with matters pending to participate.

- **45 or below:** discusses "separation of church and state" without the governing tests.

**Common mistakes**
1. Applying strict scrutiny automatically. Under *Smith*, a neutral, generally applicable law needs only a rational basis; explain *why* this one is not generally applicable.
2. Comparing secular activities without tying them to the government's stated interests. *Tandon* measures comparability by the risks the law addresses.
3. Treating any government money reaching a church as an Establishment Clause violation. Neutral programs open to religious and secular recipients alike are permitted, and excluding religious recipients usually violates Free Exercise.
4. Saying legislative prayer is unconstitutional because it is sectarian. *Town of Greece* permits sectarian prayer; the problems here are discrimination among faiths and coercion.
5. Applying *Lemon* as the test. It has been abandoned; you may mention it only as the former test.

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: the PRC Constitution protects "normal religious activities" under state administration of religious affairs; the US First Amendment instead limits government both from burdening religious practice (Free Exercise) and from favoring or coercing religion (Establishment) (approx.).
