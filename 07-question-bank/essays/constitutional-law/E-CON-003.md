# E-CON-003 — The Safe Micromobility Act

## Facts

During 2025, overheating lithium-ion batteries in electric bicycles ("e-bikes") caused hundreds of apartment fires across the country. In 2026 Congress enacted the Safe Micromobility Act. Its findings state that "e-bike batteries and battery cells are manufactured, sold and shipped in a national and international market," that "uncertified batteries, including packs assembled at home from salvaged cells, caused more than 400 residential fires in 2025," and that "home-assembled packs are routinely sold, traded or installed in bicycles that are later resold." The Act provides:

- **Section 2.** It is a federal crime to manufacture, assemble, sell or possess an e-bike battery pack that has not been certified by the Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) as meeting federal fire-safety standards. The section applies "whether or not the battery or its components have moved in interstate commerce, and whether or not the battery is intended for sale."
- **Section 3(a).** Each State's fire marshal "shall inspect, at least annually, every retail business in the State that sells or repairs e-bikes, and shall report every violation of Section 2 to the CPSC."
- **Section 3(b).** Each State "shall enact," by January 1, 2028, a building code requiring every apartment building with five or more units to provide a fire-rated battery-charging room. In any State that has not done so, state building-code provisions on charging rooms are preempted, and the CPSC shall enforce a federal charging-room standard directly against building owners.
- **Section 4(a).** A new Safe Streets Micromobility Grant program will distribute $2 billion a year to States to build protected bike lanes. A State may receive these grants only if it "adopts appropriate measures to reduce battery fires in multi-unit housing." The Act does not define "appropriate measures."
- **Section 4(b).** Any State that has not, by January 1, 2028, enacted a law requiring every e-bike rider under 18 to wear a helmet "shall lose all federal highway funds" thereafter.

State X has received federal highway funds every year since the 1950s. They now total about $1.1 billion a year, roughly 8% of the State's total annual budget. State X's share of the new Section 4(a) grants would be about $40 million a year. State X has no helmet law for e-bike riders. Its fire marshal's office has 60 inspectors; the Governor estimates that Section 3(a) would require inspecting about 2,300 businesses a year.

Dev Rao, a State X engineering student, builds battery packs in his garage from used laptop cells he buys online from sellers in other States. He uses them only in his own e-bike and has never sold one. In June 2026 the United States Attorney for State X announced that her office "will prosecute home builders of uncertified batteries to the full extent of the law." Dev has stopped riding his e-bike and wants to resume building packs.

Dev and State X sued the United States and the responsible federal officials in federal court, seeking declaratory and injunctive relief. Dev challenges Section 2 as applied to him. State X challenges Sections 3 and 4.

## Call of the Question

1. Does Dev have standing to challenge Section 2, and is Section 2 a valid exercise of congressional power as applied to him? Discuss.
2. Is Section 3 constitutional? Discuss.
3. Are the conditions in Section 4 constitutional? Discuss.

## Model Answer

### 1. Dev and Section 2

**Standing and ripeness.** A plaintiff must show an injury in fact that is concrete, particularized and actual or imminent, fairly traceable to the defendant, and likely to be redressed by a favorable decision (*Lujan*). A person need not violate a law and risk prosecution first: a pre-enforcement challenge is ripe when he intends conduct arguably affected with a constitutional interest, the conduct is arguably proscribed, and there is a credible threat of enforcement (*Steffel*; *SBA List*).

Dev wants to keep assembling and possessing uncertified packs, which Section 2 squarely forbids, and he claims the Constitution denies Congress the power to forbid them. The threat is not speculative: the United States Attorney publicly announced she "will prosecute home builders," which describes Dev exactly. The government may argue that Dev has not been charged or individually threatened. However, a targeted public announcement of enforcement against a defined group is a credible threat, and Dev has already stopped riding and building, a concrete present harm caused by the Act and its announced enforcement. The issue (whether Congress has power to reach his garage) is a legal question fit for decision now. A declaratory judgment and injunction against enforcement would redress the injury. **Dev has standing, and his as-applied claim is ripe.**

**Commerce power.** Congress may regulate (1) the channels of interstate commerce, (2) its instrumentalities and persons or things in interstate commerce, and (3) activities that substantially affect interstate commerce (*Lopez*). Intrastate economic activity may be regulated if, in the aggregate, it substantially affects interstate commerce, and the Court asks only whether Congress had a rational basis for that conclusion (*Wickard*; *Raich*).

Categories 1 and 2 fit poorly. Dev's packs never cross state lines, and the statute expressly reaches batteries that have not moved in commerce. The government's best argument is category 3.

*Economic activity.* *Raich* defined economics as the production, distribution and consumption of commodities, and upheld federal regulation of marijuana grown at home for the grower's own use. Dev *produces* a commodity, a battery pack, for which there is a national and international market, as Congress found. Building a pack for oneself substitutes for buying one, just as Filburn's home-grown wheat substituted for purchased wheat (*Wickard*). Congress also found that home-assembled packs are "routinely sold, traded or installed in bicycles that are later resold," so home production leaks into the interstate market and undermines a certification regime aimed at that market.

*Aggregation.* Dev's own packs are trivial, but the question is the class of activity in the aggregate. Aggregated across the country, home assembly of uncertified packs plainly could affect the interstate battery market and the 400 fires Congress identified. Under the deferential rational-basis inquiry, Congress could rationally conclude that exempting home builders would leave a gap that is hard to police, because an uncertified pack in a resold bike looks the same whether it came from a garage or a factory.

**Lopez and Morrison counter-arguments.** Dev will argue that possession of a battery is like possession of a gun near a school, a noneconomic act that Congress may not regulate on attenuated reasoning (*Lopez*), and that the real target is home fire safety, a traditional state police-power concern like the violence in *Morrison*. He will stress that Section 2 has no jurisdictional element; it disclaims one. These are serious points: the statute regulates possession in a person's own garage.

However, *Lopez* involved a stand-alone criminal statute unconnected to any market regulation, while Section 2 is part of a comprehensive scheme regulating the manufacture and sale of a commercial product. *Raich* held that where Congress regulates a class of economic activity, courts do not carve out individual "trivial" instances, and that Congress may reach local activity that is an essential part of a larger regulation of economic activity. Under the Necessary and Proper Clause, reaching home-built packs is a rational means of making the certification rule for the interstate market effective (*McCulloch*; *Comstock*), and it does not commandeer the States or otherwise offend state sovereignty (contrast *NFIB*). Unlike the individual mandate in *NFIB*, Section 2 does not compel anyone to *enter* commerce; it regulates people who choose to make batteries.

**Conclusion.** Dev's argument is respectable but likely fails: under *Raich*, Section 2 is a valid exercise of the commerce power, supported by the Necessary and Proper Clause, as applied to him.

### 2. Section 3

**State X's standing.** A State may sue to protect its own sovereign and proprietary interests (*Massachusetts v. EPA*). Section 3(a) orders State X's fire marshal to perform about 2,300 inspections a year with a 60-person staff, and Section 3(b) orders its legislature to enact a code. Those are concrete, imminent injuries to State X's sovereignty and treasury, caused by the Act and redressable by a declaration and injunction. **State X has standing.**

**Section 3(a): inspections and reports.** Congress may not compel state legislatures to enact or administer a federal program, and may not conscript state executive officers to enforce federal law (*New York*; *Printz*). It may instead regulate private parties directly, pass generally applicable laws that bind States and private actors alike (*Garcia*), or offer funds on conditions.

Section 3(a) orders each State's fire marshal to inspect "every retail business" that sells or repairs e-bikes and to report "every violation of Section 2" to a federal agency. That is the enforcement of a federal criminal statute by state officers, the very thing *Printz* forbade when Congress ordered sheriffs to run background checks. The burden is heavy: 2,300 inspections a year for a 60-person office, diverting State X's officers from its own priorities and blurring political accountability for an unpopular federal program.

The United States will make three arguments. First, *Garcia* lets Congress regulate States through generally applicable laws. But Section 3(a) applies only to state fire marshals; it does not regulate private parties at all. Second, in *Reno v. Condon* the Court upheld restrictions on the sale of drivers' personal information. That law regulated States as owners of databases, in the same way it regulated private resellers, and required no enforcement of federal law against third parties. Third, the government may describe the duty as mere "reporting," which *Printz* did not decide. However, the report is only the last step: the statute first requires the State to *conduct inspections* to discover federal crimes. That is investigation and enforcement, not the passing along of information the State already holds. **Section 3(a) unconstitutionally commandeers State X's executive officers.** Congress could achieve the same goal with federal inspectors, or by funding state inspections as a voluntary condition of a grant.

**Section 3(b): charging-room code.** Read alone, the words "Each State shall enact" are a direct command to state legislatures, which *New York* and *Murphy* forbid: Congress cannot order a State to legislate. State X will argue the provision is invalid on its face.

The United States will answer that the statute's only consequence for a State that does not legislate is that its charging-room rules are preempted and the CPSC regulates building owners directly. That is the "conditional preemption" *New York* approved: Congress may offer States the choice of regulating according to federal standards or having state law preempted by federal regulation of private parties. Preemption is valid because the federal standard regulates private actors, not the States (*Murphy*), and regulating commercial apartment buildings that house e-bike charging is within the commerce power. State X keeps a genuine choice: legislate, or let federal officers do the job.

Because courts construe statutes to avoid constitutional problems and sever invalid parts where possible, a court would likely read "shall enact" as the first option of a permitted choice, or sever the command and keep the preemption mechanism. **The command to legislate cannot be enforced against State X, but the conditional-preemption scheme in Section 3(b) is valid.**

### 3. Section 4

**Rule.** Congress may spend for the general welfare and may attach conditions to federal funds if the conditions (1) serve the general welfare, (2) are stated unambiguously, so that States accept knowingly, (3) are related to the federal interest in the program, (4) do not require the State to violate the Constitution, and (5) are not so coercive that pressure turns into compulsion (*South Dakota v. Dole*; *NFIB v. Sebelius*). Clear notice is judged from the perspective of a state official deciding whether to accept the money (*Pennhurst*; *Arlington Central*). Because a State may decline the funds, a valid condition does not commandeer.

**Section 4(a): new bike-lane grants.**
- *General welfare.* Building bike lanes and reducing housing fires plainly serve the general welfare; courts defer to Congress here.
- *Unambiguous condition.* This is the weakest point. The condition requires "appropriate measures to reduce battery fires in multi-unit housing" and the Act does not define them. A State official cannot tell whether smoke detectors suffice, whether charging rooms are required, or whether a public-education campaign counts. The United States may argue that Section 3(b)'s charging-room standard tells States what Congress had in mind. But a condition that leaves the State to guess what it is accepting does not give the clear notice *Pennhurst* requires. **The condition is likely unenforceable as written**: State X can take the grants without being bound by a requirement it could not have understood.
- *Relatedness.* State X will argue that bike lanes (road infrastructure) have little to do with fire safety inside apartment buildings. The United States will respond that the program's purpose is safe micromobility, and that fires from charging e-bikes at home are one of its main hazards, so a condition limiting those fires relates to the federal interest in promoting e-bike use. *Dole* requires only a reasonable relationship, so this element is probably met, although it is closer than in *Dole*.
- *Independent bar and coercion.* Reducing fire risk violates no constitutional right. The grants are *new* money worth about $40 million a year, and declining them leaves State X where it was. That is encouragement, not compulsion.
- **Conclusion:** Section 4(a) passes every factor except clear notice; the condition is likely unenforceable because it is ambiguous.

**Section 4(b): helmets and all highway funds.**
- *General welfare and clarity.* Protecting minors from head injuries serves the general welfare, and "a law requiring every e-bike rider under 18 to wear a helmet" is perfectly clear.
- *Relatedness.* Highway funds build and maintain roads on which e-bikes are ridden; head injuries to young riders on those roads are a highway-safety problem. As in *Dole*, where the drinking age was related to highway safety, this condition is related to the federal interest in safe highways.
- *Independent bar.* A helmet law violates no one's constitutional rights, so Congress may induce it even if it could not command States to enact it.
- *Coercion.* This is the decisive issue. In *Dole*, withholding 5% of a State's highway funds, under 1% of its budget, was "relatively mild encouragement." In *NFIB*, threatening a State's entire existing Medicaid funding, over 10% of its budget, to force acceptance of a new program was "a gun to the head."

The United States will argue that Section 4(b) is closer to *Dole*. It conditions the highway program itself on a highway-safety rule and does not use an old program to force acceptance of a new one, which *NFIB* emphasized. State X will respond that the threat is the *loss of all* highway funds, which it has received for 70 years and which equal about 8% of its budget. That is many times the *Dole* penalty and close to the magnitude found coercive in *NFIB*. Like the drinking age in *Dole*, the helmet rule governs state law outside the funded program, so the money is being used as leverage over independent state policy. A State realistically cannot give up its entire road-building budget, so its "choice" is illusory.

Courts have given little guidance between 5% of one program and over 10% of a budget, so this is close. Because the penalty is total, falls on a long-established program on which the State depends, and is used to dictate separate state legislation, **Section 4(b) is likely unconstitutionally coercive**. Congress could cure it by withholding a modest share of highway funds, as in *Dole*.

**Overall.** Section 2 is likely valid as applied to Dev. Section 3(a) is invalid commandeering. Section 3(b) is valid as conditional preemption, but its command to legislate cannot be enforced. Section 4(a)'s condition is likely unenforceable for ambiguity, and Section 4(b) is likely coercive.

**Common mistakes**
1. Calling every federal law touching state matters a Tenth Amendment violation. Only commandeering (or action outside an enumerated power) fails; conditional preemption and conditional spending are valid.
2. Ignoring the word "unambiguously" in *Dole*. An undefined condition such as "appropriate measures" is a separate ground for attack.
3. Treating *Lopez* as controlling every "possession" statute. *Raich* governs local activity that is part of a comprehensive regulation of an economic market.
4. Treating *Reno v. Condon* as permitting any federal duty on state officers. It upheld a law regulating States as data owners, alongside private parties.
5. Saying the spending power is limited to enumerated subjects. It is not (*Butler*); the limits are the *Dole*/*NFIB* factors.

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: in the PRC, central authorities may direct local governments to implement national law (a unitary system); the US anti-commandeering rule forbids Congress from ordering state legislatures or officers to carry out federal programs (approx.).

**Accuracy notes.** Rules follow R-CON-008, -009, -014, -015, -020 to -022, -024, -045, -046 and -051. The *Printz* majority did not decide whether statutes requiring States only to provide information to the federal government are valid (Justice O'Connor's concurrence referred to "purely ministerial reporting requirements"); the answer treats Section 3(a) as commandeering because it requires inspections, not just reports of information the State already holds. The coercion conclusion on § 4(b) is a judgment call: the Supreme Court has decided only the *Dole* (mild) and *NFIB* (coercive) endpoints, and an answer reaching the opposite result with reasons should receive full credit. *Hodel v. Virginia Surface Mining & Reclamation Ass'n*, 452 U.S. 264 (1981), is the classic conditional-preemption case relied on in *New York*; it is not named in the rulebook and is not needed for full credit.
