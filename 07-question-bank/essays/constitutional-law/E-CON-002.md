# E-CON-002 — The Home Care Quality Act

## Facts

In 2026, State X enacted the Home Care Quality Act. Its findings state that "agencies headquartered in State X can be inspected more easily and are more accountable to State X residents" and that "citizen aides are more likely to remain in State X and serve our elderly residents for the long term." The Act provides:

- **Section 101:** No agency may provide in-home care services in State X without a license from the State Department of Health. Licenses "shall be issued and renewed only for agencies whose principal office is located in State X."
- **Section 102:** No person may be certified as a home-care aide unless the person is a United States citizen. Only certified aides may provide in-home care.
- **Section 103:** The Director of the Department "may revoke a license upon a finding that the agency has failed to comply with this Act." Revocation "is effective immediately upon written notice, and no hearing shall be required."

Home-care aides help elderly and disabled clients bathe, dress, cook and take their medications. They are employed by private agencies. About 60% of State X home-care clients pay privately or through private insurance; the rest are covered by the State's Medicaid program, under which the State pays agencies for services to eligible residents.

**CarePoint, Inc.** is incorporated in Delaware and has its principal office in neighboring State Y. Since 2019 it has served about 900 State X clients through a branch office in State X's capital, where it keeps all client and personnel records. In June 2026 the Department refused to renew CarePoint's State X license solely because its principal office is in State Y. The Department's inspectors have never had difficulty inspecting the branch office.

**Maria Lopez**, a citizen of Mexico, has been a lawful permanent resident of the United States for nine years and has lived in State X the entire time. She completed State X's approved 120-hour aide training course with top marks. The Department denied her certification under Section 102.

**Hank Ross** has owned Ross Family Home Care, a State X agency, since 2015. On May 1, 2026, the Director sent Hank a letter revoking his license, effective immediately, "based on an anonymous complaint that Ross Family Home Care has failed to perform required criminal background checks on its aides." Hank has records showing that every aide passed a background check. He asked for a chance to show the records, but the Department replied that the Act requires no hearing. Hank's 45 clients were transferred to other agencies, his 30 aides lost their jobs, and his business has closed.

CarePoint, Maria and Hank have each sued the appropriate State X officials in federal court.

## Call of the Question

1. What constitutional challenges may CarePoint raise to Section 101, and are they likely to succeed? Discuss.
2. Is Section 102 constitutional as applied to Maria? Discuss.
3. Did the revocation of Hank's license violate his rights under the United States Constitution? Discuss.

## Model Answer

**Threshold.** The Act is a state statute enforced by state officials, so there is state action. Suing the responsible officials for prospective relief avoids the Eleventh Amendment bar on suing the State itself (*Ex parte Young*).

### 1. CarePoint's challenges to Section 101

**Standing.** CarePoint lost its license and its 900 State X clients because of Section 101. The injury is concrete, caused by the Act's principal-office rule, and an injunction requiring the Department to renew the license would redress it. **CarePoint has standing.**

**Dormant Commerce Clause: discrimination.** The Commerce Clause implicitly forbids States from discriminating against interstate commerce. A state law that discriminates against out-of-state economic interests on its face, in purpose or in effect, to benefit in-state interests, is virtually per se invalid; it survives only if the State shows that it serves a legitimate local purpose that cannot be adequately served by reasonable nondiscriminatory alternatives (*City of Philadelphia v. New Jersey*; *Maine v. Taylor*).

Section 101 allows licenses "only for agencies whose principal office is located in State X." On its face it excludes every agency headquartered elsewhere, however well it serves State X clients, and reserves the State X market to in-state firms. **Section 101 discriminates on its face.**

**Justification and alternatives.** The State asserts two purposes: easier inspection and greater accountability to State X residents.

- *Legitimate purpose.* Protecting vulnerable elderly and disabled clients through effective inspection is a legitimate, non-protectionist goal.
- *Nondiscriminatory alternatives.* The State cannot show that its goal requires excluding out-of-state firms. It could require every agency to keep a State X branch office holding all client and personnel records open to inspection, designate a local agent for service of process, post a bond, or reimburse travel costs for out-of-state inspections. CarePoint already keeps all its records at its State X branch, and inspectors have "never had difficulty inspecting" it. The "accountability" rationale, standing alone, amounts to a preference for local businesses, which is protectionism, not a legitimate local purpose.

Unlike *Maine v. Taylor*, where no alternative test for baitfish parasites existed, obvious alternatives exist here. **Section 101 violates the dormant Commerce Clause** unless an exception applies.

**Market-participant exception.** When a State acts as a market participant, buying or selling with its own funds, rather than as a regulator, it may favor in-state firms (*Reeves*; *White*). The State will argue that it pays for about 40% of home care through Medicaid and may choose its own contractors. But Section 101 is a licensing law: it bars out-of-state agencies from serving *anyone* in State X, including the 60% of clients who pay privately. Regulating the whole market goes beyond the State's own purchases, and the exception does not cover regulation of transactions in which the State is not a party (*South-Central Timber v. Wunnicke* (plurality on this point); *New Energy*). At most, the State might steer its own Medicaid contracts, which Section 101 does not purport to do. **The exception does not apply.**

**Article IV Privileges and Immunities.** The Privileges and Immunities Clause protects the right of individual citizens of other States to pursue a livelihood, but it does not protect corporations, which are not "citizens" for this purpose (*Paul v. Virginia*). **CarePoint cannot invoke Article IV**, although an individual out-of-state owner could.

**Equal protection.** A corporation is a "person" under the Equal Protection Clause. A classification by the location of a business's headquarters is economic and gets rational-basis review: CarePoint must show the law is not rationally related to any legitimate interest (*Williamson*; *Beach Communications*). The State will say easier inspection is a conceivable legitimate goal and the law is rationally related to it, even if imperfect. CarePoint will respond that the Supreme Court has held that promoting domestic business by discriminating against nonresident competitors is not itself a legitimate state purpose under the Equal Protection Clause (*Metropolitan Life Ins. Co. v. Ward*), and that the inspection rationale is belied by the record. Because rational basis is so deferential, and inspection convenience is at least a conceivable interest, **the equal-protection challenge is weaker**; CarePoint's strongest claim is the dormant Commerce Clause.

### 2. Section 102 as applied to Maria

**Level of scrutiny.** State laws that classify by alienage are inherently suspect and get strict scrutiny: the State must prove that the law is necessary to achieve a compelling interest (*Graham v. Richardson*). Federal alienage rules get deference because of Congress's immigration power, but Section 102 is a state law. It classifies on its face: only U.S. citizens may be certified, so Maria, a lawful permanent resident, is excluded solely because she is not a citizen.

**The political-function exception.** States may exclude non-citizens from positions intimately related to the process of democratic self-government, such as police officers (*Foley*), public-school teachers (*Ambach*) and probation officers (*Cabell*); those exclusions get only rational-basis review. The exception is narrow and applies to positions that involve broad discretion in executing public policy or exercising the State's sovereign power.

The State may argue that aides serve a vulnerable population under state certification and that care for the elderly is a public responsibility. But a home-care aide helps private clients bathe, dress and take medications while employed by a *private* agency. The job carries no policymaking authority, no coercive state power and no role in shaping citizens' civic values. State certification of a private occupation does not turn it into a governmental function: the Court has applied strict scrutiny to citizenship requirements for lawyers (*In re Griffiths*) and notaries public (*Bernal v. Fainter*). **The exception does not apply; strict scrutiny governs.**

**Applying strict scrutiny.**

- *Interest.* The State's finding is that citizen aides "are more likely to remain in State X." Ensuring continuity of care for elderly residents is a legitimate interest, but it is hard to call it compelling, and nothing shows that lawful permanent residents leave more often than citizens.
- *Narrow tailoring.* The rule is badly overinclusive: Maria has lived in State X for nine years. It is also underinclusive: citizens may move away at any time. If quality of care is the concern, the 120-hour training course, competency tests and background checks, which Maria passed, are direct and nondiscriminatory means.

**Section 102 fails strict scrutiny and violates the Equal Protection Clause as applied to Maria.**

### 3. Hank's license revocation

**Protected property interest.** Procedural due process applies when the State deprives a person of property, meaning a legitimate claim of entitlement created by an independent source such as state law (*Board of Regents v. Roth*). Section 103 allows revocation only "upon a finding that the agency has failed to comply with this Act." That for-cause standard gives Hank a legitimate claim of entitlement to keep his license unless he violates the Act, much like the driver's license in *Bell v. Burson*. Revoking it was an intentional deprivation by the State. **Hank has a protected property interest.**

**What process is due.** The process required is set by balancing (1) the private interest affected, (2) the risk of erroneous deprivation under the procedures used and the probable value of additional safeguards, and (3) the government's interest, including the burden of added procedures (*Mathews v. Eldridge*). At a minimum, due process requires notice and a meaningful opportunity to be heard.

- *Private interest.* Very strong. The revocation destroyed an eleven-year-old business and Hank's livelihood, and cost 30 aides their jobs.
- *Risk of error and value of safeguards.* The revocation rested on a single *anonymous* complaint, and Hank was never allowed to respond. He has records showing every aide passed a background check, so even a brief opportunity to present them would very likely have prevented the error.
- *Government interest.* The State has a strong interest in protecting vulnerable clients from unscreened aides, and it may argue that the danger justified acting immediately. The cost of a hearing is modest compared with the harm of a wrongful shutdown.

**Pre- or post-deprivation hearing.** Where the government must act quickly to protect public health or safety, it may take summary action and provide a hearing afterward, but the post-deprivation hearing must be prompt (*Barry v. Barchi*). The State has a plausible argument that an allegation about missing background checks for aides working alone with vulnerable people justified interim action, although an anonymous complaint is thin support for an emergency, and a short pre-revocation chance to produce records would have cost little (compare *Loudermill*). Even if immediate action was justified, Section 103 provides **no hearing at all**, before or after, and the Department refused Hank's request. **The revocation violated procedural due process.**

**Substantive due process and remedy.** Hank might also argue that revoking a license on an unverified anonymous tip is arbitrary. Economic regulation gets only rational-basis review, and licensing home-care agencies and enforcing background checks is plainly rational, so a substantive due-process claim is weak; the defect here is procedural. Hank may obtain an injunction reinstating his license until the State provides a constitutionally adequate hearing, and he may seek damages under § 1983 from the officials in their personal capacities, subject to qualified immunity (the Eleventh Amendment bars damages from the State itself).

**Common mistakes**
1. Letting a corporation sue under Article IV P&I. Only individual citizens are protected.
2. Treating the State's Medicaid spending as making the whole licensing law "market participation." The exception covers the State's own transactions, not regulation of private ones.
3. Applying rational basis to a state citizenship requirement for an ordinary occupation. Only positions in the self-government function qualify for deference.
4. Saying Hank had no property interest because a license is a "privilege." A for-cause license is an entitlement.
5. Stopping at "an emergency allows summary action." Summary action still requires a prompt post-deprivation hearing.
