# E-CON-006 — The Pavilion, the nursery and the dummy carry

## Facts

**The Pavilion.** The State X Port Authority, a state agency, owns Harborview Pavilion, a building on public pier land built with state bonds. Since 1998 the Authority has leased the Pavilion's top floor to the Mariners Club, a private nonprofit social club founded in 1921. The Club's bylaws limit membership to men. Under the lease, the Club pays rent equal to 12% of its gross food and beverage revenue; the Authority maintains the building, provides security, and gives Club members free parking in its garage. The Club's dining room is the only restaurant in the Pavilion and serves only members and their guests. The Authority's seven-member board holds its monthly public meetings in the Club's private dining room without charge, and four of the seven commissioners are Club members. The Authority's website calls the Club "our Pavilion partner." The lease says nothing about membership policies.

Lena Park, a maritime lawyer, applied for membership in March 2026. Most of the shipping executives she hopes to represent are members and hold business lunches at the Club. The Club rejected her application, writing that "membership has been limited to men since our founding, and our members value that tradition."

**The nursery.** Under the State X Family Leave Act, "a state employee who becomes a mother, by birth or by adoption, is entitled to 16 weeks of paid parental leave," and "a state employee who becomes a father, by birth or by adoption, is entitled to 4 weeks of paid parental leave." The Act's findings state that "mothers need time to recover from childbirth and are, in most families, the primary caregivers of infants." Separately, all state employees may use paid sick leave for any medical condition, including recovery from childbirth.

Marcus Webb, a state highway engineer, and his wife adopted a newborn in August 2026. His wife works full time for a private employer that offers no paid leave. Marcus asked for 16 weeks of parental leave and received 4.

**The dummy carry.** In 2024, the State X Highway Patrol replaced its entrance test, which required applicants to drag a 120-pound dummy 100 feet, with a new test requiring them to carry a 165-pound dummy 100 feet in 30 seconds. About 60% of women passed the old test. Under the new test, 81% of male applicants and 23% of female applicants pass. The Patrol did not conduct a validation study. It explains that 165 pounds is roughly the weight of an average adult and that troopers may have to carry injured motorists from wrecks. Patrol records show that in 2025 troopers carried a person in 3 of about 9,000 crash responses, each time with a partner. Two weeks before the change, the Patrol's training chief emailed the commander: "The new test will cut down on recruits who can't pull their weight. We've had too many of those, mostly the women." Dana Cruz, a paramedic who had passed the old test in a practice session, failed the new test by four seconds and was rejected.

Lena, Marcus and Dana have each sued the appropriate parties in federal court under 42 U.S.C. § 1983.

## Call of the Question

1. Can Lena establish that the Mariners Club's exclusion of women violates the Equal Protection Clause? Discuss.
2. Is the Family Leave Act constitutional as applied to Marcus? Discuss.
3. Is Dana likely to succeed on an equal protection challenge to the Patrol's physical test? Discuss.

## Model Answer

**Equal protection framework.** The Equal Protection Clause requires that similarly situated persons be treated alike. Sex classifications get intermediate scrutiny: the government must show an "exceedingly persuasive justification," meaning the classification serves important governmental objectives and is substantially related to them, and it may not rest on overbroad generalizations about the different talents, capacities or preferences of men and women (*Craig v. Boren*; *United States v. Virginia*).

### 1. Lena v. the Mariners Club

**Standing.** The Club rejected Lena's application solely because she is a woman, denying her access to the venue where her prospective clients do business. That injury is concrete and personal, caused by the rejection, and an order requiring the Club to consider her application would redress it.

**State action: general rule.** The Fourteenth Amendment restrains only government. A private entity's conduct is state action only if it performs a function traditionally and exclusively reserved to the State, or if the State is sufficiently entangled with it (*Civil Rights Cases*; *Jackson v. Metropolitan Edison*). Running a social club and restaurant is not a traditional, exclusive public function (contrast running elections or a company town), so Lena must rely on entanglement.

**Symbiotic relationship and joint participation.** In *Burton v. Wilmington Parking Authority*, a private restaurant leasing space in a publicly owned parking garage was a state actor when it refused service to Black customers. The State owned and maintained the building, the restaurant's rent helped finance the public facility, and the State profited from the discrimination; it had so far insinuated itself into a position of interdependence that it was a joint participant. By contrast, a state liquor license and pervasive regulation do not make a private lodge a state actor (*Moose Lodge v. Irvis*), and state approval of or acquiescence in private choices is not enough (*Blum*).

Lena's case is close to *Burton*, and in some respects stronger:
- *Public property and services.* The Pavilion sits on public land, was built with state bonds, and is maintained, secured and supplied with free parking by the Authority.
- *Financial interdependence.* Rent is 12% of the Club's food and beverage revenue, so the Authority profits directly from the Club's business, including the exclusive, men-only character that members "value."
- *Public identification.* The Authority calls the Club "our Pavilion partner," and its board holds its official public meetings, without charge, in the Club's private dining room.
- *Overlap of personnel.* Four of seven commissioners are Club members. *Brentwood Academy* found state action where public officials were pervasively entwined in a nominally private association's management.

The Club will argue that later cases have read *Burton* narrowly; that the Authority is merely a landlord that neither required nor approved the men-only rule (the lease is silent); that, as in *Moose Lodge*, the State's involvement does not touch the discriminatory policy itself; and that commissioners who join the Club as individuals do not run it in their official capacity, unlike *Brentwood*, where state officials sat on the association's governing bodies. These points weaken the entwinement theory.

But the *Burton* factors do not depend on the lease mentioning membership: the State has placed a discriminatory enterprise in a public building, subsidized it with public services, shares in its revenue and publicly presents it as a partner while using it for official business. **A court would likely find state action under *Burton*.** If it does not, Lena's constitutional claim fails, although she may have state-law remedies.

**Intermediate scrutiny.** Treated as a state actor, the Club classifies by sex on its face. Its only justification is tradition: membership "has been limited to men since our founding." Tradition is not an important governmental objective, and the preference of members for male-only company is the kind of generalization *Virginia* forbids. No justification connects excluding women to any legitimate purpose of a public building. **The exclusion fails intermediate scrutiny; if state action exists, it violates the Equal Protection Clause.**

### 2. Marcus and the Family Leave Act

**Classification and tier.** The Act gives 16 weeks to employees who become "mothers" and 4 weeks to those who become "fathers," by birth or adoption. It classifies by sex on its face, so intermediate scrutiny applies, and the State bears the burden. Marcus, denied 12 weeks of paid leave because he is a man, has standing.

**Childbirth recovery.** The State's first interest, recovery from childbirth, is important, and real biological differences may justify different treatment (*Nguyen v. INS*; *Michael M.*). A rule granting leave to "employees recovering from childbirth" would not even be a sex classification, because pregnancy-based rules are not treated as sex classifications (*Geduldig*).

But this Act does not track childbirth. It gives *adoptive* mothers, who do not give birth, the same 16 weeks, and gives *birth* fathers only 4. The classification is therefore not substantially related to physical recovery. The fit is also poor because every employee may already use paid sick leave for recovery from childbirth, so the 16 weeks are labeled, and function as, parental leave, not medical leave.

**Caregiving.** The State's second rationale, that mothers "are, in most families, the primary caregivers of infants," is exactly the kind of overbroad generalization *Virginia* forbids. Even if statistically true, the Act turns the generalization into a rule that keeps fathers like Marcus, whose wife works full time without paid leave, from caring for an infant, and reinforces the expectation that caregiving is women's work. *Sessions v. Morales-Santana* struck down a citizenship rule built on the assumption that unwed mothers, not fathers, would care for their children. The State may argue that the interest in infant bonding is important. It is, but it applies equally to fathers and adoptive parents and supports equal leave, not a sex-based line.

**Conclusion.** The State cannot show an exceedingly persuasive justification. **The Act violates equal protection as applied to Marcus.**

**Remedy.** When a statute grants a benefit on a sex-based line, a court may either extend the benefit to the excluded class or withdraw it from the favored class, guided by what the legislature would have wanted (*Morales-Santana*, which leveled down). Given the Act's purpose of supporting new parents, extension to fathers (16 weeks for all new parents, with separate medical leave for birth recovery) is the more likely remedy, but the legislature may choose to equalize at a different level.

### 3. Dana and the physical test

**Neutral rule; purpose required.** The test applies the same standard to every applicant. A facially neutral law is a sex classification only if it was adopted with a discriminatory purpose; disparate impact alone triggers only rational-basis review (*Washington v. Davis*). Purpose means the decisionmaker acted "because of," not merely "in spite of," the effect on women (*Feeney*, upholding a veterans' preference that overwhelmingly favored men). Dana, rejected under the test, has standing; the impact (81% of men vs. 23% of women) is stark, but she must prove purpose.

**Arlington Heights factors.** Courts infer purpose from the impact, historical background, the sequence of events, departures from normal procedures, and contemporaneous statements (*Arlington Heights*).
- *Impact.* Women pass at less than a third of the men's rate. Impact alone is not enough, but it is a starting point.
- *Sequence and background.* The Patrol replaced a test that 60% of women passed with one that only 23% pass, after a period in which the training chief complained about recruits who "can't pull their weight... mostly the women."
- *Departures and fit.* The Patrol adopted a much harder screening test without a validation study (a procedural departure only if validation was its normal practice, which the facts do not show), and its own records show that carrying a person arises in 3 of 9,000 crash responses, always with a partner.
- *Statements.* The chief's email links the test's goal ("cut down on recruits who can't pull their weight") to a group he identified as "mostly the women."

The Patrol will argue that the email shows a concern with physical ability, not with sex, and that the chief merely noted, "in spite of" rather than "because of," that more women than men had struggled, as in *Feeney*. It will add that 165 pounds approximates an average adult, a sex-neutral rescue rationale. Dana will respond that the timing, the unexplained jump in difficulty, the absence of validation and the chief's focus on women together show that excluding women was at least a motivating purpose. If Dana proves that, the Patrol may still avoid liability by showing that it would have adopted the same test anyway for neutral reasons (*Arlington Heights*), a hard showing given the weak job-relatedness evidence.

This is a close factual question. Because the chief, who drove the change, tied it to women in a contemporaneous email and the Patrol departed from its prior test without validation, **a court could reasonably find discriminatory purpose**; a court reading the email as a comment on ability could find none.

**Resulting tier.**
- *If no purpose is found,* rational basis applies: Dana must show the test is not rationally related to any legitimate interest. Ensuring that troopers can carry injured people is legitimate, and a strength test is rationally related to it even if imperfect. **The test would be upheld.**
- *If purpose is found,* the test is treated as a sex classification and the Patrol must show an exceedingly persuasive justification. The interest in rescue capability is important, but the test is not substantially related to it: carrying a person alone over 100 feet in 30 seconds bears little relation to a task that arose in 3 of 9,000 responses and was always done with a partner, and the Patrol never validated the standard. **The test would fail.**

**Conclusion.** Dana's success depends on proving purpose. On these facts she has a credible but uncertain case. (Any disparate-impact claim under Title VII is beyond this call.)

- **45 or below:** applies strict scrutiny to sex, or puts the burden on the challenger under intermediate scrutiny.

**Common mistakes**
1. Skipping state action because the Port Authority is involved. The Club is the defendant whose conduct must be attributed to the State.
2. Treating disparate impact as proof of a sex classification. *Davis* and *Feeney* require purpose.
3. Putting the burden on the challenger under intermediate scrutiny. The government must justify a sex classification.
4. Accepting "real differences" without testing the fit. The Leave Act gives adoptive mothers the same leave as birth mothers, so childbirth cannot explain it.
5. Applying strict scrutiny to sex because California's Constitution does. The call raises the federal Equal Protection Clause; California's stricter rule (Cal. Const. art. I, § 7) is not in issue.
