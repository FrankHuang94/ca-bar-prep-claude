# E-EVD-001 — People v. Pratt (California Evidence Code, criminal)

## Facts

On the night of March 3, 2026, a man wearing a gray hooded sweatshirt and a surgical mask entered Sunrise Pharmacy in Fresno, held a box cutter toward pharmacist Lena Ruiz, and demanded cash and oxycodone. He fled with $900 and three bottles of pills. A week later Owen Pratt was arrested and charged in Fresno County Superior Court with robbery. Owen pleaded not guilty; his defense at trial was mistaken identity. Over defense objections, the court admitted the following evidence.

**Item 1.** Lena testified that about 30 seconds after the robber ran out the door, her coworker Marcus Bell, who had been crouched behind the back counter during the robbery, jumped up, shaking, and shouted, "That's the guy from Iron Gym — the one with the snake tattoo on his neck!" Owen has a snake tattoo on the side of his neck and is a member of Iron Gym. Marcus moved out of state in May 2026, and the prosecution's diligent efforts to locate him failed.

**Item 2.** Kyle Dunn, a friend of Owen's, was arrested on March 5 with two of the stolen bottles. At the police station, after Miranda warnings, Detective Soto recorded an interview in which Kyle said: "Owen did the robbery. I just drove the car and waited around the corner. He gave me two bottles for driving." Called by the prosecution at trial, Kyle invoked his privilege against self-incrimination, and the court upheld the claim. The court then allowed Detective Soto to play the recording of Kyle's interview for the jury.

**Item 3.** The prosecution called Arturo Vega, a clerk at Valley Drug in nearby Clovis. Arturo testified that in October 2024 a man in a hooded sweatshirt and a mask demanded oxycodone at Valley Drug while holding a box cutter, and that Arturo recognized the man's voice as Owen's because Owen had been a regular customer for two years. No charges were ever filed for the Valley Drug incident. The prosecutor offered the evidence "to prove identity and intent."

**Item 4.** Owen testified that he was home alone watching television on the night of March 3. On cross-examination, the prosecutor (a) introduced a certified record of Owen's 2020 felony conviction for possession of methamphetamine for sale, and (b) asked Owen, "Isn't it true that in 2023 you shoplifted two jackets from a Fresno department store?" Owen answered, "Yes, but I paid for them later."

**Item 5.** Tess Morgan, Owen's girlfriend, testified that at 9:47 p.m. on March 3, about an hour after the robbery, she received a text message from the number saved in her phone as "Owen," which she knew to be Owen's cell number, reading: "Got what I needed tonight. Don't tell anyone I was out." Tess testified that she took a screenshot of the message the next morning and later deleted the whole message thread after an argument with Owen. Owen's phone was never recovered. The screenshot was admitted. Defense counsel argued that Owen's roommate often borrowed Owen's phone.

Assume that all appropriate objections were timely made.

## Call of the Question

Did the court err in admitting the evidence in each of items 1 through 5? Discuss. Answer according to California law.

## Model Answer

### Preliminary: Proposition 8 (Truth-in-Evidence)

Because this is a California criminal prosecution, the Truth-in-Evidence provision of the California Constitution (art. I, § 28(f)(2)) applies: relevant evidence may not be excluded except under the hearsay rule, privileges, Evidence Code §§ 352, 782 and 1103, and statutes enacted after 1982 by a two-thirds vote of the Legislature. Section 1101 survives because it was re-enacted by a two-thirds vote (*People v. Ewoldt*). Federal constitutional limits, including the Confrontation Clause, apply regardless. Each item is analyzed below with these limits in mind.

### Item 1 — Marcus's shout ("That's the guy from Iron Gym…")

**Relevance.** Evidence is relevant if it has any tendency in reason to prove or disprove a disputed fact of consequence (§ 210). Identity is the only real dispute, and Marcus's statement links the robber to a specific gym and a snake neck tattoo, both of which match Owen. It is highly relevant.

**Personal knowledge.** A declarant's statement offered under a hearsay exception must rest on the declarant's own perception (§ 702; § 1240(a) requires an event "perceived by the declarant"). Owen will argue that Marcus was crouched behind the back counter and could not have seen a masked, hooded robber's neck. The prosecution will respond that Marcus "jumped up" as the robber fled and that the specificity of his statement (the gym, the tattoo's location) itself shows he saw the man. Because only evidence sufficient to support a finding of perception is required, the court could properly find this foundation met, leaving the weight of the observation to the jury.

**Hearsay.** Hearsay is an out-of-court statement offered to prove the truth of the matter stated, and it is inadmissible unless an exception applies (§ 1200). Marcus's statement was made in the pharmacy, not at trial, and is offered to prove that the robber was the snake-tattooed man from Iron Gym. It is hearsay, and Prop 8 does not override the hearsay rule.

**Spontaneous statement (§ 1240).** A statement is admissible if it narrates, describes or explains an act or event perceived by the declarant and was made spontaneously while the declarant was under the stress of excitement caused by that perception. An armed robbery at a pharmacy counter is a startling event. Marcus spoke about 30 seconds after the robber left, "shaking" and "shouting," which shows he was still under the stress of the event and had no time to reflect or fabricate. His words describe the robber he had just seen. The exception is satisfied. (Unavailability is not required, so Marcus's absence does not matter.)

**Confrontation Clause.** The Sixth Amendment bars testimonial hearsay of a declarant who does not testify unless the declarant is unavailable and the defendant had a prior opportunity to cross-examine (*Crawford v. Washington*). A statement is testimonial if its primary purpose, viewed objectively, is to establish past facts for a later prosecution (*Davis v. Washington*; *Michigan v. Bryant*). Marcus spoke to a coworker, not to the police, within seconds of the robbery, in an excited and informal outburst. Its primary purpose was not to create evidence for trial. Owen may argue that naming the robber is accusatory, but statements to private persons are rarely testimonial (*Ohio v. Clark*). The statement is nontestimonial, so *Crawford* does not apply even though Owen never cross-examined Marcus.

**§ 352.** Probative value on identity is high and nothing about the statement is unfairly inflammatory, so § 352 does not require exclusion.

**Conclusion:** The court properly admitted item 1.

### Item 2 — Kyle's recorded station-house statement

**Relevance.** Kyle's statement that "Owen did the robbery" directly identifies Owen and is highly relevant.

**Hearsay.** The recording is an out-of-court statement offered to prove that Owen committed the robbery. It is hearsay.

**Unavailability (§ 240).** A declarant who is exempted from testifying on the ground of privilege is unavailable (§ 240(a)(1)). The court sustained Kyle's privilege against self-incrimination, so Kyle is unavailable.

**Declaration against interest (§ 1230).** A statement of an unavailable declarant is admissible if, when made, it so far subjected the declarant to criminal liability (or civil liability, or social disgrace) that a reasonable person would not have made it unless it were true. California admits only the portions that are specifically disserving to the declarant; collateral assertions that inculpate others are excluded (*People v. Leach*; *People v. Duarte*), and courts also consider whether the circumstances indicate trustworthiness.
- "I just drove the car and waited" and "He gave me two bottles" expose Kyle to liability as an aider and abettor and for receiving stolen property, so those portions are against his penal interest.
- "Owen did the robbery" is not against Kyle's interest. It shifts the main blame to Owen and minimizes Kyle's own role ("I *just* drove"). Kyle made it in custody, after arrest with stolen pills, when he had an obvious motive to curry favor with police by naming someone else. This is exactly the blame-shifting collateral assertion *Leach* and *Duarte* exclude.
- The self-inculpatory portions, standing alone, have little value against Owen except through the reference to "He," which is the inadmissible collateral part.

So § 1230 would admit at most the redacted portions about Kyle's own conduct, not the identification of Owen.

**Confrontation Clause.** Independently, the statement is testimonial: it was made to a detective, at the station, after Miranda warnings, in a recorded interview about a completed crime. Its primary purpose was to establish past events for prosecution (*Crawford*; *Davis*). Kyle did not testify (invoking the privilege is not testifying subject to cross-examination), and Owen never had an opportunity to cross-examine him. Prop 8 cannot override this federal constitutional bar.

**Conclusion:** The court erred in admitting item 2. The Confrontation Clause bars the entire recording, and § 1230 would not admit the portion naming Owen in any event.

### Item 3 — The Valley Drug incident

**Relevance.** If Owen robbed Valley Drug in a similar way, that tends to show he committed this robbery, so the evidence is logically relevant.

**Character evidence — § 1101(a).** Evidence of a person's other acts is inadmissible to prove his conduct on a specific occasion by showing a propensity (§ 1101(a)). Section 1101 survives Prop 8 (*Ewoldt*). The prosecution cannot use Valley Drug to argue "Owen robs pharmacies, so he robbed this one."

**Non-propensity purposes — § 1101(b).** Other acts are admissible when relevant to prove a fact such as motive, intent, plan, knowledge or identity. Under *Ewoldt*, the degree of similarity required depends on the purpose:
- **Identity** requires the greatest similarity: the common features must be so distinctive as to act like a signature. The prosecution will point to a pharmacy target, a demand for oxycodone, a mask, a hooded sweatshirt, a box cutter and nearby towns within 17 months. Owen will respond that masks, hoodies and opioid demands are generic features of pharmacy robberies and that a box cutter is a common weapon; nothing here is a unique "signature." The combination is suggestive but likely not distinctive enough to prove identity without relying on propensity.
- **Intent** requires the least similarity, but intent is not genuinely disputed. Owen does not claim the robber lacked intent to steal; he claims he was not the robber. Where the only contested issue is identity, other-acts evidence offered on intent adds little legitimate value and invites propensity reasoning (*Ewoldt*).
- **Proof of the other act.** The uncharged act must be proved sufficiently for the jury to find Owen committed it. Arturo's voice identification of a regular customer is some evidence, though made through a mask.

**§ 352.** Even if a § 1101(b) purpose fits, the court must exclude the evidence if its probative value is substantially outweighed by the probability of undue prejudice, confusion or undue consumption of time; § 352 survives Prop 8. Here probative value on identity is modest, while the prejudice is severe: the jury hears of an uncharged robbery for which Owen was never tried, which invites it to punish him for that act and to reason from propensity. A mini-trial about the 2024 incident would also consume time.

**Limiting instruction.** If admitted, Owen was entitled on request to an instruction limiting the evidence to identity (§ 355), but an instruction cannot cure a weak § 1101(b) foundation.

**Conclusion:** The court likely erred. The similarities are not distinctive enough to prove identity, intent is not in dispute, and § 352 favored exclusion. The question is close on identity, and a court could find the combination of features sufficient, but the better view is exclusion.

### Item 4 — Impeachment of Owen

By testifying, Owen put his credibility in issue and may be impeached like any witness.

**(a) The 2020 felony conviction for possession of methamphetamine for sale.**
*Rule.* In a California criminal case, art. I, § 28(f)(4) allows any prior felony conviction to be used for impeachment "without limitation," but due process limits this to felonies involving moral turpitude — a readiness to do evil, judged by the least adjudicated elements of the offense — and the court retains discretion under § 352 (*People v. Castro*).
*Moral turpitude.* *Castro* distinguished simple possession of drugs (not moral turpitude) from possession for sale, which involves an intent to corrupt others and therefore shows moral turpitude. Owen's conviction is for possession for sale, so it qualifies.
*§ 352.* The court weighs (1) how strongly the crime bears on honesty, (2) remoteness, (3) similarity to the charged offense, and (4) the effect on the defendant's decision to testify. Drug sale is not a crime of dishonesty in the strict sense, which reduces its value. But the conviction is only six years old, and it is dissimilar to robbery, which reduces the risk that the jury will infer guilt from it. Owen's credibility is central, because his alibi rests entirely on his own testimony. Owen may argue that a drug conviction will make the jury think he robbed the pharmacy for drugs, but the court can address that with a limiting instruction.
*Conclusion:* The court likely acted within its discretion in admitting the conviction, with a limiting instruction confining it to credibility (§ 355).

**(b) The 2023 shoplifting.**
*Rule.* In civil cases, § 787 bars specific instances of conduct to attack credibility. In criminal cases, Prop 8 abrogates § 787, so prior conduct involving moral turpitude, including misdemeanor conduct, is admissible to impeach, subject to § 352 (*People v. Wheeler*). The admissible evidence is the conduct itself, not a misdemeanor conviction record.
*Application.* Theft is a crime of dishonesty and therefore involves moral turpitude. The prosecutor proved the conduct by asking Owen about it, and he admitted it. That he "paid later" goes to weight, not admissibility. Under § 352, the conduct is three years old and minor, and the questioning took one question. Owen will argue that theft is similar to robbery and invites a "he's a thief" inference. That is a real risk, but it is limited by a one-question exchange about a minor shoplifting, and a limiting instruction can address it.
*Conclusion:* The court likely properly allowed the question.

### Item 5 — The text-message screenshot

**Relevance.** A message from Owen an hour after the robbery saying he "got what I needed tonight" and asking Tess not to tell anyone he "was out" contradicts his alibi and shows consciousness of guilt. It is relevant.

**Authentication (§§ 1400–1401).** A writing must be authenticated by evidence sufficient to sustain a finding that it is what the proponent claims, here a message sent by Owen. Methods include the testimony of a witness with knowledge and the writing's content and circumstances. Tess knew the number was Owen's cell number; the content refers to being "out" that night, which matches the prosecution's theory and contradicts Owen's alibi; and the timing fits. Tess, who took the screenshot, can testify that it accurately shows the message she received. Owen's claim that his roommate used his phone goes to weight, not admissibility, because the foundation is enough to sustain a finding of authorship.

**Secondary evidence rule (§§ 1520–1523).** California replaced the best evidence rule with the secondary evidence rule. The content of a writing, which includes a text message, may be proved by otherwise admissible secondary evidence, such as a screenshot. The court must exclude the copy only if a genuine dispute exists about its material terms and justice requires exclusion, or if admitting it would be unfair (§ 1521). Owen disputes *who* sent the message, not *what* it said, so there is no genuine dispute about its terms. The original was deleted by Tess, not by the prosecution, and there is no suggestion of bad faith. The screenshot is admissible.

**Hearsay — party admission (§ 1220).** If offered for its truth, the message is hearsay, but a statement by a party offered against him by the opposing party is admissible under § 1220. Once authenticated as Owen's, it falls within that exception.

**Privilege.** No spousal privilege applies (§§ 970, 980) because Tess is Owen's girlfriend, not his spouse.

**Conclusion:** The court properly admitted item 5.

**Common mistakes**
1. Calling § 1220 statements "not hearsay." That is the FRE label; under the CEC a party admission is hearsay admitted by an exception.
2. Treating unavailability alone as enough for Kyle's statement. *Crawford* also requires a prior opportunity to cross-examine, and Prop 8 cannot override it.
3. Admitting the whole § 1230 statement. Only the portions specifically against Kyle's interest qualify; "Owen did the robbery" is blame-shifting.
4. Accepting "identity and intent" as labels. Intent is not disputed in a mistaken-identity defense, and identity needs signature-level similarity.
5. Writing "the best evidence rule bars the screenshot." California uses the secondary evidence rule (§ 1521).
