# E-EVD-003 — Avery v. Stone and Anchor Tavern (Federal Rules of Evidence, civil)

## Facts

At 1:45 a.m. on August 15, 2025, closing time at the Anchor Tavern, the bouncer, Rick Stone, told Jordan Avery, 29, to finish his drink and leave. They argued near the exit. A grainy surveillance video shows Rick stepping behind Jordan; the two men then pass out of the camera's view for about four seconds, after which Jordan is seen lying beside a large concrete planter. Jordan suffered a fractured C5 vertebra in his neck and needed spinal-fusion surgery.

Jordan sued Rick for battery and Anchor Tavern, LLC for negligent retention of Rick and, as Rick's employer, for his conduct. The case is in federal court on diversity jurisdiction. Rick testified that Jordan swung at him first, that he grabbed Jordan's shoulders to push him away, and that Jordan tripped backward over the planter. The Tavern contends that Rick was not its employee but was supplied and supervised by SecureStaff, Inc., a staffing company, and that it was not negligent. At the jury trial, the following evidence was offered.

**Item 1.** Jordan called Tina Ruiz, who waited tables at the Tavern from 2023 to 2025. She testified that she saw Rick put patrons in chokeholds during closing-time arguments in March 2024 and January 2025, and that after each incident she told the Tavern's owner, Lou Grady, "Rick choked another customer." Jordan also called Marty Fox, a regular customer, who testified, "Whenever somebody argued with Rick at closing, he'd put them in a headlock and walk them out. I saw that maybe ten times in two years."

**Item 2.** Rick called Jordan's former roommate, who testified that Jordan "has a reputation around town as a hothead who starts fights when he drinks." Rick also offered a certified record of Jordan's 2023 misdemeanor assault conviction, arising from a fight at another bar. Rick testified that, a month before August 15, a coworker had told him that Jordan "got in a fight at the Wharf last year."

**Item 3.** Jordan offered the Tavern's commercial liability insurance policy, produced by the Tavern in discovery. The policy lists "all door staff, including R. Stone" as covered employees, based on an application signed by Lou Grady.

**Item 4.** Before trial, SecureStaff settled Jordan's claim against it for $40,000. Under the agreement, SecureStaff will receive back 25% of any amount Jordan recovers from the Tavern, up to $40,000. At trial, SecureStaff's manager, Dana Wills, testified for Jordan that Lou Grady "gave Rick his instructions every night." On cross-examination, the Tavern offered the settlement agreement. Jordan objected.

**Item 5.** (a) Jordan called Dr. Helen Park, the neurosurgeon who operated on him. She testified that the fracture pattern on Jordan's CT scan and what she saw in surgery showed a forward-bending (flexion) force with compression of the neck, consistent with a chokehold and inconsistent with a backward fall onto a planter, which typically causes a backward-bending (extension) injury. She explained that she used the standard method of matching fracture patterns to injury mechanisms described in peer-reviewed spine literature.

(b) The Tavern called Glen Price, a security consultant who worked as a bouncer and bar manager for 25 years and now trains door staff. He testified (i) that the Tavern's practice of using two door staff for a 150-person bar met industry practice, based on his work at more than 40 bars and published industry training guidelines; and (ii) that, having watched the surveillance video, he believed Jordan's neck injury "was caused by his fall over the planter, not by any hold." Price has no medical or engineering training. He conceded that the video does not show the four seconds when the men were out of view.

## Call of the Question

Assuming all appropriate objections were made, should the court admit the evidence offered in items 1 through 5? Discuss. Answer according to the Federal Rules of Evidence.

## Model Answer

**Governing law and relevance.** The Federal Rules of Evidence govern in this diversity case. Evidence is relevant if it has any tendency to make a fact of consequence more or less probable (FRE 401) and is admissible unless a rule excludes it (FRE 402); the court may exclude relevant evidence whose probative value is substantially outweighed by unfair prejudice, confusion or waste of time (FRE 403). The facts of consequence are whether Rick choked Jordan, whether any force was privileged, whether Rick was the Tavern's employee, whether the Tavern knew or should have known that Rick was dangerous, and what caused the fracture.

### Item 1 — Rick's prior chokeholds; Marty's "habit" testimony

**Character evidence rule.** Evidence of a person's character trait, including through other acts, is not admissible to prove that on a particular occasion the person acted in accordance with the trait (FRE 404(a)(1), 404(b)(1)). Other acts may be admitted for another purpose, such as intent, knowledge or absence of mistake (FRE 404(b)(2)). When a trait is an essential element of a claim, it may be proved by specific instances of conduct (FRE 405(b)).

**Against Rick (battery).** Offered to show that Rick choked Jordan because he had choked others, Tina's testimony is classic propensity evidence. Jordan may argue it shows intent or absence of accident. But Rick does not claim an accidental hold; he denies any hold and says he pushed Jordan's shoulders. The only route from the prior chokeholds to this one is "he does it, so he did it again." **Inadmissible against Rick.**

**Against the Tavern (negligent retention).** Negligent retention requires proof that the employer knew or should have known of the employee's dangerous propensities. Rick's violent conduct and the Tavern's knowledge of it are therefore elements of the claim, so character is "in issue" and may be proved by specific instances (FRE 405(b)). The incidents also show notice, a non-character purpose.
- *Personal knowledge.* Tina saw both incidents (FRE 602).
- *Hearsay.* Her statements to Lou, "Rick choked another customer," are offered to show that the owner was told, not that the choking occurred (Tina proves that from her own observation). Offered for their effect on the listener, they are not hearsay (FRE 801(c)).

**FRE 403 and limiting instruction.** The danger is that the jury will use the incidents against Rick on the battery claim. That danger does not substantially outweigh the strong probative value on the retention claim. The court should, on request, instruct the jury to consider the evidence only on the Tavern's knowledge and Rick's fitness, not on whether Rick choked Jordan (FRE 105), and could consider separate trials of the claims if the risk is severe.

**Marty's testimony: habit or character?** Evidence of a person's habit is admissible to prove that he acted in accordance with it on a particular occasion (FRE 406). A habit is a regular, specific, semi-automatic response to a recurring situation; a general disposition is character. Jordan will argue that Marty describes a specific response ("headlock and walk them out") to a specific, recurring situation (an argument at closing), observed "maybe ten times." Rick will respond that a decision to use force against a resisting person is volitional and depends on the circumstances; "maybe ten times" over two years, without knowing how many closing-time arguments ended differently, does not show near-invariable regularity; and courts are reluctant to treat violent conduct as habit. **The testimony is better characterized as character evidence, inadmissible to prove that Rick choked Jordan.** It may, however, be admitted against the Tavern on whether it *should* have known of Rick's practice, since frequent headlocks on its own premises at closing could be observed by management, with the same limiting instruction.

**Conclusion:** Admissible against the Tavern only (notice and character in issue), with a limiting instruction; inadmissible against Rick.

### Item 2 — Jordan's reputation and 2023 conviction

**Reputation to show Jordan was the aggressor.** FRE 404(a)(2)(B) lets an *accused* in a *criminal* case offer evidence of the alleged victim's pertinent trait. This is a civil case, so that exception does not apply, and the general ban in FRE 404(a)(1) controls. The roommate's testimony that Jordan is a "hothead who starts fights" is offered only to show Jordan probably swung first. **Inadmissible.**

**The conviction record.**
- *As propensity:* barred for the same reason.
- *As impeachment if Jordan testifies:* FRE 609 permits impeachment by a crime punishable by more than one year, or by a crime whose elements required a dishonest act or false statement. A misdemeanor assault meets neither test. **Not admissible to impeach.**

**Rick's knowledge of the 2023 fight.** A defendant claiming self-defense must show he reasonably believed force was necessary. What Rick *knew* about Jordan bears on the reasonableness of his fear, which is a non-character purpose: the evidence is offered to show Rick's state of mind, not Jordan's conduct.
- The coworker's statement ("got in a fight at the Wharf last year") is offered to show its effect on Rick, not that the fight happened, so it is not hearsay.
- But Rick's knowledge extends only to what he heard. The certified record adds that Jordan was convicted, which Rick did not know, and its only additional value is propensity.

**FRE 403 and limiting instruction.** Rick's testimony about what he heard is probative of his fear and only moderately prejudicial; it should be admitted with an instruction limiting it to Rick's state of mind. The conviction record adds little to that purpose and invites a propensity inference, so the court should exclude it.

**Conclusion:** Exclude the reputation testimony and the conviction record; admit Rick's testimony about what he had heard, for his state of mind only.

### Item 3 — The Tavern's liability insurance policy

**Rule (FRE 411).** Evidence that a person was or was not insured against liability is not admissible to prove that the person acted negligently or otherwise wrongfully, but may be admitted for another purpose, such as proving a witness's bias or prejudice or proving agency, ownership or control.

**Application.** Offered to show the Tavern was negligent, the policy is barred. But the Tavern disputes that Rick was its employee, and the policy lists "all door staff, including R. Stone" as covered *employees*, on an application signed by the Tavern's owner. That is probative of agency, a permitted purpose that is genuinely disputed.

**Hearsay.** The employee listing derives from the application Lou Grady signed for the Tavern, so it is the Tavern's own statement offered against it (FRE 801(d)(2)(A)); the policy also describes operative contract terms.

**Authentication.** The Tavern produced the policy in discovery, which is sufficient to support a finding that it is the Tavern's policy (FRE 901).

**FRE 403.** The risk is that jurors will assume an insurer will pay and inflate damages. The court should redact coverage limits and irrelevant terms and give a limiting instruction (FRE 105).

**Conclusion:** Admissible to prove agency, in redacted form, with a limiting instruction.

### Item 4 — The settlement agreement

**Rule (FRE 408).** Evidence of furnishing or accepting valuable consideration in compromising a disputed claim, and statements in compromise negotiations, are not admissible to prove or disprove the validity or amount of the claim or to impeach by a prior inconsistent statement or contradiction. The court may admit such evidence for another purpose, such as proving a witness's bias or prejudice (FRE 408(b)).

**Application.** Jordan's claim against SecureStaff was disputed, and the agreement compromised it for $40,000, so FRE 408 applies. The Tavern does not offer the agreement to show that SecureStaff was liable or that the claim is worth any amount. It offers it to show that Dana's employer will recover 25% of whatever Jordan wins from the Tavern, up to $40,000. That gives Dana's employer a direct financial stake in Jordan's success against the Tavern, and Dana testified on the very issue (who controlled Rick) that shifts responsibility to the Tavern. Bias is always relevant, and extrinsic evidence of it is permitted.

Jordan will argue that the jury will infer that SecureStaff's payment proves the claim is valid, or that the $40,000 sets its value. The court can reduce that risk by instructing the jury to consider the agreement only in weighing Dana's credibility, and it may redact terms irrelevant to bias.

**Conclusion:** Admissible for bias under FRE 408(b), with a limiting instruction.

### Item 5 — Expert testimony

**Rule (FRE 702, as amended 2023).** A witness qualified by knowledge, skill, experience, training or education may give an opinion if the proponent demonstrates to the court that it is more likely than not that (a) the expert's specialized knowledge will help the trier of fact, (b) the testimony is based on sufficient facts or data, (c) it is the product of reliable principles and methods, and (d) the opinion reflects a reliable application of those principles and methods to the facts. The judge is the gatekeeper for all expert testimony, scientific or experience-based (*Daubert*; *Kumho Tire*), deciding these questions under FRE 104(a). The 2023 amendment makes clear that sufficiency of the basis and reliability of the application are admissibility questions for the court, not merely matters of weight for cross-examination.

**(a) Dr. Park.**
- *Qualification.* A neurosurgeon who operated on Jordan is qualified to explain how spinal fractures occur.
- *Helpfulness.* Injury mechanics are beyond lay knowledge and go to the central dispute (chokehold vs. fall).
- *Basis.* She relied on the CT scan and her own observations in surgery, data of the kind spine specialists use.
- *Method and application.* Matching fracture patterns to injury mechanisms using peer-reviewed literature is a tested and accepted method (*Daubert* factors: peer review, general acceptance). She applied it to Jordan's specific fracture and explained why a backward fall typically produces a different (extension) pattern.
- *Ultimate issue.* Her opinion that the fracture is "consistent with a chokehold" embraces an ultimate issue, which is permitted (FRE 704(a)), and it stays within what her method supports.

The Tavern may argue that a fall in an unusual position could produce flexion, or that she did not review the video. Those points go to weight; they do not show that her method or its application is unreliable. **Admissible.**

**(b)(i) Price on staffing.** Expertise may rest on experience (*Kumho Tire*). Price spent 25 years as a bouncer and bar manager and now trains door staff; he compared the Tavern's staffing with practice at more than 40 bars and with published training guidelines. An experience-based witness must explain how his experience leads to his conclusion and why it is a sufficient basis, and Price ties his opinion to specific comparisons and written guidelines. Industry custom on staffing levels is outside jurors' knowledge and helpful on the standard of care. **Admissible**, subject to cross-examination on how comparable the 40 bars were.

**(b)(ii) Price on causation.** This opinion fails FRE 702 on several grounds:
- *Qualification.* Expertise must match the subject of the opinion. Price has no medical or engineering training; bar security work does not qualify him to say what force fractured a vertebra.
- *Basis.* He relied on a video that, as he conceded, does not show the four seconds in which the injury occurred.
- *Method and application.* Watching a video and forming a belief is not a reliable method for determining a spinal injury mechanism, and nothing connects the footage to the conclusion except his say-so (*Joiner*).

Under the amended rule, the court may not admit the opinion and leave these defects to cross-examination; the Tavern must show reliability by a preponderance and cannot. Nor is it admissible as lay opinion (FRE 701): Price did not perceive the event, and the opinion requires specialized knowledge. **Excluded.**

- **45 or below:** discusses relevance and "prejudice" generally without the specific rules.

**Common mistakes**
1. Applying the criminal "mercy rule" exceptions (404(a)(2)) in a civil case.
2. Forgetting that character can be "in issue" (negligent retention or hiring) and then provable by specific instances.
3. Treating violent conduct as habit because it happened several times. Habit requires near-automatic regularity.
4. Treating 411 and 408 as absolute bars. Both allow other purposes, including agency and bias.
5. Leaving an unqualified or unsupported expert opinion to cross-examination. After 2023, the court must find each FRE 702 requirement more likely than not.
