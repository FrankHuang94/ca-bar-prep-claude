# E-EVD-002 — Kim v. ZipRide (Federal Rules of Evidence, civil)

## Facts

ZipRide, Inc. rents electric scooters to the public through a phone app. On June 14, 2026, Nora Kim rented ZipRide scooter #4471 and was riding it down steep Hillcrest Avenue when, she says, the rear brake failed. She crashed into a parked car and broke her right wrist. Nora sued ZipRide in federal district court, alleging that ZipRide negligently maintained the scooter. ZipRide denies that the brake failed and contends that Nora was riding too fast. At the jury trial, the following evidence was offered.

**Item 1.** ZipRide called its field technician, Jared Wills, who testified that he inspected scooter #4471 on June 10 and that its brakes "worked perfectly." On cross-examination Nora's lawyer asked Jared whether, at the crash scene, he had told Nora, "I flagged this one's brake cable last week. I told my supervisor, and he said to leave it out until after the weekend rush." Jared denied saying it. In her rebuttal case, Nora testified that Jared, who had arrived in a ZipRide van about 20 minutes after the crash to collect the scooter, looked at it and made exactly that statement to her. Jared still works for ZipRide.

**Item 2.** Nora offered a printout of ZipRide's electronic maintenance log for scooter #4471. ZipRide's records custodian, testifying under subpoena, explained that company policy requires technicians to enter a note from their work phones at the time of every inspection and every repair, and that the system stores each entry automatically with the technician's name and a time stamp. A June 10 entry by "J. Wills" reads: "Rear brake cable frayed — replace." The log contains no later entry for scooter #4471 showing that the cable was replaced.

**Item 3.** ZipRide's maintenance director, Rhea Owens, testified on direct examination that "there was no practical way to detect a failing brake cable on a scooter except visual inspection." Nora then offered evidence that on July 1, 2026, ZipRide installed on all of its scooters an electronic sensor that automatically locks a scooter out of service when brake pressure drops.

**Item 4.** Nora offered a letter that ZipRide's customer-care manager sent her on June 20, before she had made any claim or hired a lawyer. It reads: "We are very sorry about your accident. Our records show that the brake cable on your scooter was overdue for replacement. ZipRide will pay your emergency-room bill." Nora also offered her own testimony that in September 2026, at a mediation held after she had sued for $250,000 and ZipRide had denied liability, ZipRide's claims adjuster said, "We know that brake was bad. We'll pay $40,000 to make this go away."

**Item 5.** Nora offered the emergency-room record prepared by Nurse Patel at Valley Medical Center on June 14. It reads: "Pt reports R wrist pain 8/10, numbness in fingers. States she fell off e-scooter going downhill when the rear brake failed. States 'that company never fixes anything.'" The hospital's records custodian testified that triage nurses record each patient's history at intake as part of the hospital's regular practice.

## Call of the Question

Assuming all appropriate objections were made, should the court admit the evidence offered in items 1 through 5? Discuss. Answer according to the Federal Rules of Evidence.

## Model Answer

**Governing law and relevance.** The Federal Rules of Evidence govern. Evidence is relevant if it has any tendency to make a fact of consequence more or less probable (FRE 401), and relevant evidence is admissible unless excluded by another rule (FRE 402), including FRE 403 (probative value substantially outweighed by unfair prejudice, confusion or waste of time). The facts of consequence are whether the brake was defective, whether ZipRide knew or should have known of it, and whether ZipRide's negligence caused Nora's injury.

### Item 1 — Jared's statement at the scene

**Relevance.** Jared's statement shows that ZipRide knew of a brake-cable problem days before the crash and kept the scooter in service anyway. It is central to negligence.

**Hearsay.** Hearsay is a statement, other than one made while testifying at the current trial, offered to prove the truth of the matter asserted (FRE 801(c)). Nora offers Jared's scene statement to prove that the cable had been flagged and left in service, so it is hearsay unless excluded.

**Opposing-party statement by an employee (FRE 801(d)(2)(D)).** A statement offered against a party is not hearsay if made by the party's agent or employee on a matter within the scope of that relationship while it existed. Jared is a ZipRide technician who arrived in a ZipRide van to collect the scooter; inspecting scooters and reporting their condition is the core of his job; and he was employed when he spoke (he still is). The employment relationship is shown by evidence other than the statement itself (the van, his own testimony that he inspects for ZipRide), as FRE 801(d)(2) requires. Personal knowledge is not required for party statements, though Jared had it. The statement is admissible against ZipRide for its truth.

**Embedded statement of the supervisor (FRE 805).** Jared repeats his supervisor's words: "leave it out until after the weekend rush." Each layer must be admissible. First, the supervisor's words are a directive, not an assertion of fact, so they arguably are not hearsay at all; they are offered to show what instruction was given (FRE 801(c); verbal act). Second, even if treated as an assertion, the supervisor is a ZipRide employee speaking about a matter within his job (deciding when scooters come out of service), so FRE 801(d)(2)(D) covers that layer too. Both layers are admissible.

**Impeachment by prior inconsistent statement (FRE 613).** Jared testified that the brakes "worked perfectly"; his scene statement that he had flagged the brake cable is inconsistent. Under FRE 613(b) as amended in 2024, extrinsic evidence of a prior inconsistent statement is admissible only after the witness is given an opportunity to explain or deny it and the adverse party is given an opportunity to examine the witness about it, unless the court orders otherwise. Nora's lawyer asked Jared about it on cross-examination and he denied it, so the foundation was laid before Nora testified to it. The matter is not collateral, because it goes to the central issue of the brake's condition, so extrinsic proof through Nora is permitted. In any event, FRE 613(b)'s foundation rule does not apply to an opposing party's statement under 801(d)(2).

**Substantive use (FRE 801(d)(1)(A)).** Before December 1, 2026, a testifying witness's prior inconsistent statement was substantive evidence only if given under penalty of perjury at a trial, hearing, other proceeding or deposition; an unsworn scene statement could only impeach. Under the amendment effective December 1, 2026, any prior inconsistent statement of a witness who testifies and is subject to cross-examination about it is admissible for its truth, subject to Rule 403. Here the choice does not matter: Jared's statement is admissible for its truth as an opposing-party statement under 801(d)(2)(D) regardless of which version of 801(d)(1)(A) applies.

**FRE 403.** The statement is highly probative and not unfairly prejudicial; ZipRide's only argument is that it is damaging, which is not *unfair* prejudice.

**Conclusion:** Admissible, both to impeach Jared and as substantive evidence against ZipRide.

### Item 2 — The maintenance log printout

**Relevance.** The June 10 entry shows ZipRide had notice of a frayed rear brake cable four days before the crash; the absence of a repair entry suggests it was never fixed.

**Authentication (FRE 901).** The proponent must produce evidence sufficient to support a finding that the item is what it is claimed to be. ZipRide's custodian testified how entries are made and stored, with names and time stamps, which authenticates the printout through testimony of a witness with knowledge (901(b)(1)) and evidence describing a system that produces an accurate result (901(b)(9)).

**Best evidence rule (FRE 1001–1002).** Nora is proving the content of the log, so the original is required. For electronically stored information, any printout or other output readable by sight that accurately reflects the information is an "original" (FRE 1001(d)). The rule is satisfied.

**Hearsay — business record (FRE 803(6)).** The June 10 entry is Jared's out-of-court assertion that the cable was frayed, offered for its truth. A record of an act, event or condition is admissible if (1) made at or near the time by, or from information transmitted by, someone with knowledge; (2) kept in the course of a regularly conducted business activity; (3) making it was a regular practice of that activity; (4) shown by the testimony of the custodian or a qualified witness; and (5) the opponent does not show that the source or circumstances indicate a lack of trustworthiness. Jared, who inspected the scooter, made the entry at the time of inspection on his work phone; company policy requires it; ZipRide's custodian laid the foundation. ZipRide cannot plausibly show untrustworthiness in its own routine maintenance system.

**Party statement (alternative).** The entry is also Jared's statement within the scope of his employment, offered against ZipRide (801(d)(2)(D)).

**Absence of an entry (FRE 803(7)).** Evidence that a business record does not include a matter is admissible to prove that the matter did not occur if a record was regularly kept for a matter of that kind and the opponent does not show untrustworthiness. Policy requires a log entry for every repair, so the absence of a replacement entry is admissible to show the cable was not replaced. ZipRide may argue that technicians sometimes forget to log repairs, but that goes to weight unless ZipRide shows the system is untrustworthy.

**Conclusion:** The printout is admissible to prove both the frayed cable and the absence of a repair.

### Item 3 — Brake sensors installed after the crash

**Rule (FRE 407).** When measures are taken that would have made an earlier injury less likely to occur, evidence of them is not admissible to prove negligence, culpable conduct, a defect, or the need for a warning. The court may admit it for another purpose, such as impeachment or, if disputed, proving ownership, control or the feasibility of precautionary measures. The policy is to encourage safety improvements.

**Application.** ZipRide installed the sensors on July 1, after Nora's June 14 crash, and they would have made a brake failure less likely to injure a rider. Offered to show that ZipRide was negligent, the evidence is barred.

**Feasibility and impeachment exceptions.** Nora will argue that Rhea's testimony that "there was no practical way to detect a failing brake cable … except visual inspection" put feasibility in dispute and opened the door to contradiction: ZipRide in fact installed an electronic detection system within three weeks. ZipRide will respond that Rhea described the state of things at the time of the accident, did not deny that sensors existed, and that courts read the impeachment exception narrowly so that it does not swallow the rule. The better view is that "no practical way" is a direct claim that a precaution was not feasible, so feasibility is now disputed and the evidence may come in for that purpose and to impeach Rhea.

**FRE 403 and limiting instruction.** The jury may misuse the evidence as an admission of fault. The court should admit it only for feasibility and impeachment and, on request, instruct the jury not to consider it as proof of negligence (FRE 105).

**Conclusion:** Admissible for the limited purposes of feasibility and impeachment, with a limiting instruction; inadmissible to prove negligence.

### Item 4 — The June 20 letter and the mediation statement

**(a) The customer-care letter.**
- *Offer to pay medical expenses (FRE 409).* Evidence of furnishing, offering or promising to pay medical or hospital expenses resulting from an injury is not admissible to prove liability. "ZipRide will pay your emergency-room bill" must be excluded.
- *Accompanying statement of fact.* Unlike FRE 408, Rule 409 does not protect statements made with the offer. "Our records show that the brake cable on your scooter was overdue for replacement" is a factual admission. It is an opposing-party statement by an employee whose job (customer care for accidents) includes communicating with injured riders (801(d)(2)(C)–(D)), offered against ZipRide.
- *FRE 408 does not apply.* Rule 408 protects offers and statements made in compromising or attempting to compromise a *disputed* claim. On June 20 Nora had made no claim and had no lawyer, and nothing in the letter offered to settle anything. There was no dispute to compromise.
- *Expression of sympathy.* "We are very sorry" is a party statement; the FRE have no rule excluding expressions of sympathy, but it says little about fault, and the court could limit it under 403 if ZipRide shows a risk the jury would treat it as an admission.
- *Authentication.* Nora received the letter from ZipRide's customer-care manager; its letterhead and content (the scooter's records) are sufficient (901(b)(4)).
- *Conclusion:* Redact the offer to pay the bill; admit the factual statement about the overdue cable.

**(b) The adjuster's statement at mediation.**
- *Rule (FRE 408).* Evidence of offering valuable consideration to compromise a disputed claim, and conduct or statements made during compromise negotiations about the claim, are not admissible to prove or disprove the validity or amount of the claim or to impeach by a prior inconsistent statement or contradiction.
- *Application.* By September there was a genuine dispute: Nora had sued for $250,000 and ZipRide had denied liability. The adjuster spoke at a mediation, which is a compromise negotiation. Both the offer of $40,000 and the statement "We know that brake was bad" are excluded when offered, as here, to prove liability. No permitted purpose (such as bias or undue delay) is suggested.
- *Conclusion:* Inadmissible.

### Item 5 — The emergency-room record

**Multiple hearsay (FRE 805).** The record contains two layers: Nurse Patel's written entries, and Nora's statements to the nurse. Each must satisfy an exception.

**Layer 1 — the record (FRE 803(6)).** A hospital is a regularly conducted business activity; intake histories are recorded at the time by triage nurses as a regular practice; the custodian laid the foundation; nothing suggests untrustworthiness. Nurse Patel recorded what she heard and observed in the course of her duties. This layer is admissible. But 803(6) covers only information from people acting in the regular course of the business; Nora was not under a business duty, so her statements need their own exception.

**Layer 2 — Nora's statements (FRE 803(4)).** A statement made for, and reasonably pertinent to, medical diagnosis or treatment that describes medical history, past or present symptoms or sensations, their inception, or their general cause is admissible, regardless of whether the declarant is available. Statements of fault are not admissible under this exception.
- "R wrist pain 8/10, numbness in fingers": present symptoms, plainly pertinent. Admissible.
- "Fell off e-scooter going downhill": the general cause of the injury, which helps the doctor understand the mechanism and force of the fall. Admissible.
- "When the rear brake failed": Nora will argue that it explains how the fall happened. ZipRide will respond that a wrist fracture is treated the same way whatever made the brake fail, so this clause attributes the cause to the scooter's condition, which is a matter of fault, not treatment. The better view is that it is not reasonably pertinent and should be redacted.
- "That company never fixes anything": an accusation of fault with no medical purpose. Excluded.
- *Party statement?* Nora is offering her own statements, so 801(d)(2) does not help her; it applies only to statements offered *against* the declarant-party.

**FRE 403.** Redaction removes the prejudicial fault statements and preserves the medical content.

**Conclusion:** Admissible in redacted form: the symptoms and the fact that she fell from a scooter going downhill; the brake-failure clause and the accusation are excluded.

- **65–70**: correct conclusions on most items; misses the embedded supervisor statement or 803(7), or treats the letter under 408.
- **55–60**: misses 801(d)(2)(D) on item 1 (treating Jared's statement as impeachment only), or admits the whole ER record under 803(6) without a second layer.
- **45–50**: conclusions without rules; several items unanalyzed.

**Common mistakes**
1. Admitting the ER note's fault clauses under 803(4). Only statements reasonably pertinent to diagnosis or treatment qualify; "that company never fixes anything" is fault.
2. Excluding the overdue-cable sentence under 409. Rule 409 excludes only the offer to pay medical expenses; accompanying factual statements come in. (Under 408, by contrast, negotiation statements are excluded, but 408 needs a disputed claim.)
3. Treating any post-accident repair as admissible once feasibility is mentioned. The exception applies only if feasibility is genuinely disputed; tie it to Rhea's "no practical way" testimony.
4. Building item 1 on the December 2026 amendment to FRE 801(d)(1)(A). The opposing-party route is the stronger and settled ground.
5. Forgetting that a party cannot offer its own out-of-court statement as an "admission."

**Study note — how the California Evidence Code would differ (not asked by this call).** Item 3: in a strict products liability claim, California's § 1151 does not exclude subsequent repairs (*Ault*); in negligence, as here, the result is similar. Item 4: § 1152 also excludes humanitarian offers and § 1160 excludes expressions of sympathy in civil cases. Item 5: the CEC has no general medical-diagnosis exception (§ 1253 covers only statements by child-abuse victims under 12); Nora's present pain would come in under § 1250. Item 1: California has no general employee-statement exception like 801(d)(2)(D) (see `03-subjects/evidence/ca-distinctions.md`).
