# E-REM-001 — Rosa v. Kurt and Quentin (stolen guitar; forged checks)

## Facts

Rosa, a retired music teacher in Sacramento, owns a collection of vintage guitars. Her most prized instrument is a 1957 archtop guitar handmade by a renowned luthier, one of about a dozen of its model known to exist. Rosa's late father gave it to her, and she has played it at every family wedding for 40 years. An appraiser valued it at $90,000 in 2025.

Kurt worked as Rosa's personal assistant for six years. He had a key to her house and helped pay her bills, but he had no authority to sign checks.

In June 2026, while Rosa was traveling, Kurt took the archtop guitar from her house and sold it to Quentin, a collector in San Francisco, for $85,000 in cash. Kurt told Quentin he was selling it for his late uncle's estate; Quentin had no reason to doubt him and paid a fair price. Kurt lost all of the $85,000 gambling in Reno within a month.

In the same period, Kurt forged Rosa's signature on four checks drawn on her account, totaling $40,000, payable to himself. His own checking account had a balance of $10,000, all his own money, on June 30. The account then showed:

| Date | Transaction | Balance |
|---|---|---|
| July 1 | Deposit of the four forged checks (+$40,000) | $50,000 |
| July 8 | Payment for a vacation cruise (−$12,000) | $38,000 |
| July 15 | Down payment on a condominium in Davis (−$30,000) | $8,000 |
| August 1 | Deposit of Kurt's paycheck (+$6,000) | $14,000 |

Kurt bought the condominium for $300,000, paying the $30,000 down payment and borrowing the remaining $270,000 from a bank, which took a recorded deed of trust. Title is in Kurt's name. The condominium is now worth $360,000. Kurt has no other significant assets and owes $60,000 on credit cards.

On October 20, 2026, Rosa saw her guitar in an online listing. Quentin has agreed to sell it for $120,000 to a collector in Japan and plans to ship it overseas in ten days. Rosa has just discovered the forged checks. She has sued Kurt and Quentin in California superior court.

## Call of the Question

1. What remedies may Rosa pursue to recover the guitar or its value? Discuss.
2. What remedies may Rosa pursue with respect to the $40,000? Discuss.
3. May Rosa recover punitive damages from Kurt? Discuss.

## Model Answer

### Call 1 — The guitar

#### A. Underlying liability

Conversion is an intentional exercise of dominion or control over a chattel that so seriously interferes with the owner's rights that the defendant may justly be required to pay its full value. Kurt took the guitar and sold it, which is conversion. A thief cannot pass title, even to a good-faith purchaser for value; only a person with voidable title (for example, one who obtained goods by fraud) can do so (UCC § 2-403(1)). Kurt stole the guitar, so Quentin acquired no title despite paying a fair price in good faith. By buying and exercising control over Rosa's guitar, Quentin is also a converter, though an innocent one. Rosa still owns the guitar.

#### B. Legal remedies

**1. Conversion damages.** The plaintiff recovers the chattel's fair market value at the time and place of conversion, plus interest; in effect the converter is forced to buy it. The guitar was worth about $90,000 in June 2026, so Rosa could recover about $90,000 plus interest from Kurt (or from Quentin, as a converter). But Kurt has no significant assets and $60,000 of debt, so a judgment against him may be uncollectible; and money cannot replace a one-of-a-kind instrument from her late father that she has played for 40 years. Damages are available but inadequate.

**2. Restitution of Kurt's gain (waiver of tort).** Rosa may instead recover Kurt's gain from the wrong, the $85,000 sale price. That is less than the $90,000 value, so damages are the better measure. A constructive trust on the proceeds is not possible: Kurt gambled all $85,000 away, so there is no identifiable asset to trace into, leaving only a personal money claim.

**3. Replevin (claim and delivery).** Replevin is a legal action to recover possession of specific personal property wrongfully held by the defendant, plus damages for its detention. The plaintiff must show a right to immediate possession. Rosa owns the guitar, Quentin took no title from a thief, and Quentin holds it. In California, Rosa may obtain a pre-judgment writ of possession after notice and a hearing by showing the probable validity of her claim and posting an undertaking; the sheriff then seizes the guitar. Quentin will argue that he is a bona fide purchaser, but that defense protects only those who take from someone with at least voidable title. **Replevin against Quentin is Rosa's best legal remedy**, and Quentin's recourse is against Kurt (for breach of the warranty of title).

#### C. Equitable relief: TRO and preliminary injunction against shipment

The writ of possession takes time, and Quentin plans to ship the guitar to Japan in ten days. Rosa should seek a TRO and then a preliminary injunction barring Quentin from selling, shipping or transferring the guitar pending trial.

**Temporary restraining order.** A TRO preserves the status quo until a hearing on a preliminary injunction can be held. It may issue without notice only if specific facts show that immediate and irreparable injury will occur before the adverse party can be heard. Here, ten days is enough time to give Quentin short notice, and the court may well require it; but if the shipment date moved up, an ex parte order would be justified because once the guitar leaves the country the court practically cannot recover it.

**Preliminary injunction.**
- *Likelihood of success.* Very high: Kurt stole the guitar and Quentin took no title.
- *Irreparable harm and inadequate legal remedy.* The guitar is unique (one of about a dozen), has deep sentimental value as her father's gift, and is about to leave the jurisdiction. Damages cannot replace it, and the realistic source of damages, Kurt, is nearly insolvent. Quentin may argue that a $90,000 or $120,000 figure makes the harm measurable, but money cannot buy another instrument of this kind.
- *Balance of hardships.* An injunction only delays Quentin's resale; he keeps the guitar pending trial. Denying it means Rosa permanently loses a unique heirloom.
- *Public interest.* The public interest favors returning stolen property to its owner.
- *California test.* California courts weigh two interrelated factors, the likelihood that the plaintiff will prevail and the relative interim harm to the parties, on a sliding scale (*IT Corp. v. County of Imperial*). Both strongly favor Rosa.
- *Status quo and feasibility.* The order is prohibitory (do not ship or sell), simple to enforce by contempt.
- *Bond.* Rosa must post an undertaking to cover Quentin's damages if he was wrongfully enjoined (for example, the lost $120,000 sale).

**Conclusion:** The court will likely grant a TRO and a preliminary injunction freezing the guitar, followed by a writ of possession and, at trial, return of the guitar.

### Call 2 — The $40,000

#### A. Basis and the inadequate legal remedy

Kurt obtained $40,000 by forging Rosa's checks, a conversion and an unjust enrichment. Rosa can get a money judgment for $40,000 (quasi-contract or conversion), but Kurt has $60,000 of unsecured debt and few assets, so as a general creditor she would share pro rata with his credit-card lenders. Equitable restitution through tracing lets her reach specific property ahead of those creditors. California recognizes a constructive trust over property gained by fraud or other wrongful act (Civ. Code § 2224).

#### B. Tracing through Kurt's account

A claimant may follow her money into whatever the wrongdoer bought with it. When the wrongdoer commingles the claimant's money with his own, he is presumed to spend his own money first, but the claimant's interest in the account can never exceed the lowest intermediate balance after her deposit; later deposits of the wrongdoer's own money do not restore it.

| Date | Event | Balance | Rosa's traceable share in account |
|---|---|---|---|
| July 1 | +$40,000 forged checks | $50,000 | $40,000 (Kurt's own: $10,000) |
| July 8 | −$12,000 cruise | $38,000 | $38,000 (Kurt's $10,000 spent first, then $2,000 of Rosa's) |
| July 15 | −$30,000 condo down payment | $8,000 | $8,000; the $30,000 was entirely Rosa's money and is traced into the condo |
| August 1 | +$6,000 paycheck | $14,000 | $8,000 (lowest intermediate balance; paycheck does not restore her share) |

- **Cruise:** $2,000 of Rosa's money was dissipated on a vacation; no asset remains, so that part of her claim is a personal money claim only.
- **Condo:** $30,000 of Rosa's money paid the down payment.
- **Account:** Rosa may claim $8,000 of the current $14,000 balance, by constructive trust or equitable lien.

#### C. The condominium — constructive trust or equitable lien?

A **constructive trust** compels the wrongdoer to convey to the claimant the property (or the share of it) acquired with her money, so she captures any appreciation. An **equitable lien** gives her a security interest in the property for the amount taken, with a deficiency judgment if the property is worth less. When the claimant's money paid for only part of an asset, courts give her a proportionate share by constructive trust or a lien.

- *Rosa's contribution.* Her $30,000 paid 10% of the $300,000 price; the rest came from the bank loan, which is Kurt's obligation.
- *Constructive trust.* A 10% share of a condo now worth $360,000 is worth $36,000.
- *Equitable lien.* A lien secures only $30,000 (plus interest).
- *Choice.* Because the condo has appreciated, a proportionate constructive trust is better for Rosa ($36,000 versus $30,000). Rosa might argue that her money supplied all of Kurt's initial equity, so she should take all of the equity and appreciation; a court is more likely to measure her share by her contribution to the price, but she could raise the argument. If the condo had lost value, she would choose the lien.

#### D. Priority and defenses

A constructive trust or equitable lien gives Rosa priority over Kurt's unsecured creditors, such as the credit-card lenders. It does not defeat the bank, which lent $270,000 for value without notice of Rosa's claim and holds a recorded, senior deed of trust; Rosa's share is subject to it. Kurt is a conscious wrongdoer, so he cannot claim a change-of-position defense. Rosa acted promptly upon discovering the forgeries, so laches does not apply.

**Conclusion on call 2:** Rosa should seek (1) a constructive trust over a 10% interest in the condo, (2) a constructive trust or lien on $8,000 in Kurt's account, and (3) a money judgment for the $2,000 dissipated on the cruise.

### Call 3 — Punitive damages against Kurt

**Rule.** Punitive damages may be awarded for tortious conduct that is malicious, willful and wanton, or fraudulent, to punish and deter; they are never available for breach of contract alone. In California, the plaintiff must prove by clear and convincing evidence that the defendant was guilty of oppression, fraud or malice (Civ. Code § 3294). Malice includes conduct intended to injure the plaintiff and despicable conduct carried on with a willful and conscious disregard of her rights; fraud is an intentional misrepresentation or concealment intended to deprive a person of property.

**Application.** Kurt's claims sound in tort (conversion and fraud), not contract. He used a position of trust as Rosa's assistant to steal a priceless family heirloom, forged her signature on four checks, and lied to Quentin about the guitar's origin. This is intentional, deceitful conduct aimed at depriving Rosa of her property: malice and fraud, provable by clear and convincing evidence (the forged checks, the sale, the false story).

**Predicates and limits.** California requires actual damages, which Rosa has (at least the dissipated $2,000, interest, and the harm from the guitar's detention), and evidence of the defendant's financial condition (*Adams v. Murakami*). Kurt's limited wealth will keep the award modest, because punitive damages must be measured against his ability to pay. Under due process (*BMW v. Gore*; *State Farm v. Campbell*), the award must be reasonable in light of the reprehensibility of the conduct (high: deliberate deceit, abuse of trust, a vulnerable older victim), the ratio to actual or potential harm (single-digit ratios are presumptively acceptable), and comparable civil penalties.

**Conclusion:** Rosa can likely recover punitive damages from Kurt, in an amount limited by his modest finances and the due-process guideposts. She cannot recover punitive damages from Quentin, an innocent purchaser.

### Election — the best combination

Rosa must avoid double recovery: if she recovers the guitar by replevin, she cannot also recover its $90,000 value from Kurt, though she may recover damages for its detention and her expenses. Her best package is:
1. A TRO and preliminary injunction freezing the guitar, then replevin from Quentin.
2. A proportionate constructive trust (10%) over the condo and a constructive trust or lien on $8,000 in Kurt's account.
3. A money judgment against Kurt for the untraceable $2,000 and other actual losses.
4. Punitive damages against Kurt.

**Common mistakes**
1. Protecting Quentin as a good-faith purchaser. A thief passes no title; UCC § 2-403 protects buyers only from a person with voidable title.
2. Letting the August paycheck restore Rosa's share of the account. Lowest intermediate balance caps it at $8,000.
3. Claiming the whole condo. Rosa's money paid only part of the price; she gets a proportionate share or a lien.
4. Choosing an equitable lien on an appreciated asset. Take the constructive trust when the asset has gone up.
5. Omitting financial-condition evidence for a California punitive award.
