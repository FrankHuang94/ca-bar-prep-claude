# E-REM-002 — Lena v. Victor (building and parking lot)

## Facts

Victor owns a two-story brick building at 12 Main Street, which sits on Lot 7 (Assessor's Parcel No. 041-007), and the adjoining paved parking lot, Lot 8 (Parcel No. 041-008). Victor's sale listing read: "Brick building + 14-space private parking lot. $900,000." Lena, a chef who has run a successful food truck for four years, wanted to open her first restaurant. In January 2026 she toured the building and the lot with Victor, who told her, "The lot comes with the building. Your customers will love having parking." They orally agreed on a price of $900,000 "for the building and its parking lot."

Victor's real-estate broker then prepared a written purchase agreement describing the property as "12 Main Street, Assessor's Parcel No. 041-007." Neither Lena nor Victor noticed that the description omitted Lot 8. Both signed on January 20, 2026. Lena deposited $45,000 in escrow; closing was set for April 30. Lena obtained a written loan commitment for the balance. Relying on the deal, she paid an architect $38,000 to design the restaurant, including an outdoor patio and a parking layout on Lot 8, and paid $7,000 in fees for a liquor-license application.

In December 2025, before the parties met, the city planning commission had held a public hearing on a proposal to rezone several Main Street parcels, including Lot 8, to allow four-story mixed-use buildings. Neither party knew of the hearing. On March 10, 2026, the city council adopted the rezoning. An appraiser now values Lot 7 at $650,000 and Lot 8 at $600,000 (up from about $250,000 before the rezoning), for a total of $1,250,000.

On March 20, Victor wrote to Lena: "The deal is off. The contract covers only Lot 7. And if it covered the parking lot, we were both wrong about what that lot was worth, so I'm entitled to cancel." The same week, Apex Development, which knew of Lena's contract, offered Victor $1,250,000 for both lots. Victor has not yet accepted.

Lena still wants the property. She projects that the restaurant would earn $200,000 a year in profit and that finding and fitting out another location would delay her opening by at least a year. She is ready to pay the full price.

## Call of the Question

1. Can Lena obtain reformation of the written agreement to include Lot 8? Discuss.
2. Can Victor rescind the agreement? Discuss.
3. Can Lena obtain specific performance? Discuss.
4. If Lena does not obtain specific performance, what damages can she recover? Discuss.

## Model Answer

### Call 1 — Reformation

**Rule.** Reformation is an equitable remedy that rewrites a written instrument to express the agreement the parties actually made, where the writing departs from that agreement because of a mutual mistake in expression, or because of one party's fraud or a unilateral mistake the other party knew of. The claimant must prove the prior agreement by clear and convincing evidence. The parol evidence rule, the statute of frauds and the claimant's negligence in failing to read the writing do not bar reformation; it is denied only where it would prejudice a bona fide purchaser or another third party who relied on the writing, or where laches applies (R2d § 155; Civ. Code § 3399).

**1. Prior agreement.** Lena must show that the parties agreed to sell both lots for $900,000. The evidence is strong: Victor's listing advertised the "building + 14-space private parking lot" for $900,000; during the tour Victor said "the lot comes with the building"; and the parties orally agreed on a price "for the building and its parking lot." Lena's architect drew a patio and parking layout on Lot 8, consistent with her understanding. Victor now says the contract covers only Lot 7, but he identifies no negotiation in which Lot 8 was dropped, and the price matched the listing for both parcels.

**2. Writing departs from the agreement.** The writing describes only Parcel No. 041-007 (Lot 7); Lot 8 has its own parcel number and was omitted.

**3. Mistake in expression.** Victor's broker prepared the description, and "neither Lena nor Victor noticed" the omission. Both parties intended to deal in both lots and both signed a writing that failed to say so: a mutual mistake in expression, the classic ground for reformation. Alternatively, if Victor knew the description was incomplete and stayed silent, Lena's unilateral mistake known to the other party also supports reformation.

**4. Clear and convincing evidence.** The listing, Victor's statement on the tour, the oral price term and Lena's reliance (the architect's plans) together make a clear and convincing case.

**5. No bar.** Victor will invoke the parol evidence rule and the statute of frauds, but neither bars reformation; they apply to the instrument as reformed. Lena's failure to check the parcel number is negligence, which is no defense. Apex is not a bona fide purchaser: it has not bought, and it knows of Lena's contract. Lena acted promptly after Victor's March 20 letter, so there is no laches.

**Conclusion:** The court will likely reform the agreement to describe both Lot 7 and Lot 8.

### Call 2 — Can Victor rescind for mutual mistake?

**Rule.** Where both parties, when contracting, are mistaken about a basic assumption on which the contract was made, and the mistake has a material effect on the agreed exchange, the contract is voidable by the adversely affected party unless that party bears the risk of the mistake (R2d §§ 152, 154). Mistakes about value or about future events are not basic-assumption mistakes. A party bears the risk if the contract allocates it to him, if he contracted aware that his knowledge was limited and treated that limited knowledge as sufficient (conscious ignorance), or if the court allocates it to him as reasonable. Rescission, if available, requires prompt notice and restoration of what was received.

**Application.**
- *Was there a mistake about an existing fact?* Victor will argue that both parties assumed Lot 8 was a parking lot worth about $250,000 and that the planning commission's December hearing, a fact existing at signing, made that assumption wrong. But at signing, Lot 8 *was* zoned for parking and *was* worth about $250,000; the parties were right about the present facts. What changed was the city council's March 10 decision, a later event. A belief that the rezoning would not happen (or not thinking about it at all) is at most a prediction about the future, not a mistake about an existing fact.
- *Value.* Victor's real complaint is that the land is now worth more. A mistake about value, or a later change in market value, is not a ground for rescission; it is the ordinary risk every seller of land takes.
- *Risk allocation.* Even if the pending proposal counts as an existing fact, Victor bears the risk. The rezoning hearing was public; as the owner, Victor was better placed than a buyer to know about proposals affecting his parcel, and he sold without investigating. Courts allocate the risk of a later rise in value to the seller who has agreed to a fixed price.
- *Material effect.* The increase is large ($350,000), but materiality alone is not enough without a basic-assumption mistake that Victor does not bear.

**Conclusion:** Victor likely cannot rescind. (He also has nothing to restore, since Lena's deposit is in escrow, so restoration would be easy if a ground existed.)

### Call 3 — Specific performance

**Rule.** Specific performance compels a party to perform. The plaintiff must show (1) a valid contract with definite and certain terms, (2) that her own conditions are performed or she is ready, willing and able to perform, (3) that the legal remedy is inadequate, (4) that enforcement is feasible, and (5) that no equitable defense applies. Mutuality of remedy is no longer required.

**1. Valid contract with certain terms.** The signed agreement identifies the parties, the price and (as reformed) both parcels, and satisfies the statute of frauds. The terms are definite enough for a court to order a deed.

**2. Lena's conditions.** Lena deposited $45,000, holds a written loan commitment and says she is ready to pay the full price. Victor repudiated on March 20, before closing; a buyer need not make a futile tender after an anticipatory repudiation, but must show she is ready, willing and able, which Lena can.

**3. Inadequate legal remedy.** Land is treated as unique, so damages are presumed inadequate. In California, Civil Code § 3387 presumes that money cannot adequately relieve the breach of an agreement to transfer real property; the presumption is conclusive only for a single-family dwelling that the buyer intends to occupy, and otherwise it shifts the burden to the seller. This is a commercial building and lot, so the presumption is rebuttable. Victor will argue that Lena is a commercial buyer whose loss is measurable in money. Lena will respond that she chose this specific building and lot on Main Street, designed her restaurant around them, and cannot find an identical substitute, and that her lost profits are hard to prove (see call 4). Victor is unlikely to rebut the presumption.

**4. Feasibility.** Ordering Victor to convey a deed is a single, simple act requiring no ongoing supervision.

**5. Mutuality.** Victor might argue that he could not have forced Lena to buy. Mutuality of remedy is no longer required where the plaintiff's own performance is assured, as it is here by the deposit, loan commitment and the court's power to condition the decree on payment.

**Defenses.**
- *Adequacy of consideration and fairness (§ 3391).* In California, specific performance cannot be enforced against a party who did not receive adequate consideration or as to whom the contract is not just and reasonable. Fairness is measured when the contract is made. In January, $900,000 matched Victor's own listing price for both parcels and their market value before the rezoning, so consideration was adequate and the contract was fair. The later increase does not make it unfair.
- *Mistake (R2d § 364(1)(a); Civ. Code § 3391(4)) [S-474].* Equity may refuse specific performance against a party whose assent was given under the influence of mistake, even where the mistake would not justify rescission. Victor will argue that he agreed without knowing of the pending rezoning. But both parties were equally unaware, the hearing was public, the price matched Victor's own listing, and the only "mistake" concerns the land's later value, a risk the seller bears. A court is unlikely to deny specific performance on this ground.
- *Hardship.* Losing a $350,000 windfall from a later event is not the kind of undue hardship that bars equity; Victor simply loses a better deal.
- *Laches and unclean hands.* Lena acted promptly; nothing suggests misconduct by her.
- *Bona fide purchaser and lis pendens.* A sale to a bona fide purchaser for value without notice would defeat specific performance. Apex knows of Lena's contract, so it could not be a bona fide purchaser, but Lena should record a notice of pending action (lis pendens) immediately to give constructive notice to anyone else.

**Conclusion:** Lena will likely obtain specific performance of the reformed contract, conveying both lots for $900,000.

### Call 4 — Damages if specific performance is not granted

**1. Expectation (benefit of the bargain).** A buyer whose seller wrongfully refuses to convey recovers the market value of the land at the time of breach minus the contract price, plus the return of any payments and incidental expenses. Under the minority "English rule," a seller who cannot convey because of a title defect and acts in good faith owes only the deposit and expenses, but Victor has no title defect; he refused to close to get a better price, so full benefit-of-the-bargain damages apply. California codifies this measure without any good-faith limitation, adding expenses of preparing to enter on the land, consequential damages according to proof, and interest (Civ. Code § 3306) [S-1221].
- With reformation: $1,250,000 (both lots at breach) − $900,000 = **$350,000**, plus return of the **$45,000 deposit**.
- Without reformation (contract for Lot 7 only at $900,000): Lot 7 is worth $650,000, less than the price, so Lena would have no benefit-of-the-bargain loss; she would recover only her deposit and reliance expenses.

**2. Consequential damages — lost restaurant profits.**
- *Foreseeability (Hadley v. Baxendale).* Consequential damages must have been foreseeable when the contract was made. Victor knew Lena was buying to open a restaurant (he told her "your customers will love having parking"), so some lost profits from delay were within his contemplation.
- *Certainty.* Damages must be proved with reasonable certainty. Lost profits of a new business are recoverable only with reliable evidence such as comparable businesses or expert projections. Lena's restaurant has never operated; her food-truck history is some evidence of her skill and customer base, but a sit-down restaurant at a new location is a different business. A projection of $200,000 a year is likely too speculative, though a court applying the modern rule might admit expert proof, subject to the trial court's gatekeeping of speculative lost-profit projections (*Sargon Enterprises, Inc. v. University of Southern California* (2012) 55 Cal.4th 747) [S-1213].
- *Mitigation.* Lena must make reasonable efforts to find another location. She cannot recover profits for a period she could have avoided; at most she might recover losses caused by the delay of a reasonable search.
- *Overlap.* The $350,000 market-value award compensates Lena for the land's value, not for business losses from a delayed opening, which California allows as consequential damages according to proof (Civ. Code § 3306) [S-1221]. But she cannot recover both the property's value and profits she can still earn at a substitute location, so any recoverable profits are limited to the delay period.
- *Conclusion:* Lost profits are unlikely to be recoverable, or will be limited to provable delay losses.

**3. Reliance as an alternative.** If expectation damages were uncertain, Lena could recover her reliance expenditures instead: $38,000 for the architect and $7,000 for the liquor-license application, plus her deposit. But a party may not recover both expectation and reliance for the same loss, because under full performance she would have spent those sums anyway. Since benefit-of-the-bargain damages here are certain (an appraisal) and much larger ($350,000), Lena should elect expectation damages. She may argue that the architect's plans, designed for this building, were wasted because of the breach and should be added as incidental loss; a court may allow the portion that is truly wasted and not duplicated.

**4. No punitive damages.** Victor's refusal to close to obtain a higher price is a willful breach of contract, but punitive damages are not available for breach of contract, however deliberate, absent an independent tort.

**Conclusion and election.** Lena's best remedy is reformation followed by specific performance. If specific performance were denied, she would elect expectation damages of $350,000 plus return of her $45,000 deposit, rather than $45,000 of reliance damages; lost profits are likely too speculative.

**Common mistakes**
1. Saying the parol evidence rule bars evidence of the oral agreement. It does not bar reformation.
2. Treating a post-contract rezoning as a mutual mistake. Mistake must concern facts existing at contracting; later events and value changes are risks the parties assume.
3. Calling the § 3387 presumption conclusive. It is conclusive only for a single-family home the buyer will occupy.
4. Measuring § 3391 fairness at the time of trial. Adequacy of consideration is judged when the contract was made.
5. Awarding expectation and reliance damages for the same expenditures, or punitive damages for a willful breach of contract.
