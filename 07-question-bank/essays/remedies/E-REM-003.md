## Facts

Harbor Point Holdings LLC ("Harbor Point") owns Harbor Point Plaza, a shopping center in Oceanside, California. In 2019 it leased a 6,000-square-foot store for 15 years to Verde Market, Inc. ("Verde"), an independent grocery owned by Sofia Alvarez. The lease provides: "Landlord shall not lease or permit any other premises in Harbor Point Plaza to be used for the sale of fresh produce, fresh meat or groceries as a primary business." It also provides: "Tenant shall not sell alcoholic beverages without Landlord's written consent." A memorandum of the lease, quoting the exclusive-use clause, was recorded in 2019. Since 2023, Verde has sold beer and wine without Harbor Point's written consent. Harbor Point learned of this in 2024 and never objected.

In January 2025, Harbor Point leased the center's vacant 25,000-square-foot anchor space for ten years to QuickGrocer, Inc., a regional discount grocery chain, for "general merchandise and grocery" use. QuickGrocer's title report listed the recorded memorandum. Its in-house lawyer wrote in an internal email: "Possible conflict with Verde's exclusive. Landlord has agreed to indemnify us. Proceed."

In February 2025, QuickGrocer hung a banner reading "QuickGrocer — Fresh Produce & Groceries — Coming Soon." Sofia immediately emailed Harbor Point's manager: "This violates my exclusive." The manager replied: "They'll mostly sell household goods. Don't worry." Sofia did nothing further for seven months. During that time QuickGrocer spent $1.4 million on refrigeration, shelving and build-out, and hired 45 local employees. About 60% of its planned sales floor was devoted to produce, meat and packaged food.

On September 25, 2025, a week before QuickGrocer's scheduled opening, Verde sued Harbor Point and QuickGrocer, seeking a permanent injunction and damages. At trial in February 2026, Verde's accountant testified that QuickGrocer's store would likely cut Verde's sales by about 35% but that lost profits are "very difficult to project." Verde operates on thin margins, and Sofia testified that the store would probably close within a year. QuickGrocer's witnesses testified that it could operate as a general-merchandise store with a small food section, but would lose much of the value of its refrigeration investment, and that shoppers would pay about 10% more at Verde than at QuickGrocer.

On February 20, 2026, the court entered a permanent injunction providing: "QuickGrocer shall not use more than 15% of the sales floor of its Harbor Point Plaza store for the display or sale of fresh produce, fresh meat or packaged groceries." The order was personally served on QuickGrocer and on its store manager, Dale Kim. QuickGrocer appealed but did not seek a stay.

In May 2026, Verde moved for contempt. Its evidence shows that about 30% of QuickGrocer's sales floor still displays food. In addition, in March 2026 QuickGrocer subleased a 2,000-square-foot corner of its store to FarmFresh Stand LLC ("FarmFresh"), owned by Dale's brother, which sells produce there. Dale arranged the sublease and gave FarmFresh a copy of the injunction "so they know what we can't do." QuickGrocer's CEO testified that the injunction is "wrong and on appeal" and that "FarmFresh is a separate business." Verde asks the court to (a) fine QuickGrocer $2,000 for each day until it complies; (b) order QuickGrocer to pay Verde's lost profits since the injunction; and (c) impose a $100,000 fine on QuickGrocer for its violations to date. Verde also asks for orders against Dale and FarmFresh.

## Call of the Question

1. Assume that the exclusive-use clause is enforceable against both Harbor Point and QuickGrocer and that QuickGrocer's planned store violates it. Was Verde entitled to a permanent injunction? Discuss.
2. What equitable defenses could Harbor Point and QuickGrocer raise, and how should the court rule on them? Discuss.
3. What relief should the court grant on Verde's contempt motion, and against whom? Discuss.

## Model Answer

### 1. Was Verde entitled to a permanent injunction?

A permanent injunction is a final order, after trial on the merits, commanding a defendant to do (mandatory) or refrain from doing (prohibitory) an act. The plaintiff must show (1) an inadequate legal remedy, (2) a protectable interest, (3) that enforcement is feasible, (4) that the balance of hardships, including the public interest, favors relief, and (5) that no equitable defense bars it (Civ. Code § 3422; CCP § 526). Liability is assumed. (A federal court would apply the similar four-factor *eBay* test.)

**A. Inadequate legal remedy.** Damages are inadequate when they are too speculative to measure, when the harm is continuing so that damages would require a multiplicity of suits, or when the harm threatens irreparable loss.
- *Speculative damages.* Verde's own accountant says its lost profits are "very difficult to project." Losses of customers and goodwill to a discount competitor next door are notoriously hard to separate from other market forces.
- *Continuing harm.* The breach would continue every day for the rest of Verde's lease, into 2034. Verde would have to sue repeatedly or accept a speculative lump-sum estimate of nine years of losses.
- *Irreparable harm.* Sofia testified that Verde would probably close within a year. Destruction of a going business is a classic irreparable injury.
- *Defendants' argument.* Harbor Point and QuickGrocer are solvent, and QuickGrocer will argue that a damages award, perhaps funded by Harbor Point's indemnity, would be adequate. But solvency does not make speculative, continuing losses measurable.

The legal remedy is inadequate.

**B. Protectable interest.** Equity traditionally protected property rights; modern courts protect any legally protectable interest. Verde holds a leasehold and the benefit of a recorded restrictive covenant on the rest of the center, both of which are interests in land. This element is easily met.

**C. Feasibility.** Prohibitory injunctions are easy to enforce because the court need only punish violations. Mandatory injunctions that require ongoing supervision are disfavored. The order here is prohibitory: QuickGrocer must *not* use more than a set share of its floor for food. The defendants will argue that "primary business" is too vague to police and that enforcement would require constant measurement of floor space. The court solved that problem by translating "primary business" into a measurable standard (no more than 15% of the sales floor). Every injunction must state its terms specifically and describe the restrained acts in reasonable detail. A floor-space limit can be checked by a single inspection, without day-to-day supervision. The order is feasible.

**D. Balancing of hardships and the public interest.** The court weighs the plaintiff's hardship if relief is denied against the defendant's (and the public's) hardship if it is granted, and may deny relief if the defendant's hardship is greatly disproportionate. But a defendant that acted willfully or with knowledge of the plaintiff's rights gets little or no benefit from the balancing (R2d § 364; *Hirshfield v. Schwartz* (Cal. App. 2001)).
- *QuickGrocer's hardship.* It invested $1.4 million, much of it in refrigeration, and hired 45 local workers. An injunction forces it to operate a different kind of store and lose much of that investment.
- *Verde's hardship.* Without relief, Verde likely closes, its 15-year bargain for exclusivity is destroyed, and the harm cannot be measured.
- *Public interest.* QuickGrocer argues that shoppers would pay about 10% less, and that jobs are at stake. Verde responds that the public also has an interest in enforcing recorded land covenants, and that the injunction still allows QuickGrocer to operate and employ people as a general-merchandise store.
- *Knowledge and willfulness.* This factor is decisive. QuickGrocer's title report listed the recorded memorandum, and its lawyer flagged the "possible conflict" and decided to "proceed" because Harbor Point promised indemnity. QuickGrocer knowingly took the risk. Its $1.4 million investment was made with notice of Verde's right, so its hardship carries little weight. Harbor Point, which granted the exclusive and then leased in violation of it, has no equitable claim to hardship at all.
- *Tailoring.* The court did not shut QuickGrocer down. It allowed up to 15% of the floor for food, a middle ground that protects Verde's exclusive while preserving most of QuickGrocer's operation and jobs.

The balance favors the injunction.

**Conclusion:** subject to the defenses below, Verde was entitled to the permanent injunction the court entered.

### 2. Equitable defenses

**A. Laches.** Laches bars equitable relief when the plaintiff unreasonably delayed after it knew or should have known of its claim, and the delay prejudiced the defendant. Delay alone is not enough.
- *Knowledge.* Sofia knew of the threatened breach in February 2025, when the banner went up.
- *Delay.* She waited seven months, until a week before opening, while QuickGrocer spent $1.4 million and hired staff. QuickGrocer argues that a prompt suit would have stopped it before it spent the money.
- *Was the delay unreasonable?* Sofia objected immediately in writing, and Harbor Point's manager reassured her that QuickGrocer would "mostly sell household goods." She could reasonably wait to see what QuickGrocer actually did. She sued before the store opened, so before the breach was complete.
- *Prejudice.* QuickGrocer's spending was not caused by Sofia's silence. It knew of the recorded exclusive and chose to proceed under an indemnity. Prejudice that a defendant knowingly brings on itself does not support laches. Harbor Point cannot claim prejudice from a delay that its own false reassurance caused.

Laches likely **fails**. Even if it applied, it would bar only equitable relief: Verde's damages claim, filed well within the limitations period, would survive (*Petrella*).

**B. Unclean hands.** A plaintiff who has acted inequitably in the same transaction that is the subject of the suit is barred from equitable relief. The misconduct must relate directly to the matter in litigation; unrelated wrongdoing is irrelevant.
- *Defendants' argument.* Verde has been selling beer and wine for years in breach of the same lease, so it comes to equity with unclean hands while asking the court to enforce that lease.
- *Verde's response.* The alcohol clause is a different covenant, protecting different interests, and has nothing to do with whether Harbor Point may lease to a competing grocer. Verde's breach did not harm QuickGrocer or affect the exclusive at all. Moreover, Harbor Point learned of the sales in 2024 and never objected, which is strong evidence of waiver or acquiescence, and it hardly shows the kind of inequitable conduct that offends the court's conscience.

Because the misconduct is not directly related to the exclusive-use covenant, **unclean hands likely fails**. Harbor Point's remedy for any alcohol breach is its own claim under the lease.

**C. Equitable estoppel.** A party is estopped from asserting a right when, by words, conduct or silence when it had a duty to speak, it led another to believe certain facts, intending or expecting reliance, and the other, ignorant of the truth, reasonably relied to its detriment. QuickGrocer might argue that Sofia's seven months of silence led it to believe Verde would not enforce. The argument fails on two elements. Sofia made no representation to QuickGrocer, and she promptly objected to Harbor Point. And QuickGrocer was not ignorant of the truth: it knew of the recorded exclusive and expressly evaluated the risk. **No estoppel.**

### 3. The contempt motion

**A. Elements and the kinds of contempt.** Disobedience of an equitable decree is punishable by contempt. Contempt requires a valid, clear order, the contemnor's knowledge of it, the ability to comply, and a willful violation. **Civil contempt** is remedial: coercive sanctions (such as a daily fine until the contemnor complies, so that the contemnor "holds the keys") or compensatory payments to the injured party. **Criminal contempt** punishes completed disobedience with a fixed fine or jail term and requires criminal procedural protections, including proof beyond a reasonable doubt (*Bagwell*).
- *Valid, clear order; knowledge.* The injunction states a precise 15% floor-space limit and was personally served on QuickGrocer and Dale.
- *Ability and willful violation.* QuickGrocer controls its own floor layout and could reduce food displays to 15%. It is at 30%, and it brought in FarmFresh to sell produce inside its store. The CEO's statement that the order is "wrong" confirms that the violation is deliberate.

**Relief requested.**
- *(a) $2,000 a day until compliance.* This is coercive civil contempt. QuickGrocer can end the fine at any time by complying, so under *Bagwell* ordinary civil procedures would satisfy federal due process. In a California court, however, a contempt fine may not exceed $1,000 for each act of contempt (CCP § 1218(a)), so the court cannot simply order $2,000 a day. It may fine QuickGrocer up to $1,000 for each violation it finds, order it to pay Verde's attorney's fees and costs of the contempt proceeding (§ 1218(a)), and repeat the process while the violation continues. California treats contempt as quasi-criminal: punitive contempt sanctions require proof beyond a reasonable doubt (*Mitchell v. Superior Court* (1989) 49 Cal.3d 1230), and practitioners apply that standard to contempt generally [S-1803] [S-1804], so Verde should be prepared to meet it. **Grant coercive relief within those limits.**
- *(b) Verde's lost profits.* A compensatory civil-contempt award reimburses the injured party for losses caused by the violation. Verde must prove those losses, which its own accountant found difficult to project. Alternatively, Verde can recover them as damages in the underlying action.
- *(c) $100,000 for violations to date.* A fixed fine for past disobedience is punitive, so it is criminal contempt. It may be imposed only after a separate proceeding with criminal protections, including notice and proof beyond a reasonable doubt; for serious penalties, a jury may be required (*Bagwell*). In California, punishment for contempt is also limited by statute, generally to a fine of up to $1,000 and up to five days in jail for each act of contempt (CCP § 1218), so a $100,000 fine would have to rest on many separate violations.

**B. The pending appeal and the collateral bar rule.** QuickGrocer's view that the injunction is "wrong and on appeal" is no defense. Under the collateral bar rule, a party that violates an injunction issued by a court with jurisdiction may not defend the contempt charge by arguing that the injunction was erroneous; it must obey it and seek review by appeal, a stay or a motion to modify (*Walker v. City of Birmingham*). The rule does not apply to an order that is transparently invalid or void. California courts permit a challenge to a void order, including an unconstitutional one, in contempt proceedings (*In re Berry* (1968) 68 Cal.2d 137) [S-1219], but nothing suggests this order, entered after a full trial by a court with jurisdiction, is void. QuickGrocer appealed but did not seek a stay, so the injunction remained in force.

**C. Who is bound: Dale and FarmFresh.** An injunction binds the parties; their officers, agents, servants, employees and attorneys; and other persons in active concert or participation with them who receive actual notice of the order (FRCP 65(d)(2) states the same rule for federal courts).
- *Dale.* He is QuickGrocer's employee and store manager, was personally served, and arranged the FarmFresh sublease. He is bound and may be held in contempt for aiding the violation.
- *FarmFresh.* FarmFresh was not a party, but it is in active concert with QuickGrocer. It operates inside QuickGrocer's store under a sublease designed to put produce sales back on QuickGrocer's floor. And it received actual notice, because Dale handed it the injunction. QuickGrocer's claim that "FarmFresh is a separate business" does not help: a party cannot evade an injunction by doing through an associated person what it is forbidden to do itself. The FarmFresh floor space should also be counted toward QuickGrocer's 15% limit, since it is part of QuickGrocer's sales floor.

**Conclusion:** the court should find QuickGrocer and Dale in contempt, impose coercive sanctions within § 1218's per-act limits, order compliance (including removal of FarmFresh's produce operation from the store), and award proven compensatory losses or leave them to the damages claim. It may hold FarmFresh in contempt for acting in concert with notice. The $100,000 punitive fine requires a separate criminal-contempt proceeding and, in California, must fit within § 1218's per-act limits.

**Common mistakes**
1. Weighing QuickGrocer's $1.4 million investment as if it were innocent. A defendant that proceeds with notice of the plaintiff's right gets little benefit from balancing.
2. Finding laches from delay alone. The defendant must show prejudice caused by the delay, and QuickGrocer's spending was a knowing risk.
3. Applying unclean hands to any breach of the same lease. The misconduct must relate directly to the right being enforced.
4. Imposing a fixed punitive fine in a civil proceeding. A fixed fine for past disobedience is criminal contempt.
5. Assuming non-parties are never bound. A person in active concert with a party who has actual notice is bound.
