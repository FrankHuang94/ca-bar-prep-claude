## Facts

Harriet Sloane, a retired music teacher, died in 2024, domiciled in Mendocino County, California. She had no children. Her valid will, signed in 2020, names Pacific Coast Bank as executor and trustee and provides:

> **Article 2.** $1,000,000 to my trustee, to be held forever as the Sloane Music Fund, the income to pay summer-camp tuition at the Redwood Youth Orchestra for children of low-income families in Mendocino County. Music gave a poor girl from Fort Bragg a life; I want other poor children here to have that chance.
>
> **Article 3.** $60,000 to my trustee to keep my 1957 Thunderbird in running condition and to drive it in the Fort Bragg Fourth of July parade every year, forever, in memory of my late husband, Ray.
>
> **Article 4.** $400,000 to my trustee for the care of my horse, Juniper, and my cats, Pip and Moss, for the rest of their lives. My neighbor Wes Hollis shall keep the animals and may enforce this gift. When the last of them dies, any money left shall go to Mendocino Animal Rescue.
>
> **Article 5.** Everything else to my niece, Cora.

The will says nothing about what should happen if any of these purposes cannot be carried out. Harriet played violin with the Redwood Youth Orchestra as a teenager and served on its board for 30 years.

**The Music Fund.** The Bank paid camp tuition for 41 children in 2024 and 2025. In January 2026 the Orchestra dissolved for lack of funding, and its camp will not reopen. The Bank has petitioned the court for instructions. It proposes to pay the income to the Coast Youth Soccer League, a nonprofit that runs free sports camps for children throughout Northern California. The Mendocino County Office of Education asks that the income instead fund its after-school program that gives free instrument lessons to low-income students in county schools. Cora claims the $1,000,000 for herself. Lina Marsh, whose son attended the camp on a Fund scholarship in 2025, has filed a separate petition asking the court to order the Bank to keep paying for her son's music lessons.

**The Thunderbird.** Cora says Article 3 is invalid because "nobody benefits from it." The Bank is willing to carry it out and has spent about $4,000 a year on maintenance and parade entry. The parade draws about 10,000 spectators each year.

**The animals.** Juniper is 20 years old; the cats are six and eight. A veterinarian estimates that caring for all three for the rest of their expected lives will cost about $150,000. Wes has cared for them since Harriet's death. Cora argues that $400,000 is "absurd" and that she should receive the excess now.

**The cottage.** In 2015 Harriet paid the entire $300,000 price of a beach cottage on Cove Road from her own savings and had the seller deed it to her nephew, Owen, her sister's son. At the closing she told the escrow officer, "Owen will hold title for me. I'm abroad half the year, and he can sign papers if something comes up." Harriet paid the property taxes and insurance, spent every summer at the cottage and kept her furniture there. Owen, who lives 300 miles away, stayed there twice. Owen says that at a 2018 family dinner Harriet told him, "The cottage will be yours someday." Nothing about the cottage was ever put in writing. Owen refuses to convey the cottage to Harriet's estate, arguing that it was a gift and that any claim to land must be in writing.

## Call of the Question

Answer according to California law.

1. What should the court do with the Music Fund, and does Lina have standing to enforce it?
2. Are the gifts in Articles 3 and 4 valid, and what should happen to the money in each?
3. Does Harriet's estate have a claim to the Cove Road cottage?

## Model Answer

### 1. The Music Fund

**A. A valid charitable trust.** A charitable trust is a trust for a charitable purpose, such as the relief of poverty, the advancement of education, or another purpose beneficial to the community, that benefits the public or an indefinite class rather than specific individuals. It needs no ascertainable beneficiary, may last indefinitely, and is enforced by the Attorney General (Restatement (Third) of Trusts; Gov. Code § 12598).

Harriet transferred $1,000,000 to a trustee with clear directions, so intent, property and trustee are present. The purpose, music education for children of low-income families, combines the relief of poverty with the advancement of education. The beneficiaries are an indefinite and changing class (low-income children in a county), not named individuals. "Held forever" is permissible because the rule against perpetuities does not limit a charitable trust's duration. **Article 2 created a valid charitable trust.**

**B. Standing; Lina's petition.** The Attorney General has primary responsibility for enforcing charitable trusts. Co-trustees and persons with a special interest may also sue, but members of the public who might benefit may not (*Holt v. College of Osteopathic Physicians & Surgeons*). The trustee may petition the court for instructions on internal trust affairs (Prob. Code § 17200), as the Bank has done.

Lina will argue that her son was an actual recipient and so has a special interest. But the trust benefits a shifting class; no child was entitled to continuing payments, and her son's past scholarship gives him no greater stake than any other low-income child in the county. **Lina lacks standing.** Her concerns may be presented through the Attorney General, who should be given notice of and joined in the Bank's petition.

**C. Cy pres**

*Rule.* When a charitable trust's specific purpose becomes impossible, impracticable or illegal, and the settlor had a general charitable intent rather than an intent limited to that particular purpose, the court will direct the property to a charitable purpose as near as possible to the original. Without a general charitable intent, the trust fails, and a resulting trust returns the property to the settlor's estate (*Estate of Gatlin*). The Restatement (Third) presumes a general charitable intent, but California courts still look for it in the instrument and circumstances.

*1. Impossibility.* The Orchestra dissolved and its camp will not reopen. Paying "summer-camp tuition at the Redwood Youth Orchestra" is no longer possible. This is not mere inconvenience.

*2. General or specific intent?*
- *Cora (specific intent).* Harriet named one institution, the Orchestra she played in as a teenager and served as a board member for 30 years. Her gift was meant to support **that** orchestra and its camp, and when it ceased to exist the purpose ended. Cora takes the property as residuary beneficiary.
- *The Bank and the Attorney General (general intent).* Harriet's own words explain her purpose: "Music gave a poor girl from Fort Bragg a life; I want other poor children here to have that chance." The stated goal is music for poor children in her county; the Orchestra was the means she knew. The will contains no gift over if the Orchestra failed; a gift over is the usual sign of a specific intent, so its absence points toward a general one. And she directed the fund to be held "forever," which shows she expected it to outlast any particular institution.
- *Likely result.* The reasons clause is the best evidence of intent, and it speaks of children and music, not of the institution. **Harriet had a general charitable intent.** Cy pres applies, and Cora's claim fails.

*3. A purpose as near as possible.*
- The *soccer league* serves children, but its purpose is sports, not music, and it operates across Northern California rather than in Mendocino County. It is a charitable purpose, but not a near one.
- The *County Office of Education's program* provides free music instruction to low-income students in Mendocino County. It matches all three elements of Harriet's purpose: music, poor children, her county. It offers lessons rather than a summer camp, but that is the kind of variation cy pres permits.

**The court should direct the income to the county music program**, perhaps with instructions to use part of it for summer music programs. Lina's son may benefit as a member of the class, but not by court order in his favor.

*Alternative.* If the court found only a specific intent, the trust would fail and the trustee would hold the fund on a resulting trust for Harriet's estate. Because Article 2 is a testamentary gift, the property would fall into the residue and pass to Cora (Prob. Code § 21111).

### 2. Articles 3 and 4

**A. The Thunderbird (Article 3)**

*Not a private trust.* A private trust needs an ascertainable beneficiary (Prob. Code § 15205). No person benefits from keeping the car running, so Article 3 cannot stand as a private trust. That is Cora's point.

*Charitable?* The Bank will argue that the car appears in a public civic parade watched by 10,000 people, which benefits the community. But the purpose is to maintain one person's car "in memory of my late husband, Ray." That is a private memorial; any public enjoyment of the parade is incidental. **Article 3 is likely not charitable.**

*Honorary (purpose) trust.* California validates a trust for a lawful noncharitable purpose with no ascertainable beneficiary: the trustee may carry it out for **21 years**, whether or not anyone can enforce it (Prob. Code § 15211). Keeping a car running and driving it in a parade is lawful, and the Bank is willing to perform. Cora's objection that "nobody benefits" is precisely the situation the statute addresses. **Article 3 is valid as an honorary trust, but only for 21 years**, not "forever."

*What happens to the money.* At about $4,000 a year, the $60,000 may last about 15 years. Any balance remaining when the 21 years end, or earlier if the purpose ends, is held on a resulting trust for Harriet's estate. Because this is a testamentary gift, it falls into the residue and passes to **Cora**.

**B. The animals (Article 4)**

*Validity.* A trust for the care of domestic or pet animals is valid. It may be enforced by a person named in the instrument or, if none, by a person appointed by the court; unless the instrument provides otherwise, it lasts until no animal covered by the trust remains alive; and its property may be used only for its intended purpose (Prob. Code § 15212). Juniper, Pip and Moss are pet animals, Harriet named Wes as enforcer, and the trust ends when the last of them dies. **Article 4 is a valid pet trust**, and the Bank must apply the funds only to the animals' care.

*The excess.* The veterinarian estimates $150,000 of lifetime costs against $400,000 of funding. Under § 15212, the court may reduce the amount held if it substantially exceeds what the intended use requires.
- *Cora* argues that the excess, about $250,000, should come to her now as residuary beneficiary.
- *Mendocino Animal Rescue* argues that Harriet expressly disposed of "any money left" over from the animals' care, so any excess belongs to it, not to the residue.
- *Likely result.* A court may well reduce the trust, because $400,000 is far more than the animals need. Harriet's will, however, already says where unneeded animal money goes, and a court should follow the instrument. **Any excess the court releases, and whatever remains at the end, should pass to the Rescue.** Cora takes nothing from Article 4.

### 3. The Cove Road cottage

**A. Purchase-money resulting trust**

*Rule.* When one person pays the purchase price of property and title is taken in another's name, the titleholder is presumed to hold the property on a resulting trust for the payor, unless the payor intended a gift or a loan. When title is taken in the name of the payor's spouse, child or other natural object of bounty, a gift is presumed instead. Both presumptions are rebuttable by evidence of the payor's intent at the time of the purchase.

*Application.*
- *Presumption.* Harriet paid the entire $300,000; title went to Owen. A resulting trust for Harriet is presumed.
- *Owen's argument.* He is Harriet's nephew; she had no children, so he was among her closest relatives and a natural object of her bounty, and a gift should be presumed. He also points to her 2018 statement, "The cottage will be yours someday."
- *The estate's response.* A nephew is not within the traditional family-gift presumption (spouse and children) unless the payor stood in a parental role, which the facts do not show. More important, the evidence of Harriet's intent **at the time of purchase** rebuts any gift: she told the escrow officer that Owen would "hold title for me" for convenience while she was abroad. Her conduct afterward was an owner's conduct: she paid the taxes and insurance, spent every summer there and kept her furniture there, while Owen visited twice. The 2018 statement points the same way: "will be yours **someday**" describes a future gift Harriet expected to make, which confirms that the cottage was still hers. An unexecuted promise of a future gift does not transfer land.
- *Conclusion.* Whichever presumption applies, the evidence shows Harriet did not intend a present gift. **Owen holds the cottage on a resulting trust for Harriet's estate.**

**B. The statute of frauds**

Owen argues that a trust of land must be in writing. A trust in real property is generally enforceable only if proved by a writing signed by the trustee or by a conveyance signed by the settlor, **but not when the trust arises by operation of law** (Prob. Code § 15206). A purchase-money resulting trust is implied by law from the payment of the price, so no writing is needed. Owen's argument fails.

**C. Recovery and who takes**

The executor may petition for an order that Owen convey the cottage to the estate (Prob. Code § 850). Nothing suggests Owen acted in bad faith, so double damages under § 859 are unlikely. Once recovered, the cottage is estate property that passes under Article 5 to **Cora**, Harriet's residuary beneficiary. Owen takes nothing because Harriet never made the promised future gift.

**Common mistakes**
1. Letting a charitable trust fail on impossibility without testing general charitable intent and cy pres.
2. Giving Lina standing because her son benefited; members of the benefited public cannot enforce a charitable trust.
3. Treating Article 3 as charitable because spectators enjoy the parade, or as valid forever; it is an honorary trust limited to 21 years.
4. Sending failed testamentary trust property to Harriet's "heirs" by intestacy rather than into the residue.
5. Applying the statute of frauds to a purchase-money resulting trust.
