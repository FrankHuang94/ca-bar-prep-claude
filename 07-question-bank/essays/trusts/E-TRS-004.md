## Facts

Walter Lindqvist created a revocable trust and died in 2021, when it became irrevocable. The trust directs the trustee to pay all net income to Walter's daughter, Greta, for her life and, on her death, to distribute the principal to Greta's children, Leif (now 26) and Sigrid (now 21). It names Walter's nephew, Erik, a commercial real estate broker, as trustee, at an annual fee of 1% of trust assets. It provides: "My trustee may retain shares of Lindqvist Lumber Co. for as long as my trustee considers it advisable, and shall not be liable for failing to diversify by reason of such retention." It says nothing else about investments or the trustee's liability.

At Walter's death the trust held $2,100,000 of publicly traded Lindqvist Lumber stock (70% of the trust), 400 acres of timberland worth about $600,000, and $300,000 in cash. Erik never reviewed the lumber holding. From 2022 to 2025 the lumber industry declined, analysts repeatedly downgraded the stock, and the company closed two of its three mills. The shares are now worth $700,000, and Greta's income has fallen by two-thirds. In 2023 Greta wrote to Erik asking him to "look at the lumber stock." He replied, "Uncle Walter loved that company. The trust says I can keep it."

**The Coastal loan.** In March 2023 Erik caused the trust to lend its $300,000 of cash, unsecured, at 2% interest, to Coastal Brokerage Partners LLC, a firm in which Erik owns a 50% interest, to open a new office. Coastal deposited the money in its operating account, which then held $40,000 of Coastal's own funds. Coastal used $120,000 from the account to buy an office condominium, titled in its name and now worth $170,000, and spent the rest on salaries and rent. The account's balance fell to $5,000 in July 2023; it now holds $60,000 from later commissions. Coastal has repaid nothing and owes its other creditors $500,000.

**The timberland.** In 2024 Erik sold the 400 acres, without an appraisal or any listing, to Pine Ridge Development LLC, owned by his friend Mitch, for $600,000. A month earlier the county had rezoned the land for residential use, and it was then worth about $1,000,000. Mitch knew of the rezoning, as did Erik. A week after the sale Mitch paid Erik a $50,000 "consulting fee." In 2025 Pine Ridge sold 100 of the acres for $400,000 to Ana and Paul Arden, who paid market value and knew nothing about the trust. Pine Ridge still owns the other 300 acres.

**Information.** Erik sent Greta an annual account for 2023 listing "Note receivable: $300,000," without identifying the borrower or his interest in it. Greta signed and returned a form "approving the 2023 account." In 2025 Leif asked Erik for a copy of the trust and "a list of what the trust owns." Erik replied, "You're only a remainder beneficiary. You'll get information when your mother dies."

## Call of the Question

Answer according to California law.

1. What breaches of trust, if any, has Erik committed?
2. What remedies do Greta, Leif and Sigrid have against Erik, and what is the effect of Greta's approval of the 2023 account?
3. What rights, if any, do the beneficiaries have against Coastal, Pine Ridge and the Ardens?

## Model Answer

### Preliminary: Erik's duties

The trust became irrevocable at Walter's death in 2021, so Erik's duties now run to the beneficiaries: Greta as income beneficiary and Leif and Sigrid as remainder beneficiaries. A trustee must administer the trust according to its terms and the law (Prob. Code § 16000), with the care, skill and caution of a prudent person acting in a like capacity (§ 16040). A trustee with special skills must use them (§ 16014); Erik is a commercial real estate broker, so he is held to a broker's skill in dealing with the timberland.

### 1. Erik's breaches

**A. Lindqvist Lumber: prudent investing and review**

*Rule.* A trustee must invest and manage trust assets as a prudent investor would, judging each decision in the context of the portfolio as a whole (§ 16047), must diversify unless it is prudent not to (§ 16048), and must review the assets within a reasonable time after accepting the trust and decide whether to retain them (§ 16049). Compliance is judged by the facts at the time of the decision, not by hindsight (§ 16051). The instrument may expand or restrict these duties, and a trustee who reasonably relies on its terms is protected (§ 16046). But a discretionary power must be exercised reasonably and in good faith, not ignored (§ 16080).

*Erik's argument.* Walter expressly allowed him to retain the lumber stock and excused him from liability "for failing to diversify by reason of such retention." The later decline is hindsight.

*The beneficiaries' argument.* The clause allows retention "for as long as my trustee **considers it advisable**." That requires Erik actually to consider the holding. He "never reviewed" it, ignored three years of downgrades and two mill closures, and answered Greta's 2023 request with sentiment ("Uncle Walter loved that company"), not judgment. The clause protects a decision to retain; it does not excuse a failure to decide. Exculpatory language is construed strictly, and it cannot relieve a trustee of liability for reckless indifference to the beneficiaries' interests (§ 16461). This is not hindsight: the warning signs were public while Erik did nothing, and Greta's income collapsed.

*Conclusion.* The clause likely shields an informed decision to keep the stock, but not Erik's complete failure to review and exercise discretion, at least after the warning signs and Greta's 2023 letter. **Erik likely breached his duties to review and to exercise his discretion prudently.**

**B. The Coastal loan: self-dealing**

*Rule.* A trustee must administer the trust solely in the beneficiaries' interest (§ 16002) and must not use or deal with trust property for his own profit or take part in any transaction in which he has an interest adverse to the beneficiaries (§ 16004(a)). Such a transaction is a breach regardless of fairness or good faith (the "no further inquiry" rule) unless the instrument authorizes it, the beneficiaries give informed consent, or a court approves.

*Application.* Erik caused the trust to lend its entire cash reserve to a firm he half owns. He stood on both sides of the loan. The trust does not authorize it, no beneficiary consented, and no court approved. Erik may say the loan was a sound business decision, but fairness is irrelevant to self-dealing. In any event the terms were not fair: an unsecured loan at 2% to fund a new office, which no prudent investor would make with 10% of a trust that must produce income for Greta (§ 16047). **Breach of loyalty, and independently of prudence.**

**C. The timberland sale**

*Loyalty.* A trustee may not profit personally from a transaction involving trust property. A week after selling the land to his friend's company for $600,000, Erik received a $50,000 "consulting fee" from the buyer. That is a secret profit from the sale of trust property and gave Erik an interest adverse to the beneficiaries, who needed the highest price (§§ 16002, 16004(a)). Erik may call the fee unrelated, but its timing and source make that implausible.

*Care.* A broker selling land would obtain an appraisal and market the property. Erik did neither, and he sold land worth $1,000,000 after a rezoning he knew about for $600,000. That is far below the standard of a prudent trustee, let alone one with a broker's skills (§§ 16040, 16014).

**Breach of loyalty and of the duty of care.**

**D. Duties to inform and account**

*Rule.* The trustee must keep the beneficiaries reasonably informed of the trust and its administration (§ 16060), must on reasonable request report information about the trust's assets, liabilities, receipts and disbursements relevant to the beneficiary's interest (§ 16061), and must give a copy of the terms of an irrevocable trust to a beneficiary who requests it (§ 16061.5). The trustee must account at least annually to each beneficiary to whom income or principal is required or authorized to be currently distributed (§ 16062).

*Leif.* The trust is irrevocable, and Leif is a beneficiary. He was entitled on request to a copy of the trust and to reasonable information about its assets. Erik's refusal ("you're only a remainder beneficiary") is a **breach**. Leif was not entitled to annual accounts, which go to current beneficiaries, but that does not justify refusing his request.

*Greta.* As income beneficiary she is entitled to annual accounts. Erik's 2023 account listed a "note receivable" without naming the borrower or disclosing his own interest in it. An account that hides the trustee's conflict is not a true and complete account, and it compounds the loyalty breach. **Breach.**

### 2. Remedies against Erik; Greta's approval

**A. Remedies available.** For a breach, a beneficiary may petition the court to compel the trustee to redress it by paying money or restoring property, set aside the trustee's acts, impose a constructive trust or equitable lien and trace trust property, remove the trustee, and reduce or deny his compensation (§§ 16420, 17200). The trustee is chargeable with the loss caused by the breach with interest, or the profit he made, or the profit the trust would have made (§ 16440(a)).

*Surcharge, item by item.*
- **Coastal loan:** the $300,000 lost, with interest, less whatever is recovered from Coastal (Call 3). Erik is liable even if the loan seemed sensible to him.
- **Timberland:** the $400,000 difference between the land's $1,000,000 value and the $600,000 price, with interest, unless the land is recovered (Call 3). Erik must also disgorge the **$50,000** payment, which he holds on a constructive trust for the trust.
- **Lumber stock:** the loss attributable to Erik's failure to review and act once a prudent trustee would have done so, measured by comparing the actual result with what a prudently managed portfolio would have earned for both income and principal.

*Removal and fees.* Self-dealing, a secret profit and refusals to inform are grounds for removal (§ 15642). The court should remove Erik, appoint a successor, and deny or reduce his 1% fee for the period of breach.

*Excuse.* The court may excuse a trustee who acted reasonably and in good faith (§ 16440(b)). That is unavailable to a trustee who profited from his own breaches.

**B. Greta's approval of the 2023 account.** A beneficiary who consents to, releases or affirms a trustee's conduct cannot later hold the trustee liable, **unless** the beneficiary did not know the material facts or her rights, or the consent was induced by the trustee's improper conduct (§§ 16463–16465). The account concealed both the borrower's identity and Erik's interest, the very facts that made the loan a breach. Moreover, a transaction in which the trustee obtains an advantage from a beneficiary, such as a release, is presumed to violate the trustee's duties (§ 16004(c)). **Greta's approval is ineffective**, and it never bound Leif or Sigrid, who signed nothing.

*Limitations.* A claim must be brought within three years after the beneficiary receives an account or report that adequately discloses it; otherwise within three years of discovery (§ 16460). The 2023 account did not adequately disclose the claim, so the period runs from discovery; the claims are timely in any event.

### 3. Rights against third parties

**A. Coastal**

*Not a protected transferee.* A third person who deals with a trustee in good faith and for value, without **actual knowledge** that the trustee is exceeding or improperly exercising his powers, is protected (§ 18100). Coastal received the funds through Erik, its 50% owner, who knew the loan was a breach; his knowledge is Coastal's. Coastal is not protected and holds the traced trust funds on a constructive trust.

*Tracing.* The beneficiaries may follow trust funds into their product and impose a constructive trust or equitable lien until the property reaches a bona fide purchaser (§ 16420; Civ. Code § 2224).
- *The condominium.* When the condominium was bought, the account held $340,000, of which $300,000 was trust money. A wrongdoer is presumed to spend its own money first, but where it buys an asset and then dissipates the rest of the account, the claimant may elect to trace into the asset (R-REM-048). Courts differ on how far that election reaches, so state the alternatives: (1) *Whole asset.* The trust's $300,000 exceeded the $120,000 price and Coastal then spent the rest of the account, so the beneficiaries may treat the whole price as trust money and impose a **constructive trust on the entire condominium**, capturing its rise in value to $170,000. (2) *Proportional share.* A court that splits a mixed purchase pro rata would treat 300/340 (about 88%) of the price as trust money, giving the beneficiaries about 88% of the condominium (about $150,000) or an equitable lien in that proportion. (3) *Floor.* Even if Coastal's own $40,000 went into the purchase first, at least $80,000 of the price was trust money, supporting a two-thirds share or a lien for $80,000. Because Coastal is not an innocent party, a court is likely to resolve the election in the beneficiaries' favor and award the whole condominium.
- *The account.* The beneficiaries' claim to the account is capped at its **lowest intermediate balance** after the deposit, $5,000. Later deposits of Coastal's commissions do not restore trust funds, so they cannot claim the full $60,000.

*Priority.* Property held on a constructive trust is not Coastal's own, so the beneficiaries take the traced condominium and $5,000 ahead of Coastal's $500,000 of general creditors. For the rest of the $300,000 they have only an unsecured claim against Coastal, competing with those creditors, plus the surcharge claim against Erik.

**B. Pine Ridge**

A third person who knowingly participates in a trustee's breach is liable for the resulting loss and holds any trust property received on a constructive trust. Pine Ridge, through Mitch, knew of the rezoning, bought without competition at 60% of value, and paid the trustee a personal "consulting fee" a week later. That is **actual knowledge** of the breach, so § 18100 gives no protection. The beneficiaries may:
- recover the **300 acres** Pine Ridge still holds by constructive trust, crediting Pine Ridge with the proportional part of the price it paid (about $450,000);
- recover Pine Ridge's **profit on the 100 acres** it resold: $400,000 received less the $150,000 allocable price, or $250,000, by constructive trust on the proceeds; or
- elect damages instead, holding Pine Ridge and Erik liable for the $400,000 shortfall.

If Pine Ridge acted in bad faith in taking trust property, the court may also award twice the value of the property recovered and attorney's fees (§§ 850, 859).

**C. The Ardens**

The Ardens did not deal with the trustee, so § 18100 does not govern their position. They bought 100 acres from Pine Ridge, which held the land on a constructive trust, and a constructive trust cannot be enforced against a bona fide purchaser: one who acquires legal title for value, in good faith and without actual, constructive or inquiry notice of the beneficiaries' claim (Restatement (Third) of Restitution § 66 [VERIFY V-350]). The Ardens paid market value and knew nothing about the trust or the breach. The beneficiaries might argue that a recorded deed from Erik as trustee put them on inquiry, but nothing in the record revealed that the price or the side payment made the sale a breach. **The Ardens take free of the trust and keep the 100 acres**; the beneficiaries pursue Pine Ridge's resale proceeds and Erik instead.

### Conclusion

Erik breached his duties of loyalty (Coastal loan; the $50,000 payment), care and prudence (timberland; failure to review the lumber stock) and his duties to inform and account. He should be surcharged for the losses with interest, made to disgorge the $50,000, removed and denied fees; Greta's approval does not bar any claim. The beneficiaries may trace into Coastal's condominium and the $5,000 balance ahead of its creditors, recover the 300 acres and the resale profit from Pine Ridge, but not the Ardens' land.

**Common mistakes**
1. Treating the retention clause as permission to ignore the holding. It authorizes a judgment to retain; it does not excuse failing to make one.
2. Asking whether the Coastal loan was "fair." Self-dealing is a breach without further inquiry.
3. Telling Leif he has no rights until Greta dies. A remainder beneficiary of an irrevocable trust is entitled to the terms and to reasonable information on request.
4. Letting the beneficiaries claim the whole $60,000 in Coastal's account; later deposits do not restore trust funds.
5. Protecting the Ardens under § 18100. They did not deal with the trustee; they keep the land because they bought from Pine Ridge for value, in good faith and without notice, and the bona fide purchaser test, unlike § 18100, also asks about constructive and inquiry notice.

**Accuracy notes.** Section numbers for the duties (§§ 16000, 16014, 16040, 16046–16051, 16060–16062) follow the rulebook, which flags several as model knowledge [VERIFY V-569]; the substance is standard. The § 16461 limits on exculpatory clauses and the §§ 16463–16465 consent rules are stated as in R-TRS-084 and R-TRS-085. The tracing rules follow R-REM-048 (Restatement (Third) of Restitution) [VERIFY V-350]; courts differ on whether a claimant whose funds were mixed with the wrongdoer's own money may claim the entire asset bought from the mixed account or only a proportional share, so the answer states both.
