## Facts

In 2016, Helen Park, a California resident, signed a document titled "Helen Park Living Trust." It named Helen as trustee and her son, Kevin, as successor trustee. The same day, Helen deeded her Pasadena duplex and her Big Bear lake house to herself "as trustee of the Helen Park Living Trust" and retitled her $1.5 million investment portfolio, then held in a mix of stock-index funds and bonds, in the trust's name. The trust provides that during Helen's life the trustee will pay her the income and any principal she requests, and that at her death "the trust estate shall be distributed outright in equal shares to my children, Kevin and Grace."

Article 9 of the trust states: "This trust may be amended by a writing signed by me and acknowledged before a notary public." The trust says nothing about whether it is revocable. Article 11 states: "No trustee shall be liable for any act or omission except for willful misconduct."

In 2020, Helen had a mild stroke. Her doctors described her as "mentally sharp," but she found managing finances tiring, so she resigned as trustee, and Kevin accepted the trusteeship in writing.

In 2021, Helen typed and signed a one-page document titled "Amendment to the Helen Park Living Trust," which read: "My Big Bear lake house shall go to my daughter, Grace, alone, in addition to her half of the rest of the trust." The document was not notarized. Helen mailed it to Kevin, who received it and put it in his trust file. He told his wife that he "didn't think it counted without a notary."

Helen died in March 2024. After her death, Kevin did the following as trustee:

- In May 2024, he bought the duplex from the trust for himself for $800,000, the value stated in a recent independent appraisal. He did not tell Grace or seek court approval. In December 2024, he sold the duplex to an unrelated buyer for $950,000.
- In June 2024, he sold most of the index funds and bonds and put $1.2 million of the $1.5 million portfolio into shares of NovaGene, a biotechnology company, because a college friend who works there told him approval of its new drug was "a sure thing." The drug was rejected in early 2025, and the NovaGene shares lost 60% of their value ($720,000).
- He deposited $12,000 of duplex rents, collected before the sale, into his personal checking account "for convenience," and later paid some trust expenses from that account.
- He has ignored three written requests from Grace for information about the trust's assets, telling her, "You'll get your half when I'm done."
- He has told Grace that he plans to divide the lake house equally between them.

It is now 2026. Grace has come to you for advice.

## Call of the Question

Answer according to California law.

1. Is Helen's 2021 amendment valid?
2. What duties, if any, has Kevin breached as trustee?
3. What remedies does Grace have?

## Model Answer

California's Probate Code governs. Where California departs from the general rule of trust law, both are noted.

### 1. Is the 2021 amendment valid?

**A. A valid revocable trust.** A trust requires a settlor with capacity who manifests the intent to create a trust, trust property, a trustee, and an ascertainable beneficiary (Prob. Code §§ 15200–15205). Helen signed a trust instrument, deeded the duplex and lake house to herself as trustee, and retitled the portfolio, so the trust was funded. There is no merger even though Helen was initially both trustee and lifetime beneficiary, because Kevin and Grace hold the remainder. The trust is valid.

*Revocable?* Under the traditional common-law rule, a trust is irrevocable unless the settlor expressly reserves a power to revoke. California reverses this: a trust is **revocable unless the instrument expressly makes it irrevocable** (§ 15400). Helen's trust is silent, and she was a California resident who signed it here. It was revocable, and therefore amendable, until her death.

**B. Did Helen validly amend it?**

*Rule.* Unless the instrument provides otherwise, a settlor may modify a revocable trust by the procedure for revocation (§ 15402). A trust may be revoked (1) by any method stated in the instrument, or (2) by a writing, other than a will, signed by the settlor and delivered to the trustee during the settlor's lifetime, unless the instrument **explicitly** makes its own method exclusive (§ 15401(a)). In *Haggerty v. Thornton* (2024), the California Supreme Court held that a settlor may amend by the statutory method unless the trust's amendment method is explicitly exclusive, disapproving earlier cases that required strict compliance with a stated amendment method.

*Instrument method not followed.* Article 9 contemplates a writing signed by Helen and acknowledged before a notary. Helen's 2021 amendment was signed but not notarized, so it does not satisfy Article 9.

*Is Article 9 explicitly exclusive?* Article 9 says the trust "may be amended" by a notarized writing. It does not say "only," "exclusively" or anything similar. It is permissive language describing one available method, not an explicit statement that no other method may be used.

*Statutory method.*
- *Writing signed by the settlor:* Helen typed and signed the amendment.
- *Not a will:* it is a trust amendment, not a testamentary instrument.
- *Delivered to the trustee during the settlor's lifetime:* Helen mailed it to Kevin, who was then the trustee, and he received it in 2021, three years before her death.

*Kevin's arguments.* Kevin will argue that Helen chose notarization to guard against fraud and hasty changes, so requiring it respects her intent, and that before 2024 some California courts required strict compliance with a trust's own amendment method. However, *Haggerty* rejected that line of cases: a settlor who wants to forbid other methods must say so explicitly, and Helen did not. Kevin's private belief that the amendment "didn't count" is legally irrelevant; as trustee he received it, and receipt is what the statute requires.

*Capacity.* A settlor must have capacity when amending a trust. Kevin might point to Helen's stroke. But her doctors described her as "mentally sharp," and the amendment is a simple one-sentence gift of a single asset she plainly understood. Capacity is not seriously in doubt.

**Conclusion.** The 2021 amendment is **valid**. Grace takes the lake house outright and half of the rest of the trust estate.

### 2. Kevin's breaches of duty

**To whom duties run.** While a trust is revocable, the trustee's duties are owed to the settlor (§ 15800). When Helen died in March 2024, the trust became irrevocable, and Kevin's duties ran to Kevin and Grace as beneficiaries. All of the conduct below occurred after Helen's death, so Kevin owed these duties to Grace.

**A. Duty of loyalty: buying the duplex (self-dealing).** A trustee must administer the trust solely in the beneficiaries' interest (§ 16002) and must not use or deal with trust property for the trustee's own profit or take part in a transaction in which the trustee has an interest adverse to the beneficiary (§ 16004(a)). Self-dealing, such as buying trust property, is a breach **regardless of fairness or good faith** (the "no further inquiry" rule), unless the instrument authorizes it, the beneficiaries give informed consent, or a court approves.

Kevin bought trust property for himself. He will argue that he paid the full appraised value, so the trust lost nothing. But fairness is no defense to self-dealing, and his resale seven months later at $150,000 more shows exactly the risk the rule guards against. The trust does not authorize purchases by the trustee, Grace did not know of the sale, let alone consent, and Kevin sought no court approval. **Kevin breached the duty of loyalty.**

**B. Duty of prudence: the NovaGene investment.** A trustee must invest and manage trust assets as a prudent investor would, considering the trust's purposes, terms and circumstances, and exercising reasonable care, skill and caution. Each investment is judged in the context of the portfolio as a whole (§ 16047). The trustee must diversify unless, under the circumstances, it is prudent not to (§ 16048). Compliance is judged by the facts at the time of the decision, not by hindsight (§ 16051).

- *Kevin's argument.* The drug's failure is hindsight, and at the time an insider told him approval was "a sure thing."
- *Grace's argument.* Judged entirely as of June 2024, the decision was imprudent. Kevin sold a diversified portfolio of index funds and bonds and put 80% of the trust's investments into one speculative biotech stock whose value turned on a single regulatory decision. He relied on a friend's tip, not on any analysis or professional advice. The trust was about to be distributed outright to two beneficiaries, which called for preserving value, not speculation. Nothing made it prudent not to diversify.

**Kevin breached the prudent investor rule and the duty to diversify.** The loss alone does not prove the breach; the concentration and the absence of investigation do.

**C. Duty to keep trust property separate.** A trustee must keep trust property separate from the trustee's own and identified as trust property (§ 16009). Commingling is a breach even if no loss results, and any resulting loss is charged to the trustee. Kevin put $12,000 of trust rents into his personal account "for convenience." Convenience is no excuse. **Breach.**

**D. Duty to inform and account.** A trustee must keep the beneficiaries reasonably informed and must respond to reasonable requests for information about the trust's assets, liabilities, receipts and disbursements (§§ 16060, 16061). Once the trust is irrevocable, the trustee must also account at least annually to beneficiaries entitled to current distributions (§ 16062). Grace made three written requests, and Kevin refused ("You'll get your half when I'm done"). This duty was not owed to remainder beneficiaries while Helen was alive and the trust was revocable, but all of Grace's requests came after Helen's death. **Breach.**

**E. Duty to administer according to the trust's terms.** A trustee must administer the trust according to its instrument (§ 16000). As amended, the trust gives the lake house to Grace alone. Kevin's stated plan to split it equally would breach the trust's terms; the duty of impartiality (§ 16003) also forbids him to favor his own share. This is at least a **threatened breach** that Grace can enjoin.

### 3. Grace's remedies

A beneficiary may petition the court about the trust's internal affairs (§ 17200) and, for a breach or threatened breach, may compel performance, enjoin the breach, compel redress, remove the trustee, reduce or deny compensation, set aside the trustee's acts, or impose a constructive trust or equitable lien on property traceable to the breach (§ 16420).

**A. The duplex: rescission or profit.** When a trustee self-deals, the beneficiaries may either set the transaction aside or affirm it and hold the trustee liable for his profit. A third person who acts in good faith and for value, without actual knowledge that the trustee is acting improperly, is protected (§ 18100). The December 2024 buyer was unrelated and paid $950,000; nothing suggests it knew of the breach. Rescission against that buyer is therefore unavailable. Instead, Grace should hold Kevin liable for **the profit he made through the breach, $150,000, with interest** (§ 16440(a)(2)). She may also seek a constructive trust on the sale proceeds Kevin still holds. The recovery goes back into the trust and is divided under its terms.

**B. The NovaGene loss: surcharge.** A trustee is chargeable with any loss or depreciation in value of the trust estate resulting from the breach, with interest, or with any profit that would have accrued but for the breach (§ 16440(a)(1), (3)). Kevin is liable for the **$720,000 loss with interest**. Grace could alternatively seek the amount by which the diversified portfolio would have grown, if that is larger.

**C. The commingled rents.** Kevin must restore any of the $12,000 not spent on trust expenses, with interest. Grace can trace the rents into his account and impose an equitable lien or constructive trust on what remains.

**D. Information, accounting and the lake house.** Grace may petition to compel information and an account (§ 17200), to instruct Kevin to distribute the lake house to her under the amendment, and to enjoin any contrary conveyance.

**E. Removal and compensation.** A court may remove a trustee for breach of trust, hostility toward a beneficiary, or other good cause (§ 15642). Kevin's self-dealing, speculation, commingling and stonewalling show he cannot be trusted to finish the administration. Grace should seek his **removal and the appointment of a neutral successor**, and a reduction or denial of his compensation.

**F. Kevin's defense: the exculpatory clause.** Article 11 relieves the trustee of liability "except for willful misconduct." California enforces exculpatory clauses, but not for breaches committed intentionally, with gross negligence, in bad faith or with reckless indifference to the beneficiary's interest, nor for any profit the trustee derived from the breach (§ 16461). Courts construe such clauses strictly.
- *Self-dealing:* the clause cannot shield Kevin's $150,000 profit, and buying trust property for himself was intentional.
- *NovaGene:* Kevin will argue he was at most negligent, which the clause covers. Grace will respond that putting 80% of the trust into one speculative stock on a friend's tip, with no analysis, is gross negligence or reckless indifference, which the clause cannot excuse. On these facts Grace has the stronger argument.
- *Commingling and refusal to inform:* both were deliberate choices, not mere oversights.

A court may also excuse a trustee who acted reasonably and in good faith (§ 16440(b)), but Kevin did not act reasonably.

*Timeliness.* Grace has three years from receiving an adequate account or report disclosing the claim, or otherwise from discovering it (§ 16460). She has received no account, and the breaches began in 2024, so her 2026 claims are timely.

**Conclusion.** The amendment is valid. Kevin breached the duties of loyalty, prudence and diversification, separation of trust property, information and accounting, and adherence to the trust's terms. Grace can recover Kevin's $150,000 profit and the $720,000 investment loss with interest, trace the commingled rents, compel distribution of the lake house to herself, and have Kevin removed. Any amount Kevin owes the trust can be offset against his own half share.

**Common mistakes**
1. Requiring strict compliance with the trust's notarization clause. Under *Haggerty* the stated method blocks the statutory method only if the trust explicitly says it is exclusive.
2. Forgetting California's revocability default, or stating the traditional rule as California law.
3. Accepting "fair appraised price" as a defense to self-dealing.
4. Treating the NovaGene loss itself as proof of breach (hindsight) instead of analyzing the concentration and the lack of investigation at the time of the decision.
5. Ordering rescission of the duplex sale without addressing the good-faith purchaser, or letting the exculpatory clause cover profits or reckless conduct.
