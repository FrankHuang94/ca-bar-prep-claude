## Facts

In 2019, Walter Okafor, a California resident, transferred $2 million to First Coast Trust Company as trustee of the irrevocable Okafor Family Trust. The trust instrument provides:

> **Article 3.** The trustee shall pay all net income to my son, Andre, at least quarterly, for his life.
> **Article 4.** The trustee may, in its sole and absolute discretion, distribute principal to Andre for his health, education and support, and may also distribute principal to me, Walter, if the trustee determines that I need it.
> **Article 5.** On Andre's death, the trustee shall distribute the remaining principal to Andre's then-living children.
> **Article 6.** No beneficiary may assign, anticipate or encumber any interest in income or principal, and no such interest shall be subject to the claims of any beneficiary's creditors before actual receipt by the beneficiary.

Until 2025, the trust's diversified portfolio produced about $80,000 of income a year, all of which First Coast paid to Andre.

Also in 2019, Walter gave his daughter, Lina, a check for $100,000 with a letter that said: "I hope you will use some of this to help your brother if he ever falls on hard times." Lina used all of the money for a down payment on her own home.

In 2023, Andre and his wife, Bianca, divorced. The court ordered Andre to pay Bianca $2,000 a month in child support for their daughter. Andre is $60,000 behind, and Bianca has a judgment for the arrears. In 2024, Pacific Card Services obtained a $40,000 judgment against Andre for unpaid credit-card charges. Also in 2024, Andre signed a document assigning to Valley Motors "all trust income payable to me in 2025" in exchange for a new truck.

In early 2025, Andre needed hip surgery costing $80,000 that his insurance did not cover. He asked First Coast for a principal distribution. First Coast refused in writing: "Our priority is to preserve principal for the remainder beneficiaries. Your father's grandchildren deserve the largest possible inheritance." Later in 2025, First Coast sold the entire portfolio and bought long-term zero-coupon bonds, which pay no current interest but are expected to roughly double in value by 2045. Andre's income from the trust has fallen almost to zero.

In 2025, Walter's business failed, and Delta Supply obtained a $500,000 judgment against him. Walter has few other assets. Delta Supply seeks to collect from the Okafor Family Trust.

## Call of the Question

Answer according to California law.

1. Did Walter create a trust for Andre's benefit when he gave Lina $100,000?
2. Can Bianca, Pacific Card Services and Valley Motors reach Andre's interest in the Okafor Family Trust?
3. Can Delta Supply reach the assets of the Okafor Family Trust?
4. Has First Coast breached any duty owed to Andre?

## Model Answer

California's Probate Code governs. Where California departs from the general rule, both are stated.

### 1. The $100,000 to Lina: trust or gift?

A trust requires the settlor's manifestation of a present intent to impose enforceable duties on a trustee to hold property for another (Prob. Code § 15201). No particular words are required, but **precatory** words such as "wish," "hope" or "request" ordinarily create no trust unless the context shows the settlor meant to impose a duty. If no duty was intended, the transferee takes an outright gift.

- *Andre's argument.* Walter named a specific beneficiary (Andre) and a specific purpose (help in hard times), and he was a father worried about a son with money problems. The letter accompanied the check, so it was part of the transfer.
- *Lina's argument.* Walter wrote "I hope," the classic precatory phrase. He asked her to use "some" of the money, so neither the amount supposedly held in trust nor the trigger ("if he ever falls on hard times") is defined. And the check was made out to Lina personally with no restriction. All of this indicates a gift to Lina accompanied by a hope, not a duty.

Indefinite words, an indefinite amount and a gift-like transfer all point the same way. **No trust was created.** Lina took an outright gift and was free to spend it on her home, and Andre has no claim against her.

### 2. Andre's creditors and assignee

**A. Andre's interest and the spendthrift clause.** Andre has two kinds of interest: a **mandatory** right to all net income (Article 3), and a **discretionary** interest in principal limited by a support standard ("health, education and support") (Article 4). A trust instrument may validly provide that a beneficiary's interest in income or principal is not subject to voluntary or involuntary transfer; then the beneficiary cannot assign the interest, and creditors cannot reach it before it is paid to the beneficiary, subject to statutory exceptions (§§ 15300, 15301(a)). Article 6 restrains both assignment and creditors' claims as to income and principal. It is a valid spendthrift clause. Andre did not create the trust, so the self-settled rule does not affect his interest.

**B. Valley Motors (voluntary assignment).** A valid spendthrift clause bars a beneficiary's voluntary transfer of his interest. Andre's assignment of his 2025 income is exactly what Article 6 forbids ("No beneficiary may assign, anticipate or encumber"). Valley Motors cannot compel First Coast to pay it the income. Once income is actually paid to Andre, however, it is his own property, and Valley Motors, like any creditor, can pursue it, along with its contract remedies against Andre for the truck. **Valley Motors cannot reach the trust interest itself.**

**C. Bianca (child support).** Notwithstanding a spendthrift clause, a beneficiary's interest may be reached to satisfy a judgment for support of the beneficiary's child, spouse or former spouse. The court may order the trustee to pay the support from income or principal that is payable to the beneficiary, or that the trustee in its discretion has decided to pay, as is equitable (§ 15305). General trust law likewise treats support claimants as exception creditors.
- *Income.* Andre's income is mandatory and therefore payable. The court can order First Coast to pay Bianca the $60,000 arrears and the ongoing $2,000 a month from Andre's income.
- *Principal.* Principal is payable only when First Coast exercises its discretion to distribute it. If First Coast decides to distribute principal (for example, for Andre's surgery), the court could order part of it applied to support, as is equitable, considering Andre's medical needs.
- *Problem.* First Coast's switch to zero-coupon bonds has cut Andre's income to nearly nothing, so there is little to reach. That is one reason the investment change matters (see Part 4).

**Bianca can reach Andre's income interest, and principal once First Coast decides to pay it, to satisfy the support judgment.**

**D. Pacific Card Services (general creditor).**
- *General rule.* Under the general rule, a valid spendthrift clause bars ordinary creditors entirely until funds reach the beneficiary.
- *California.* California, however, allows a general judgment creditor to obtain an order directing the trustee to pay the creditor up to **25 percent** of each payment that would otherwise be made to the beneficiary, after the court considers the support needs of the beneficiary and his dependents (§ 15306.5). An order reaching amounts that **exceed what is needed for his education and support** (§ 15307) does not add to that figure: under *Carmack v. Reynolds* (2017), orders under §§ 15306.5 and 15307 together are capped at 25 percent of each payment. (Principal already due but still held by the trustee could be reached in full, § 15301(b), but none is due here.)
- *Application.* Andre's mandatory income is a "payment that would otherwise be made" to him. If the income returns to about $80,000 a year, Pacific could seek up to $20,000 a year. The court must first consider Andre's own support needs and his child-support obligation, which reduce what is available, so the actual order may be smaller. Pacific is unlikely to show that Andre's income exceeds his support needs.
- *Discretionary principal.* A creditor cannot compel the trustee to pay any amount that may be paid only in the trustee's discretion (§ 15303). Pacific cannot force a principal distribution.

**Pacific likely can obtain an order for a limited percentage of Andre's income payments, but not principal. Under the general rule, it would get nothing.**

### 3. Delta Supply and Walter's interest

**Self-settled trust rule.** If the settlor is also a beneficiary, a restraint on transfer of the settlor's interest is invalid against the settlor's creditors. If the trustee has discretion to pay income or principal to or for the settlor, the settlor's creditors may reach **the maximum amount the trustee could pay** to or for the settlor under the trust's terms, not exceeding the settlor's proportionate contribution to the trust (§ 15304). The general rule is the same: a settlor cannot shield assets from his own creditors in a trust for his own benefit.

**Application.** Walter is the settlor, and Article 4 makes him a discretionary beneficiary of principal ("may also distribute principal to me, Walter, if the trustee determines that I need it").
- *First Coast's argument.* The trust is irrevocable; Walter is only a contingent, discretionary beneficiary; his right depends on First Coast's finding of "need"; and paying his creditors would defeat Andre's income interest and the grandchildren's remainder, which Walter gave away in good faith in 2019, years before his business failed.
- *Delta's argument.* The statute looks to what the trustee *could* pay, not what it is likely to pay. Under Article 4, First Coast could distribute any amount of principal, up to all of it, to Walter. The spendthrift clause is ineffective as to him.

The statute favors Delta. First Coast could pay Walter the entire principal, and Walter contributed all of it, so the contribution cap does not limit Delta. **Delta can likely reach trust principal up to its $500,000 judgment.** (If Delta could also show that the 2019 transfer was made to hinder creditors or left Walter insolvent, it might attack the transfer itself as fraudulent; the facts suggest neither, so the self-settled rule is Delta's route.) Because the trust is irrevocable, the rule for settlors' creditors reaching *revocable* trusts (§ 18200) does not apply.

### 4. First Coast's duties to Andre

**A. Refusing principal for Andre's surgery: abuse of discretion.** A discretionary power is not left to the trustee's arbitrary discretion; it must be exercised reasonably, in good faith, and in accordance with the trust's terms and purposes (§ 16080). Even "sole," "absolute" or "uncontrolled" discretion does not permit bad faith or disregard of the trust's purposes (§ 16081). A court will not substitute its judgment for a reasonable exercise of discretion, but it will intervene to prevent an abuse.
- *First Coast's argument.* Article 4 gives it "sole and absolute discretion," and preserving principal for the remainder beneficiaries is a legitimate consideration.
- *Andre's argument.* Article 4 sets a standard: principal for Andre's "health, education and support." Uninsured hip surgery is a health need squarely within that standard. First Coast's letter shows it did not apply the standard at all; it substituted its own preference ("the grandchildren deserve the largest possible inheritance"). Walter, however, made Andre the primary beneficiary and gave the grandchildren only what remains at Andre's death. Refusing a core health expense in order to maximize the remainder disregards the trust's purpose.

The refusal rested on a consideration that contradicts the trust's stated standard, not on a judgment about Andre's needs. **First Coast likely abused its discretion.** A court can direct a reasonable distribution for the surgery.

**B. Moving to zero-coupon bonds: impartiality and prudence.** When a trust has two or more beneficiaries, the trustee must act impartially in investing and managing the trust property, taking into account their differing interests, notably balancing income for the life beneficiary against growth for the remainder beneficiaries (§ 16003). The trustee must also invest as a prudent investor, considering the trust's purposes, terms and **distribution requirements** (§ 16047).
- *First Coast's argument.* Long-term bonds are safe, and the trust is meant to last for Andre's lifetime.
- *Andre's argument.* The trust requires that all net income be paid to Andre at least quarterly. An entire portfolio of zero-coupon bonds is designed to produce no income, transferring all of the return to the remaindermen. Combined with the refusal letter, the move shows First Coast deliberately favoring the grandchildren over Andre, the opposite of impartiality. It also ignores the trust's distribution requirements, which the prudent investor rule requires the trustee to consider.

**First Coast breached the duty of impartiality and the prudent investor rule.**

**C. Andre's remedies.** Andre may petition the court (§ 17200) to compel a distribution for his surgery, to instruct First Coast to rebalance the portfolio so that it produces reasonable income, and to surcharge First Coast for the income lost since the change (§ 16440(a)). He may also seek First Coast's removal for breach of trust (§ 15642). Restored income would also benefit Bianca, whose support claim depends on Andre's income.

**Common mistakes**
1. Finding a trust in "I hope you will use some of this." Precatory words with no definite amount create no trust.
2. Stating only the general rule for Pacific Card (no recovery) and missing California's 25% and excess-over-support provisions, or stating only California's rule without the general rule.
3. Letting a creditor compel a discretionary principal distribution.
4. Forgetting that the spendthrift clause is ineffective against the settlor's own creditors when the settlor is a beneficiary.
5. Accepting "sole and absolute discretion" as ending the inquiry, or missing that zero-coupon bonds defeat the mandatory income interest.
