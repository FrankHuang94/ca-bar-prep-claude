# E-PRP-003 — The Mill Street Building

## Facts

In 2019 Owen bought the Mill Street Building, a two-story commercial building in the town of Westport, for $800,000. He paid $200,000 in cash and borrowed $600,000 from First Bank, giving First Bank a mortgage on the building to secure his note. First Bank recorded its mortgage the same day Owen's deed was recorded.

In 2022 Owen borrowed $150,000 from his brother-in-law, Carl. Owen signed a note and gave Carl a mortgage on the building, which Carl put in his desk drawer.

In April 2023 Owen applied to Delta Credit Union for a $200,000 loan. On the application, under "Other debts," Owen wrote: "Family loan—$150,000—unsecured." Delta's title search on April 28 showed only First Bank's mortgage. On May 1, Delta made the loan and took a mortgage on the building, which it recorded on May 10. Meanwhile, Carl, who had heard that Owen was borrowing more money, recorded his mortgage on May 3. Delta's mortgage provides that if the borrower misses any payment, the entire debt becomes due at the lender's option.

The jurisdiction's recording statute provides: "Every conveyance of an interest in real property, including a mortgage, that is not recorded is void as against any subsequent purchaser or mortgagee who, without notice of it, acquires an interest in the same property for value."

In 2024 Owen sold the building to Pia. Pia paid Owen $120,000 in cash. The deed, which both Owen and Pia signed and which was recorded, states: "Grantee takes subject to the mortgage held by First Bank, which Grantee does not assume, and Grantee expressly assumes and agrees to pay the debt secured by the mortgage held by Delta Credit Union." The deed does not mention Carl's mortgage, although Pia's title report listed all three mortgages. Neither First Bank nor Delta agreed to release Owen.

In 2026, after the building lost its main tenant, Pia stopped making payments on both the First Bank and Delta loans. Delta declared the entire balance due and brought a judicial foreclosure action, naming Pia, Owen and Carl as defendants. It did not name First Bank. At the foreclosure sale in September 2026, Quinn bought the building for $160,000. The costs of the sale were $10,000.

At the time of the sale the unpaid balances were: First Bank, $500,000; Delta, $190,000; Carl, $150,000. The building's fair market value, free of all mortgages, is about $700,000.

## Call of the Question

1. As between Carl and Delta, whose mortgage has priority? Discuss.
2. What are Owen's and Pia's obligations on each of the three loans? Discuss.
3. What interest did Quinn acquire, how should the sale proceeds be distributed, and what further claims do First Bank, Delta and Carl have? Discuss.

## Model Answer

### 1. Priority between Carl and Delta

**A. First in time, as adjusted by the recording act.** Interests in the same land generally rank in the order they were created, but the recording act lets a later purchaser or mortgagee who qualifies under it defeat an earlier unrecorded interest. An unrecorded mortgage is still valid between the parties. Carl's mortgage (2022) came before Delta's (May 1, 2023), so Carl wins unless the statute protects Delta.

The type of statute is read from its language. A **race** statute protects whoever records first; a **notice** statute protects a later purchaser for value without notice, whether or not it records; a **race-notice** statute protects such a purchaser only if it also records first. This statute protects any subsequent mortgagee "who, without notice of it, acquires an interest... for value." It contains no requirement that the later taker record first, so **it is a notice statute.** The relevant moment is when Delta acquired its interest and paid value: May 1, 2023.

**B. Is Delta a mortgagee for value without notice?**

- *Mortgagee for value.* Delta advanced $200,000 of new money on May 1 in exchange for its mortgage. That is value; this was not security for an existing debt.
- *Actual notice.* Nothing shows that Delta actually knew of Carl's mortgage.
- *Record (constructive) notice.* Carl recorded on May 3, two days after Delta took its mortgage. Delta's April 28 search, the last before closing, showed only First Bank. Delta had no record notice when it paid value.
- *Inquiry notice.* A purchaser is charged with what a reasonable inquiry would reveal when facts would lead a prudent person to investigate. Carl will argue that Owen's application disclosed a $150,000 family loan, a large debt that any prudent lender would ask about, and that asking would have revealed the mortgage. Delta will respond that the borrower expressly described the loan as "unsecured" in the application, the record was clean, and nothing about the property (such as someone else in possession) hinted at an unrecorded interest. A lender may rely on a clean record and the borrower's own written statement; it was Owen's misstatement, not Delta's carelessness, that hid the mortgage.

On balance, **Delta likely had no inquiry notice and is a mortgagee for value without notice.**

**C. Result and contrasts.** Under this notice statute, Carl's mortgage was unrecorded when Delta acquired its interest, so it is void as against Delta. Carl's recording two days later does not help him, and Delta's own later recording (May 10) does not matter, because a notice statute does not require the later taker to record first. **Delta's mortgage has priority over Carl's.**

The result would differ under the other types:
- *Race-notice.* California's statute is race-notice (Civ. Code § 1214): it protects a later good-faith mortgagee for value only if its mortgage is "first duly recorded." Carl recorded on May 3, before Delta's May 10 recording, so **in California Carl would prevail.**
- *Race.* Carl recorded first, so he would also prevail.

Either way, Carl's mortgage remains valid against Owen.

**D. First Bank.** First Bank's 2019 mortgage secured the loan that paid for the building, so it is a purchase-money mortgage. It was created first and recorded immediately. **First Bank is first in priority**; under the notice statute, the order is First Bank, then Delta, then Carl.

### 2. Owen's and Pia's obligations

**A. Owen's continuing liability.** A mortgagor may sell mortgaged land, and the mortgages remain on the land. The sale does not release the original borrower from his notes unless the lender agrees to a release (a novation). Neither First Bank nor Delta released Owen, and Carl was not asked. **Owen remains personally liable on all three notes.**

**B. First Bank: Pia took "subject to."** A buyer who takes subject to a mortgage is not personally liable on the debt; the lender may look to the land and to the original borrower. The deed says Pia takes subject to First Bank's mortgage, "which Grantee does not assume." **Pia has no personal liability to First Bank**; she risks only losing the building. First Bank may foreclose on the land and may sue Owen on his note.

**C. Delta: Pia assumed.** A buyer who expressly assumes a mortgage debt becomes personally liable as the primary obligor, and the original mortgagor remains liable as a surety unless the lender releases him.

- *Pia's liability.* Pia signed a deed in which she "expressly assumes and agrees to pay" the Delta debt. Her promise was made to Owen, but its purpose was to pay Owen's debt to Delta, so Delta is an intended (creditor) beneficiary and may sue Pia directly.
- *Owen's position.* Owen is now a surety: Delta may still collect from him, but as between Owen and Pia, Pia must pay. If Owen pays, he is subrogated to Delta's rights against Pia and the security, and he may obtain reimbursement from Pia.

**Pia and Owen are both liable to Delta, with Pia primarily liable.**

**D. Carl.** The deed says nothing about Carl's mortgage, but it was recorded in May 2023, before Pia bought in 2024, and her title report listed it. Pia therefore had record and actual notice and **took subject to Carl's mortgage.** She never promised to pay it, so she has no personal liability to Carl. Owen alone is personally liable on Carl's note.

### 3. The foreclosure

**A. Default, acceleration and foreclosure.** On default the mortgagee may foreclose. With an acceleration clause it may declare the entire debt due; without one it may foreclose only for the missed installments. Pia missed payments, and Delta's mortgage lets it accelerate the whole balance on any missed payment, so Delta properly declared $190,000 due. Judicial foreclosure is available in every state.

The necessary parties are the current owner (Pia), anyone against whom a deficiency is sought (Owen and Pia), and junior lienholders (Carl). Delta joined all of them. A senior lienholder such as First Bank need not be joined, because its interest is not affected. Before the sale, Pia, Owen or Carl could have redeemed by paying the full accelerated debt, but none did.

**B. Effect of the sale; Quinn's title.** Foreclosure extinguishes the foreclosing mortgage and every junior interest whose holder was properly joined. The buyer takes subject to all senior interests, which are unaffected.

- *Senior interest.* **Quinn owns the building subject to First Bank's $500,000 mortgage.** Quinn did not assume that debt, so he is not personally liable, but First Bank may foreclose on the building if the loan is not paid.
- *Junior interests.* Delta's mortgage is satisfied out of the sale and extinguished. Carl, a joined junior, loses his lien.
- *Statutory redemption.* About half the states let the mortgagor redeem for a fixed period after the sale by paying the sale price plus costs and interest. In California, when the decree permits a deficiency, the property is sold subject to a right of redemption that lasts one year where, as here, the proceeds do not satisfy the debt (CCP § 729.030). Quinn's title would then be subject to Pia's right to redeem for that year.

**C. Distribution of the proceeds.** Proceeds go first to the costs of sale, then to the foreclosing mortgagee's debt, then to junior interests in order of priority, with any surplus to the mortgagor/owner. Senior lienholders are not paid from the proceeds.

| Step | Amount | Remaining |
|---|---|---|
| Sale price | $160,000 | $160,000 |
| Costs of sale | $10,000 | $150,000 |
| Delta (debt $190,000) | $150,000 | $0 |
| Carl (junior) | $0 | — |
| Pia (owner) | $0 | — |

First Bank receives nothing from the proceeds; its lien remains on the building.

**D. Delta's deficiency.** Delta is short $40,000. If the proceeds do not satisfy the debt, the mortgagee may obtain a personal deficiency judgment against those personally liable, unless a statute limits it. **Delta may obtain a $40,000 deficiency against Pia (the assuming grantee) and Owen (the original mortgagor, as surety).** If Owen pays, he may recover from Pia by subrogation.

In California, the one-action rule is satisfied because Delta foreclosed on its security first. After a judicial sale, however, the deficiency is limited to the amount by which the debt, with interest and the costs of sale and of the action, exceeds the fair value of the real property or interest sold, and it may never exceed the debt minus the sale price (CCP § 726(b)). Delta sold the building subject to First Bank's $500,000 senior mortgage, so the interest sold is likely valued at the building's value less that debt, about $200,000 ($700,000 − $500,000) [VERIFY V-830]. That roughly equals Delta's $190,000 debt plus the $10,000 costs of sale. In California, Delta's deficiency therefore lies between zero and $40,000 and is likely little or none; how a court values a junior lienholder's interest at the fair-value hearing is the open point. Had Delta used a nonjudicial trustee's sale, no deficiency would be allowed (CCP § 580d).

**E. Carl and First Bank.**
- *Carl.* His lien is gone, but his debt is not. He may sue Owen personally on the $150,000 note. He has no claim against Pia, who never assumed it, or against Quinn or the building.
- *First Bank.* Pia's default lets First Bank foreclose on the building, now owned by Quinn, subject to any acceleration terms in its mortgage. It may sue Owen personally for any deficiency, but not Pia (she took subject to) or Quinn.

**F. If Carl were senior to Delta** (under a race-notice or race statute, as in California). Delta's foreclosure could not affect Carl's senior mortgage, so Quinn would take subject to both First Bank's and Carl's mortgages, and Quinn would presumably have bid less. The net proceeds would go to Delta, and Carl would keep his lien and could foreclose on the building himself and sue Owen on the note.

**Common mistakes**
1. Applying race-notice because California uses it. The facts quote a notice statute; apply it, then note California's different result.
2. Testing notice at the time Delta recorded (May 10) instead of when it paid value (May 1).
3. Paying the senior mortgage (First Bank) out of a junior's foreclosure sale.
4. Holding Pia personally liable on Carl's or First Bank's loan.
5. Forgetting that the original mortgagor stays liable after an assumption, as a surety.
