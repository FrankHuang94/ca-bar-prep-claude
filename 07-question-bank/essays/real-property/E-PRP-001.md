# E-PRP-001 — Lou, Tara and Sam (assignment, habitability, constructive eviction, mitigation)

## Facts

On January 1, 2025, Lou leased a three-bedroom house to Tara for a term of three years ending December 31, 2027, at a rent of $2,500 per month. The written lease, signed by both, provided: "Tenant shall not assign this lease without Landlord's prior written consent," and "Landlord shall keep the furnace, plumbing and electrical systems in good repair." The lease said nothing about subleasing.

In March 2026, Tara accepted a job in another state. Without contacting Lou, she signed a document with her friend Sam titled "Sublease." It provided that Tara "transfers to Sam all of Tara's rights in the premises for the remainder of the lease term," and that Sam "will pay the monthly rent of $2,500 directly to Lou." The document did not say that Sam assumed Tara's obligations under the lease. Sam moved in on April 1, 2026. From April through November 2026, Sam mailed Lou a personal check each month. Lou knew Tara had moved away and that Sam was living in the house, and he cashed every check without comment.

In late November 2026 the furnace stopped working during a cold spell. On December 1, Sam gave Lou written notice. Lou replied that he would "get to it after the holidays." Night temperatures fell near freezing. Sam bought space heaters, closed off two bedrooms, and slept in the living room; in mid-December a pipe in a closed-off bedroom froze and burst, flooding the hallway carpet. Sam paid no rent for December or January, telling Lou in writing that he would pay "when the house has heat." Lou never repaired the furnace. On February 1, 2027, Sam moved out, returned the keys, and wrote to Lou: "This house has been unlivable for two months. I'm done."

Lou did nothing to re-rent the house until June 1, 2027, when he listed it with a rental agent. A new tenant moved in on August 1, 2027, under a one-year lease at $2,300 per month.

Lou has now sued Tara and Sam for $20,000 (unpaid rent of $2,500 per month for December 2026 through July 2027) plus $1,000 (a shortfall of $200 per month for August through December 2027). Tara says she has had nothing to do with the house since March 2026. Sam says he owes nothing.

## Call of the Question

1. Was Tara's transfer to Sam an assignment or a sublease, and did it breach the lease? Discuss.
2. What are the rights and liabilities of Lou, Tara and Sam with respect to the rent Lou claims? Discuss.

## Model Answer

### Call 1 — Assignment or sublease, and breach

**Assignment vs. sublease.** An assignment transfers the tenant's entire remaining interest in the leasehold; a sublease transfers less than the whole remaining term, so the original tenant keeps a reversion. Under the majority (objective) approach, the label the parties use does not control; the question is whether the tenant kept any part of the term. A minority of courts look to the parties' intent, and some treat a retained right of re-entry as making the transfer a sublease.

Here, the document is titled "Sublease," which Tara will cite as evidence that the parties intended a sublease. But it transfers "all of Tara's rights in the premises for the remainder of the lease term." Tara kept no reversion, no right of re-entry and no period at the end of the term. The document also has Sam pay rent "directly to Lou" rather than to Tara, which is consistent with Sam stepping into Tara's shoes. Even under the intent approach, the substance of the deal shows that the parties meant Sam to take over the entire tenancy.

**Conclusion:** The transfer was an assignment.

**Breach of the anti-assignment clause.** Absent a lease restriction, a tenant may freely transfer. Restrictions on transfer are strictly construed against the landlord: a clause barring assignment does not bar subleasing, and vice versa. Lou's clause forbids assignment "without Landlord's prior written consent" and says nothing about subleasing. Had the transfer been a sublease, Tara would not have breached the clause; because it was an assignment, the clause applies. Tara never asked for consent.

As to the consent standard, under the traditional rule a landlord whose consent is required may refuse for any reason; under the modern trend (followed in California for commercial leases, *Kendall v. Ernest Pestana, Inc.*), where the lease requires consent but states no standard, consent may not be unreasonably withheld; whether California extends that rule to a residential lease like this one is unsettled. Lou might well have had a reasonable basis to evaluate Sam's creditworthiness, but the standard does not matter here because Tara never asked.

**Conclusion:** Tara breached the anti-assignment clause.

**Waiver.** A landlord who, knowing of a prohibited transfer, accepts rent from the transferee waives the breach. Lou knew Tara had moved away and that Sam was living in the house, and he cashed Sam's personal checks for eight months without objection. Lou may argue that he simply accepted money owed under the lease, but accepting rent from an unauthorized assignee with knowledge of the facts is the classic act of waiver.

**Conclusion:** Lou waived the breach. He cannot now terminate the lease or recover damages because of the assignment, and the assignment is effective.

### Call 2 — Rights and liabilities for the rent

#### A. Who is potentially liable

**Sam — privity of estate.** An assignee is in privity of estate with the landlord and is directly liable for rent and for covenants that run with the land while he holds the estate. A non-assuming assignee's liability ends when he reassigns; an assignee who expressly assumes the lease is also in privity of contract and liable for the rest of the term.
- *Assumption?* Lou will argue that Sam's promise to "pay the monthly rent of $2,500 directly to Lou" was an assumption that makes Lou a third-party beneficiary of Sam's promise. Sam will respond that the clause only described how rent would be paid while he lived there, and that the document conspicuously did not say he assumed Tara's obligations. The better reading is that Sam did not assume the lease generally, though the point is arguable.
- *Effect of moving out.* Even without an assumption, privity of estate does not end merely because the assignee moves out; it ends when he reassigns or the landlord accepts a surrender. Sam neither reassigned nor obtained Lou's acceptance of a surrender (Lou never retook the house for his own use or released Sam). So, unless a defense applies, Sam remains liable for rent accruing during the term.
- **Conclusion:** Sam is liable for the rent as an assignee in privity of estate, subject to his defenses below.

**Tara — privity of contract.** After an assignment, the original tenant remains liable to the landlord for the rent by privity of contract for the whole term, as a surety, unless the landlord releases her by a novation. Tara's belief that she has had "nothing to do with the house" is no defense. Lou's acceptance of rent from Sam did not release her; a novation requires the landlord's agreement to substitute the new tenant and release the old one, and Lou never agreed to that.
- **Conclusion:** Tara is liable for any rent Sam owes, with the same defenses as the tenant in possession. Lou may sue both, but may recover only once.

#### B. Defenses — the furnace failure

**Implied warranty of habitability.** In a residential lease the landlord impliedly warrants that the premises are fit for human habitation and will be maintained so during the term; the warranty cannot be waived (*Javins*; in California, *Green v. Superior Court*). A breach requires a condition that substantially affects health or safety, notice to the landlord, and a reasonable time to repair, where the tenant did not cause the condition.
- *Breach.* This is a residential lease. A house without heat during near-freezing nights, forcing the occupant to close off rooms and sleep in the living room, is a serious health and safety condition, and the burst pipe made it worse.
- *Notice and time.* Sam gave written notice on December 1. Lou promised to fix it "after the holidays" and never did. Two months without heat in winter exceeds any reasonable repair time.
- *Cause.* Nothing suggests Sam caused the furnace failure. Lou may argue that Sam's decision to close off rooms caused the pipe to freeze, but that goes only to the flood damage, not to the lack of heat.
- *Remedies.* A tenant may remain and withhold or reduce rent, repair and deduct, sue for damages measured by the difference between the agreed rent and the fair rental value of the premises in their defective condition, or terminate. Sam stayed, withheld rent and told Lou he would pay when the house had heat. The rent for December and January is reduced to the fair rental value of a house without heat in winter, which is far below $2,500 and may be close to nothing given the conditions. (California's version also allows repair and deduct up to one month's rent, but Sam did not use that remedy.)
- **Conclusion:** Lou breached the warranty, and the December and January rent is substantially abated. Tara, as surety, gets the same reduction.

**Express covenant to repair — does it run to Sam?** A lease covenant binds and benefits assignees if the original parties intended it to run, it touches and concerns the land, and there is privity of estate. Lou's covenant to keep the furnace in repair directly affects the use and enjoyment of the premises, so it touches and concerns the land, and Sam, as assignee, is in privity of estate with Lou. Sam can therefore enforce Lou's express repair covenant, which reinforces his claim that Lou breached a duty, independent of the implied warranty.

**Constructive eviction.** A constructive eviction occurs when the landlord's act or breach of a legal duty substantially interferes with the tenant's use and enjoyment, the tenant gives notice and the landlord fails to cure within a reasonable time, and the tenant vacates within a reasonable time. The tenant's rent obligation then ends.
- *Substantial interference and landlord's duty.* No heat in winter, closed rooms and a burst pipe are a substantial interference, caused by Lou's breach of his express covenant and the implied warranty.
- *Notice and failure to cure.* Written notice on December 1; no repair.
- *Vacating within a reasonable time.* Lou will argue that Sam stayed two months and so waived constructive eviction. Sam will respond that he stayed because Lou promised a repair "after the holidays," that he moved out promptly once it was clear no repair was coming, and that his February 1 letter tied his departure to the conditions. Staying a reasonable time while the landlord promises to cure does not waive the claim. The better view is that Sam left within a reasonable time.
- **Conclusion:** Sam was likely constructively evicted on February 1, 2027. The lease obligation to pay rent ended then, for both Sam (estate) and Tara (contract, whose liability is derivative of the tenant's).

#### C. Alternative — if there was no constructive eviction: abandonment and mitigation

If a court found that Sam waited too long, his departure would be an abandonment: he vacated with the intent not to return and stopped paying. The landlord may then accept a surrender (ending future rent), relet on the tenant's account and recover any deficiency, or, under the traditional rule, leave the premises vacant and sue for rent as it comes due. The modern majority rule, at least for residential leases, requires the landlord to make reasonable efforts to mitigate by reletting.
- *Surrender?* Sam's returning the keys was an offer to surrender, but Lou did not accept it by retaking the house for his own use; he later relet it. So there was no surrender by operation of law, and the lease continued.
- *Mitigation.* Lou did nothing for four months (February through May) and listed the house only on June 1. A landlord who fails to make reasonable efforts recovers only the rent he could not have avoided by reasonable reletting. Here, once Lou listed the house it took two months to find a tenant, which suggests that a reasonable effort starting in February would have produced a tenant by about April 1. Lou's recovery for February through July would then be limited to roughly two months' rent ($5,000), plus $200 a month for April through July if a replacement could have been found only at the $2,300 Lou later obtained (about $5,800 in all), not six months' rent.
- *The $200 shortfall.* When the landlord relets on the tenant's account for less, the tenant owes the difference for the rest of the original term. The $1,000 shortfall (August–December 2027) would be recoverable in this alternative.

#### D. Likely outcome

- **Lou v. Sam and Tara (jointly, one recovery):** December and January rent only, reduced by the habitability breach to the fair rental value of an unheated house, likely a small fraction of $5,000.
- **February–December 2027:** Nothing, because Sam was constructively evicted and the lease ended. If the court rejected constructive eviction, Lou's recovery would be limited by his failure to mitigate (about two months' rent, any shortfall from April, and the $1,000 shortfall for August–December).
- **Sam's counterclaim:** Sam may recover damages for Lou's breach of the warranty and the repair covenant, such as the cost of the space heaters.

**Common mistakes**
1. Following the document's title. The test is whether the tenant transferred the whole remaining term.
2. Releasing Tara because Lou accepted Sam's rent. Only a novation releases the original tenant.
3. Ending Sam's liability when he moved out. Privity of estate ends only on reassignment or an accepted surrender.
4. Treating habitability and constructive eviction as the same doctrine. Habitability lets the tenant stay and pay less; constructive eviction requires leaving and ends the rent obligation.
5. Letting Lou recover rent for months he made no effort to relet, under the modern duty to mitigate.
