# E-PRP-002 — Opal's land (implied easement, covenant, joint tenancy)

## Facts

Until 2016, Opal owned a 30-acre parcel in the foothills. The west 10 acres ("Parcel W") front County Road and contain a farmhouse and a barn. The east 20 acres ("Parcel E") were pasture. A graveled lane runs from County Road across Parcel W to Parcel E; for many years Opal drove her tractor along it about once a week to tend the pasture. Parcel E's eastern edge touches Old Mill Road, a public road, but along that edge Parcel E drops into a steep ravine, and a contractor estimated in 2016 that building a driveway from Old Mill Road would cost about $90,000.

In 2016 Opal sold Parcel E to Dev. The deed said nothing about the lane. It contained this clause: "Grantee, for himself, his heirs and assigns, covenants that Parcel E shall be used only for single-family residential purposes. This covenant runs with the land and benefits Parcel W." Dev recorded the deed, built a house on Parcel E, and used the lane every day to reach it.

Opal died in 2019. Her will devised Parcel W "to my children, Hank and Iris, as joint tenants with right of survivorship." Hank moved into the farmhouse in 2021; Iris lived in another state. Beginning in 2021, Hank rented the barn to a horse-boarding business for $1,500 a month and kept all the rent. When Iris asked for half, Hank replied, "You don't live here, so you don't get anything." Iris never asked to live on Parcel W.

In 2022 Dev sold Parcel E to Kim. Kim's deed did not mention the covenant.

In 2023, without telling Hank, Iris signed and recorded a deed conveying "all of Iris's interest in Parcel W, from Iris as joint tenant, to Iris as tenant in common." Iris died in 2024. Her will left "all of my property" to her son, Jay.

In 2025 Kim began renting her house on Parcel E to vacationers through an online platform, for stays of two to five nights; she now hosts about 15 groups a month, and guest cars have raised traffic on the lane from about 4 to about 20 trips a day. Hank installed a locked gate across the lane at County Road and refused to give Kim a key, telling her that she has no right to use the lane and that her rentals violate the covenant. Kim responds that she has an easement and that renting out her home is a residential use. Jay has asked Hank for "my share" of Parcel W and of the barn rent.

## Call of the Question

1. Does Kim have a right to use the lane, and if so, may her guests use it? Discuss.
2. Can Hank and Jay enforce the residential-use covenant against Kim? Discuss.
3. What interests do Hank and Jay hold in Parcel W, and is Jay entitled to any share of the barn rent? Discuss.

## Model Answer

### Call 1 — Kim's right to use the lane

An easement is a nonpossessory right to use another's land. Kim needs an easement over Parcel W (servient) benefiting Parcel E (dominant).

**Express easement.** An express easement must be created by a writing satisfying the statute of frauds. Opal's deed to Dev said nothing about the lane, so there is no express easement.

**Easement implied from prior use.** An easement is implied when (1) one person owned both parcels, (2) before severance the owner used one part for the benefit of the other, (3) the use was apparent and continuous, and (4) the use was reasonably necessary to the enjoyment of the dominant part at severance.
- *Common ownership and severance.* Opal owned all 30 acres and severed them by the 2016 deed to Dev.
- *Prior use.* For years Opal used the lane across Parcel W to reach and tend the pasture on Parcel E, a use of one part for the benefit of the other.
- *Apparent and continuous.* The lane is graveled and visible on inspection, and it was used regularly (weekly), with a permanent physical roadway. Hank may argue that weekly tractor trips are sporadic, but "continuous" means regular and permanent in character, not constant.
- *Reasonable necessity.* The only alternative is a $90,000 driveway through a ravine. When the easement is implied in favor of the grantee (an implied grant), reasonable necessity is enough; strict necessity is not required. Spending $90,000 to avoid an existing lane makes the lane reasonably necessary. (California codifies the same idea: a transfer carries with it uses of the land that were obvious and permanent at the time, Civ. Code § 1104.)
- **Conclusion:** Dev acquired an easement implied from prior use.

**Easement by necessity.** An easement by necessity arises when common land is divided and one part is left without access. It requires strict necessity at severance. Parcel E touches Old Mill Road, a public road, and access is possible, though expensive. Cost and inconvenience are not strict necessity. **This theory likely fails.**

**Prescription (alternative).** A prescriptive easement requires use that is open and notorious, continuous and adverse for the statutory period (five years in California). Dev used the lane daily from 2016, openly, and Kim may tack Dev's use. But if Dev's use was under an implied easement, it was of right rather than adverse, and Opal and Hank may have permitted it. Prescription is a fallback, not Kim's best theory.

**Transfer and binding effect.** An easement benefiting a parcel is appurtenant, and courts prefer that construction. The benefit passes automatically with the dominant estate even if the deed is silent, so it passed from Dev to Kim. The burden binds later owners of the servient estate who take with notice. Hank took Parcel W by devise, so he is not a purchaser for value entitled to recording-act protection, and the visible graveled lane in daily use would give inquiry notice in any event. **The easement binds Hank.**

**Scope — may Kim's guests use the lane?** The scope of an implied easement is fixed by the prior use and the parties' reasonable expectations at severance, including the normal development of the dominant estate. A change in degree (more of the same use) is generally allowed; a change in kind, or a use that unreasonably increases the burden on the servient estate, is not.
- *Kim's argument.* At severance the parties expected Parcel E to become a residence; the covenant itself contemplates a single-family home. Guests driving to and from a house are using the lane for the same purpose, access to a dwelling. More cars is a change in degree.
- *Hank's argument.* The prior use was weekly tractor trips; Dev's residential use raised it to about four trips a day; Kim's commercial lodging business has raised it to about twenty, by strangers, which is a different kind of use and a much heavier burden on the farmhouse property.
- *Resolution.* Ingress and egress to a dwelling remains the same kind of use, so guest traffic is likely within the easement's scope unless the court finds that 20 trips a day unreasonably burdens Parcel W; the remedy for excessive use would be to limit the excess, not to terminate the easement. (Whether the rentals violate the covenant is a separate question, below.)

**The locked gate.** The servient owner may use his land in any way that does not unreasonably interfere with the easement, and a gate may be reasonable if the easement holder is given a key. A locked gate with no key completely blocks Kim's access. **Kim may obtain an injunction requiring Hank to remove the gate or give her a key.**

### Call 2 — Enforcing the residential-use covenant

Hank and Jay can sue for damages if the promise is a real covenant, or for an injunction if it is an equitable servitude.

**Real covenant — burden on Kim.** The burden runs to a successor of the promisor if there is (1) a writing, (2) intent to bind successors, (3) touch and concern, (4) horizontal privity, (5) vertical privity, and (6) notice.
1. *Writing.* The covenant is in the recorded deed. A grantee who accepts a deed is bound by covenants in it even if he did not sign.
2. *Intent.* "For himself, his heirs and assigns" and "runs with the land" show intent to bind successors.
3. *Touch and concern.* A restriction on the use of Parcel E burdens Kim as a landowner and protects the rural, residential character of Parcel W. Negative use restrictions almost always touch and concern.
4. *Horizontal privity.* Opal and Dev were grantor and grantee in the very conveyance that created the covenant.
5. *Vertical privity.* Dev conveyed his entire fee to Kim.
6. *Notice.* Kim's own deed is silent, but Dev's deed containing the covenant was recorded in Kim's chain of title, so she had record (constructive) notice.

**Benefit running to Hank and Jay.** The benefit runs to the promisee's successors if the parties intended it and it touches and concerns the benefited land; horizontal privity is not required and vertical privity is relaxed (any succession to the estate suffices). The covenant expressly "benefits Parcel W," and Hank and Iris succeeded to Parcel W by devise (and Jay succeeded to Iris's share if she severed the joint tenancy, Call 3). **The real covenant runs; damages are available.** (California's statute also requires that the instrument describe both parcels, state that successors are bound and be recorded, Civ. Code § 1468; Opal's recorded deed names both parcels and says the covenant runs.)

**Equitable servitude.** A promise concerning land use is enforceable in equity against a successor with notice if it is in writing, intended to bind successors, and touches and concerns the land; no privity is required (*Tulk v. Moxhay*). Each element is met for the reasons above, and Kim had record notice. **An injunction is available.**

**Does a short-term rental violate "single-family residential purposes"?** Servitudes are interpreted according to the parties' intent shown by the words and circumstances, and older cases resolve true ambiguity in favor of the free use of land.
- *Kim's argument.* Guests use the house as a home: they sleep, cook and live there. The covenant forbids commercial structures and multi-family buildings, not renting a single-family house. Long-term renting would plainly be residential, and the covenant does not set a minimum rental period. Ambiguity favors free use.
- *Hank and Jay's argument.* "Single-family residential purposes" implies occupancy by a household as its home. Fifteen groups a month, staying two to five nights each, is a transient lodging business that functions like an inn. The increase from 4 to 20 trips a day is exactly the kind of harm a residential restriction on rural land is meant to prevent.
- *Resolution.* Courts are divided on short-term rentals. Given the volume and the commercial, hotel-like turnover, the better view is that Kim's current operation exceeds "single-family residential purposes," although occasional rentals would likely be allowed.

**Who may enforce; defenses; remedy.** Hank, and Jay if the joint tenancy was severed, may enforce as owners of the benefited Parcel W. No defense is strong: the restriction has not been abandoned, conditions in the area have not changed radically, and Hank objected promptly. Kim may argue unclean hands because Hank unlawfully locked her out of the lane; unclean hands must relate to the transaction at issue, and the gate dispute is closely related, so a court might condition relief on Hank's removing the gate. **Hank and Jay can likely obtain an injunction limiting Kim to residential use (damages for a real covenant would be hard to measure).**

### Call 3 — Interests in Parcel W and the barn rent

**Joint tenancy created by the will.** A joint tenancy requires the four unities of time, title, interest and possession, plus clear language of survivorship. Opal's will gave Parcel W to Hank and Iris "as joint tenants with right of survivorship," and both took at the same time (Opal's death), by the same instrument, in equal shares, with equal rights of possession. **Hank and Iris held as joint tenants.**

**Severance by Iris's 2023 deed to herself.** A joint tenant may sever unilaterally by conveying her interest during her life; the transferred interest becomes a tenancy in common.
- *Traditional rule.* At common law a conveyance to oneself was ineffective, because a person could not convey to herself; severance required a conveyance to a third party (a "straw") and a reconveyance. Under this rule, Iris's deed did nothing, the joint tenancy continued, and at Iris's death in 2024 Hank took all of Parcel W by survivorship.
- *Modern trend (and California).* A growing number of states, including California, permit a joint tenant to sever by a deed to herself, because the straw-person requirement serves no purpose (*Riddle v. Harmon* in California). California also requires that the severing deed be recorded before the severing tenant's death (or, if executed and acknowledged within three days before death, recorded within seven days after it) to defeat survivorship (Civ. Code § 683.2); Iris recorded hers in 2023. Secret severance is permitted; Hank's consent or knowledge is not required.
- **Conclusion:** Under the modern rule, which California follows (*Riddle*), Iris's deed severed the joint tenancy, and Hank and Iris became tenants in common, each with an undivided one-half interest.

**Jay's interest.** A tenancy in common interest is devisable. Iris's will left "all of my property" to Jay, so on her death Jay took Iris's undivided one-half interest. **Hank and Jay are now tenants in common, each owning one half.** As a cotenant, Jay may seek partition; courts prefer partition in kind, which may be feasible on 10 acres, though the house and barn could make a sale more practical. (Under the traditional rule, Hank would own all of Parcel W and Jay nothing.)

**The barn rent.**
- *Possession by Hank.* Each cotenant may possess the whole property, so a cotenant in sole possession owes no rent for his own occupancy unless he has ousted the others. Hank's statement "you don't get anything" refused Iris a share of rents but did not refuse her possession, which she never sought, so there was likely no ouster.
- *Rents from third parties.* A cotenant who collects rent from a third party must account to the other cotenants for their share of the net rent. Hank collected $1,500 a month from the horse-boarding business beginning in 2021 and kept all of it. Iris was entitled to half of the net rent from 2021 until her death (about $750 a month, less her share of expenses such as taxes on the barn).
- *Iris's accrued claim.* Iris's right to an accounting for rents already collected was a personal claim for money. It passed to her estate and, under her will, to Jay, even under the traditional rule in which survivorship gave Hank the land itself.
- *After Iris's death.* If the tenancy was severed, Jay as a tenant in common is entitled to half of the net barn rent from 2024 onward.
- **Conclusion:** Jay may obtain an accounting for half the net barn rent from 2021 to the present (through Iris's estate for the period before her death, in his own right afterward).

**Common mistakes**
1. Concluding there is no easement because the deed is silent. Implied easements arise without a writing.
2. Treating the $90,000 alternative as defeating every implied easement. It defeats necessity (strict) but not an implied grant from prior use (reasonable necessity).
3. Saying Kim lacks notice because her own deed omits the covenant. A recorded deed in her chain of title gives record notice.
4. Charging Hank rent for living in the farmhouse. Without ouster, a cotenant in possession owes nothing for his own use; third-party rents are different.
5. Forgetting that the traditional rule required a straw for self-conveyance; give both results.

**Accuracy notes.** The call does not specify California law; California points are flagged in one sentence each (*Riddle*; Civ. Code §§ 683.2, 1104, 1468). The answer does not rely on the Restatement's servient-owner relocation rule (repo item V-653) or on California's exclusive-prescriptive-easement doctrine (V-654). Courts are divided on whether short-term rentals breach residential-use covenants, so the rubric gives credit for either reasoned conclusion.
