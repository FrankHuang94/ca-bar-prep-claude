# E-PRP-004 — Greenfield Ranch

## Facts

In 2004 Ruth Hale owned Greenfield Ranch, 300 acres of grazing land. Her daughter, Alma, then 40, had two children: Beth, born in 1990, and Cal, born in 1995. On June 1, 2004, Ruth signed, delivered and recorded a deed conveying Greenfield Ranch "to my daughter Alma for life, then to those of Alma's children who reach the age of 25, in equal shares; but if no child of Alma reaches 25, then to my nephew Tom and his heirs." Alma's third child, Dina, was born in 2006. Beth turned 25 in 2015 and Cal in 2020. Dina is now 20, and Alma is 62. All of them, and Tom, are alive.

In 2010 Ruth conveyed an adjoining 20-acre parcel, the Creek Parcel, by a recorded deed "to Valley Riding Academy, Inc. so long as the land is used as a riding school." Valley Riding Academy is a for-profit business.

Ruth also owned a house on Elm Street. She died in 2015. Her valid will left "all my property, including any interest I hold in Greenfield Ranch or the Creek Parcel, and my Elm Street house, to my sons, Dan and Evan, as joint tenants with right of survivorship."

Alma lives in the city. Since 2016 she has leased Greenfield Ranch to neighboring ranchers for grazing. In January 2026 she signed a ten-year lease with Rock Co., a gravel company, which has begun digging a large gravel pit on 40 acres of the ranch. No gravel or other minerals had ever been taken from the ranch before. Rock Co. pays Alma a royalty for each ton of gravel it removes.

In 2024 Dan, without telling Evan, borrowed $100,000 from Summit Bank and signed a deed of trust on "all of Dan's interest" in the Elm Street house to secure the loan.

In March 2025 Valley Riding Academy closed its riding school and leased the Creek Parcel to a cellular company, which built a tower on it. No one has taken any action concerning the Creek Parcel since then.

Dan died in April 2026. His will leaves all his property to his wife, Fay. Summit Bank's loan has not been repaid.

## Call of the Question

1. What interests in Greenfield Ranch did Ruth's 2004 deed create, and who holds them today? Discuss.
2. What rights, if any, do the holders of future interests in Greenfield Ranch have against Alma and Rock Co.? Discuss.
3. What are the rights of Evan, Fay and Summit Bank in the Creek Parcel and the Elm Street house? Discuss.

## Model Answer

### 1. Greenfield Ranch

**A. Alma: life estate.** A life estate is a possessory estate measured by a life. "To my daughter Alma for life" gives **Alma a life estate.** She may possess, lease and use the ranch, but anyone who takes from her, including a lessee, takes only for her life.

**B. The remainder to Alma's children who reach 25.** A remainder is a future interest in a transferee that can become possessory on the natural end of the prior estate. It is contingent if it is given to unascertained persons or subject to a condition precedent, and vested if given to ascertained persons with no condition precedent other than the end of the prior estate. A vested remainder in a class that can still gain members is vested subject to open.

- *At creation (2004).* The gift is a class gift to "those of Alma's children who reach the age of 25." Beth was 14 and Cal 9, so no child had met the condition. The remainder was a **contingent remainder**.
- *Today.* Beth reached 25 in 2015 and Cal in 2020, so (if the gift is valid) each holds a **remainder vested subject to open**: their shares shrink if another child qualifies. Dina, born in 2006, is a class member, but she is 20, so her interest remains **contingent** until she turns 25.
- *Class closing.* Under the rule of convenience, the class closes when a member is entitled to possession, here at Alma's death. Children born afterward are excluded; physiologically the class closes then anyway. A child already born, such as Dina, who has not reached 25 at Alma's death can still share if she later reaches 25.

**C. Tom's interest and Ruth's reversion.** "But if no child of Alma reaches 25, then to my nephew Tom and his heirs" creates an **alternative contingent remainder** in Tom: the two remainders are conditioned on opposite events. "And his heirs" are words of limitation giving Tom a fee simple, not words giving his heirs any interest. Because Beth reached 25 in 2015, the condition for Tom's gift can never occur, so **Tom's interest has failed.** When a grantor creates contingent remainders, she keeps a **reversion** in case none vests. Ruth therefore retained a reversion in 2004.

**D. The common-law rule against perpetuities.** No interest is good unless it must vest, if at all, not later than 21 years after the death of some life in being at the creation of the interest. The rule judges what might happen, not what actually happens; a class gift is all-or-nothing; and everyone is presumed able to have children.

- *Creation.* The interests were created on June 1, 2004, when the deed was delivered.
- *Lives in being.* Ruth, Alma, Beth, Cal and Tom.
- *The class gift.* Alma was 40 and could have another child after 2004, as she in fact did. Suppose a child is born in 2006, and Alma, Beth and Cal all die in 2007. That child would reach 25 in 2031, more than 21 years after the last life in being died. Because the interest might vest too late, it fails. Because a class gift is all-or-nothing, **the entire remainder to Alma's children is void**, including the shares of Beth and Cal, even though they have since reached 25. The age-25 contingency on a class of children of a living person is the classic trap.
- *Tom's gift.* It vests only when it becomes certain that no child of Alma will reach 25. In the same scenario, that might not be known until more than 21 years after all lives in being have died, so **Tom's gift is also void.**
- *Result.* Strike the void interests: Alma has a life estate, and Ruth's reversion is a reversion in fee simple. Reversions are interests in the grantor and are not subject to the rule.

**E. California: the Uniform Statutory Rule Against Perpetuities.** California has adopted USRAP. A nonvested interest is valid if it either satisfies the common-law rule or actually vests or terminates within 90 years after its creation (Prob. Code § 21205). If it does neither, the court reforms the disposition to approximate the transferor's plan within the 90-year period (§ 21220).

- *Tom's gift* terminated in 2015, when Beth reached 25. Terminating within 90 years satisfies the statute.
- *The class gift* will vest completely when the class closes at Alma's death and every member has either reached 25 or died. Alma is 62 and the youngest child is 20, so this will almost certainly happen long before 2094. Under wait-and-see, the gift is valid as long as it actually vests within that time, and reformation is available if it somehow does not.

**In California, the remainder is valid**: Alma has a life estate, Beth and Cal hold remainders vested subject to open, and Dina holds a contingent interest in a share. Because the remainder has vested in Beth and Cal, Ruth's reversion has no remaining practical value.

**F. Who holds Ruth's reversion today.** Interests in the grantor are devisable. Ruth's will left "any interest I hold in Greenfield Ranch" to Dan and Evan as joint tenants (validity discussed in call 3). When Dan died in 2026, his interest ended and **Evan, as survivor, holds the reversion.** Under the common-law answer, that reversion will become possessory at Alma's death. Under USRAP, it is effectively worthless.

### 2. Waste: rights against Alma and Rock Co.

A life tenant must not commit waste, meaning conduct that unreasonably injures the future interest holders. Affirmative (voluntary) waste includes exploiting natural resources such as minerals. Under the open-mines doctrine, a life tenant may continue mining operations that were already open when her estate began, but may not open new ones.

- *Affirmative waste.* Digging a large gravel pit on 40 acres removes the land's substance and permanently changes a grazing ranch.
- *Open-mines doctrine.* No gravel or minerals had ever been taken from the ranch, so no pit was "open" when Alma's estate began in 2004, and the doctrine gives her no protection.
- *Alma's defense.* Alma will argue that her grazing leases since 2016 were legitimate and that gravel income is simply another way to use the land. Grazing leases are an ordinary use of the land. Selling its gravel consumes the property the remaindermen will receive.
- *Rock Co.* A lessee takes no greater rights than its lessor, so Rock Co. is equally bound and equally liable for waste. Its ten-year lease also ends automatically if Alma dies sooner.

*Who may sue.* Under the common-law answer, Evan holds a vested reversion and may sue for damages and an injunction. Under California's USRAP answer, Beth and Cal, as holders of vested remainders, may sue for damages and an injunction. Dina, as a contingent remainderman, can at least obtain an injunction, and courts commonly hold any damages for the benefit of those who ultimately take.

*Remedies.* An injunction against further excavation, plus damages for the injury to the future interests (the diminished value of the land or the value of gravel removed). Because royalties for removed gravel represent part of the land itself, the future interest holders may also seek an accounting so that those royalties are held for them rather than kept by Alma.

**The future interest holders may enjoin the excavation and recover for the gravel already removed from both Alma and Rock Co.**

### 3. The Creek Parcel and the Elm Street house

**A. Joint tenancy created by Ruth's will.** A joint tenancy has a right of survivorship and at common law requires the four unities of time, title, interest and possession, plus clear language of survivorship. Ruth's will gave Dan and Evan equal interests, by the same instrument, taking effect at the same time (Ruth's death), with equal rights to possession, "as joint tenants with right of survivorship." California likewise allows a joint tenancy created by will if it is expressly declared (Civ. Code § 683). **Dan and Evan held everything Ruth left them, including the Creek Parcel interest and the Elm Street house, as joint tenants.**

**B. The Creek Parcel.**
- *Classification.* "So long as the land is used as a riding school" is durational language, so the Academy received a **fee simple determinable**, and Ruth kept a **possibility of reverter**. A possibility of reverter is an interest in the grantor and is not subject to the rule against perpetuities. It is devisable, so it passed under Ruth's will to Dan and Evan.
- *Common law.* A fee simple determinable ends automatically when the stated event occurs. When the Academy closed the school and leased the parcel for a cell tower in March 2025, the land stopped being "used as a riding school." Title reverted at once to Dan and Evan as joint tenants, in fee simple. The cellular company's lease cannot outlast its lessor's estate, so it now occupies without right. When Dan died in 2026, **Evan became the sole owner** by survivorship.
- *California.* California has abolished the fee simple determinable and the possibility of reverter. A would-be determinable fee is treated as a fee simple subject to a restriction in the form of a condition subsequent, and the grantor's successors hold a power of termination (Civ. Code § 885.020). Forfeiture is therefore not automatic: Evan, as surviving holder, must exercise the power by notice or suit. A recorded power of termination expires 30 years after recording unless a notice of intent to preserve it is recorded (§ 885.030). The deed was recorded in 2010, so the power remains exercisable until 2040. The restriction still serves the grantor's purpose and is not obsolete. **In California, Evan may terminate the Academy's estate, but until he acts the Academy still owns the parcel.**

**C. The Elm Street house: Dan's deed of trust.** Whether a mortgage or deed of trust given by one joint tenant severs the joint tenancy depends on the mortgage theory.

- *Lien theory (majority; California).* The mortgagee or beneficiary holds only a lien, and the mortgagor keeps title. One joint tenant's deed of trust therefore does not sever the joint tenancy. If the borrowing joint tenant dies first, his interest ends at death and the survivor takes the whole free of the lien (*People v. Nogarr*; *Hamel v. Gootkin* for a deed of trust).
- *Title theory.* The mortgage transfers legal title to the lender, which breaks the unities and severs the joint tenancy. Dan's half would then have been a tenancy in common that passed under his will to Fay, subject to Summit Bank's lien.

Dan did not tell Evan, but unilateral action without consent can sever under either theory; the question is only whether this transaction severs. In California, Dan's deed of trust did not sever. At Dan's death **Evan became sole owner of the Elm Street house, free of Summit Bank's deed of trust.**

**D. Fay and Summit Bank.** A will cannot sever a joint tenancy, because it takes effect only at death, when the decedent's joint interest ends. Fay therefore takes nothing in the Elm Street house, the Creek Parcel or the Greenfield reversion; she inherits only property Dan owned outright. **Summit Bank is left with an unsecured claim against Dan's estate for the $100,000.** It took the risk that Dan would die first.

**Common mistakes**
1. Saving Beth's and Cal's shares under the common-law rule because they have already reached 25. The rule asks what might have happened when the deed was delivered, and the class gift fails as a whole.
2. Applying the RAP to the possibility of reverter or the reversion. Interests retained by the grantor are exempt.
3. Allowing Alma to open a new gravel pit under the open-mines doctrine, which protects only operations already open when her estate began.
4. Treating the Creek Parcel forfeiture as automatic in California.
5. Letting Dan's will or his deed of trust sever the joint tenancy in a lien-theory state.
