# E-CRM-001 — The Copper Pot fire

## Facts

Grace Lin owned the Copper Pot, a restaurant in a one-story commercial building she also owned. The restaurant was losing money, and Grace carried a $600,000 fire-insurance policy on the building and its contents.

In August 2026, Grace told her cousin Theo, "I need the Copper Pot to disappear. Make it look like an electrical fire, and I'll pay you $10,000 when the insurance pays." Theo agreed. Grace gave him a key and said the restaurant would be empty after it closed at 10 p.m. Grace knew that Luis, a dishwasher with nowhere else to live, sometimes slept on a cot in the storeroom after late shifts. She had approved the staff schedule, which showed Luis working the closing shift on September 4. She said nothing about Luis to Theo.

Theo asked his friend Marco to drive him to the restaurant at 2 a.m. on September 5, saying he needed to "pick up some equipment Grace said I could have." When they arrived, Theo took a gas can out of a duffel bag he had put in Marco's trunk. Marco asked what was going on. Theo said, "Grace wants the place torched for the insurance. Keep watch and honk if anyone comes, and I'll give you $500." Marco said, "Make it quick," and stood by the car watching the street.

Theo let himself in with the key, poured gasoline near the electrical panel in the kitchen and lit it. As the flames spread, Marco became frightened, shouted "I'm out!" to Theo through the open door, and drove away alone. Ten minutes later he called 911 from a pay phone without giving his name and reported the fire. By the time firefighters arrived, the fire had destroyed the kitchen and charred the dining-room walls. Luis, asleep in the storeroom, died of smoke inhalation.

Two days later, Theo saw a news report that a neighbor, Ruth Adams, had told police she saw "a man with a gas can" near the restaurant that night. Theo loaded his pistol and texted his girlfriend, "Going to make sure the old lady never testifies." He drove to Ruth's house, walked to the front door with the pistol in his jacket pocket and knocked twice. No one answered because Ruth was out of town. As Theo walked back to his car, police officers who had been watching the house arrested him.

When Grace later learned what Theo had done at Ruth's house, she told a friend, "Good—someone had to keep her quiet." Grace had not known Theo planned to go there. The insurer has not paid Grace's claim.

The jurisdiction's arson statute provides: "Any person who willfully and maliciously sets fire to or burns any structure, including a structure owned by that person if done with intent to defraud an insurer, is guilty of arson." First-degree murder includes any murder committed in the perpetration of arson. Conspiracy requires an overt act.

## Call of the Question

1. With what crimes can Grace reasonably be charged, and is she likely to be convicted? Discuss.
2. With what crimes can Theo reasonably be charged, and is he likely to be convicted? Discuss.
3. With what crimes can Marco reasonably be charged, and does he have any defense? Discuss.

## Model Answer

### 1. Grace

**Solicitation.** Solicitation is asking or encouraging another to commit a crime with the intent that it be committed; it is complete when the request is made. Grace asked Theo to make the restaurant "disappear" by staging "an electrical fire" and offered $10,000, intending the burning. She committed solicitation of arson. Because Theo agreed, **the solicitation merges into the conspiracy** and she cannot be punished for both.

**Conspiracy.** Conspiracy is an agreement between two or more persons to commit an unlawful act, with intent to agree and intent to achieve the objective, plus (here, by statute) an overt act by any conspirator.

- *Agreement and intent.* Grace proposed the burning and payment; Theo agreed. Both intended the building to burn so the insurer would pay.
- *Overt act.* Grace's handing over the key, and Theo's bringing gasoline, are overt acts.

**Grace is guilty of conspiracy to commit arson** (and to defraud the insurer). Conspiracy does not merge with the completed crime, so she may be convicted of both.

**Was the burning arson?** Common-law arson is the malicious burning of the dwelling of another. *Dwelling.* The prosecution will argue that a structure regularly used for sleeping can be a dwelling even if it is also used for business, and that Luis, who had nowhere else to live, slept there after late shifts. Grace will respond that the building is a restaurant, and an employee's occasional use of a cot in the storeroom does not make it a place regularly used as a home. *Of another.* Common-law arson protects occupancy, so an owner can burn the dwelling "of another" if someone else occupies it; but Grace owned and ran the building, and Luis, an employee allowed to sleep there sometimes, had no possessory interest of his own. **At common law, there was likely no arson.** The statute, however, covers "any structure," including one's own structure burned "with intent to defraud an insurer." Theo set the fire willfully and maliciously; the charring of the dining-room walls satisfies "burns"; and the purpose was to collect the $600,000 policy. **Under the statute, the burning was arson.**

**Grace as accomplice.** An accomplice is one who aids, counsels or encourages the crime with the intent that it be committed, and is punished as a principal. Grace planned the fire, supplied the key, promised payment and told Theo when the building would be empty. **She is guilty of arson as an accomplice**, as well as through her conspiracy.

**Murder of Luis: felony murder.** Murder is the unlawful killing of a human being with malice aforethought. A killing during the commission of an inherently dangerous felony is murder, with malice supplied by the intent to commit the felony; arson is a classic predicate. Under the majority agency theory, each felon is liable for a killing committed by a co-felon in furtherance of the felony.

Theo, Grace's co-felon, set the fire, and Luis died of smoke inhalation during it. Grace was not present, but felony-murder liability extends to accomplices and co-conspirators in the underlying felony. The arson is independent of the killing, so the merger limitation does not apply. Grace will argue that she never intended anyone to die, but felony murder requires no intent to kill. **Grace is likely guilty of felony murder**, which the statute makes first-degree murder because it occurred in the perpetration of arson. (If a court held the common-law definition controlled and there was no arson, the felony-murder theory would fail, which is why the depraved-heart theory matters.)

**Murder of Luis: depraved heart.** Malice also exists where a defendant consciously disregards an extremely high risk of death, showing extreme indifference to human life. Grace knew Luis sometimes slept in the storeroom after late shifts and had approved a schedule showing him on the closing shift that very night. She nonetheless arranged a gasoline fire at 2 a.m. and told Theo the building would be empty. Grace will argue she did not know Luis would actually stay that night and that he usually went elsewhere. But she was aware of a real chance that a sleeping man was inside a building she was having set on fire, and she chose to say nothing. That is conscious disregard of a very high risk of death with no social justification. **Grace is likely also guilty of depraved-heart murder** (second degree, absent the felony-murder rule), an independent route that does not depend on the arson definition.

**Attempted murder of Ruth: Pinkerton liability.** Under *Pinkerton*, a conspirator is liable for crimes committed by a co-conspirator in furtherance of the conspiracy that were reasonably foreseeable. A conspiracy lasts until its objective is achieved; concealment alone is not part of it unless agreed.

The prosecution will argue that the objective, collecting the insurance money, was not yet achieved because the insurer has not paid, so silencing a witness who could reveal the arson furthered the scheme. Grace will respond that she agreed only to a staged fire, not violence, and that shooting a witness is not a natural or foreseeable consequence of an insurance-fraud arson; she did not know of the plan. Her later statement ("Good—someone had to keep her quiet") is approval after the fact, which does not make her an accomplice because she gave no aid or encouragement before or during the attempt. Because killing a witness goes well beyond the nonviolent scheme Grace planned, **she is likely not liable for the attempted murder**, though this is close. The MPC rejects *Pinkerton* altogether and would impose liability only as an accomplice, which she was not.

**Attempted false pretenses (the insurance claim).** False pretenses is obtaining title to another's property by a knowing false representation of a past or present material fact, made with intent to defraud. By submitting a claim on the policy for a fire she had arranged, Grace represented that the building was lost to a covered fire, knowing that was false, and intended the insurer to pay her. The insurer has not paid, so the crime is incomplete; but filing the claim goes beyond preparation under either act test. **Grace is likely guilty of attempted false pretenses.**

**Conclusion (Grace):** conspiracy; arson (statutory) as an accomplice; murder of Luis (first-degree felony murder and, alternatively, depraved-heart murder); attempted false pretenses; likely not attempted murder.

### 2. Theo

**Conspiracies.** Theo conspired with Grace (above). He also likely conspired with Marco: Theo explained the plan, offered $500, and Marco said "Make it quick" and kept watch, a tacit agreement formed at the scene with an overt act (lighting the fire). **Guilty of conspiracy.** Under *Pinkerton*, Theo is also liable for Grace's attempted fraud on the insurer, the object of their agreement.

**Arson.** Theo poured gasoline and lit it, intending to burn the building, and the walls were charred. For the reasons above, this is arson under the statute though likely not at common law. **Guilty of arson.**

**Murder of Luis.**

- *Causation.* But for Theo's fire, Luis would not have died. Death by smoke inhalation is the foreseeable kind of harm a building fire creates, and nothing intervened. Theo will argue he did not know anyone was inside, but that goes to his mental state, not causation.
- *Felony murder.* Luis died during the arson itself, before Theo reached a place of temporary safety. Felony murder needs no intent to kill or awareness of the victim; the intent to commit arson supplies malice. Theo's lack of knowledge is no defense.
- *Other malice theories.* Theo relied on Grace's statement that the building would be empty, so depraved-heart malice is weaker for him than for Grace, and there is no evidence of intent to kill Luis.
- *Degree.* By statute, a murder in the perpetration of arson is first-degree murder; premeditation is not required.

**Theo is likely guilty of first-degree felony murder.**

**Attempted murder of Ruth.** Attempt requires the specific intent to commit the target crime plus an act beyond mere preparation.

- *Intent to kill.* Attempted murder requires intent to kill; intent to frighten or injure is not enough. Theo will argue he only meant to scare Ruth into silence. But he loaded his pistol, carried it to her door, and texted that he would "make sure the old lady never testifies." "Never" signals permanence, and bringing a loaded gun to the home of the one witness against him strongly supports an intent to kill. **The intent element is likely satisfied.**
- *The act.* Under the common-law dangerous-proximity test, the defendant must come dangerously close to completing the crime. Theo will argue he never saw Ruth, never drew the gun and was walking away, so he was far from killing her. Under the MPC and modern majority, it is enough to take a substantial step strongly corroborative of criminal purpose, such as lying in wait, searching for the contemplated victim, or possessing materials for the crime at or near the place of its commission. Arming himself, traveling to Ruth's house and knocking on her door with a loaded pistol are substantial steps that corroborate the plan stated in his text. Even under proximity, knocking on the victim's door while armed is arguably the last step before the shooting.
- *Impossibility.* Ruth's absence is factual impossibility, which is no defense: had the circumstances been as Theo believed, he would have completed the crime.
- *Abandonment.* Walking back to his car after no one answered is not a voluntary, complete renunciation; he left because his target was absent.

**Theo is likely guilty of attempted murder**, certainly under the substantial-step test.

**Conclusion (Theo):** conspiracy; arson; first-degree felony murder of Luis; attempted murder of Ruth; attempted false pretenses through *Pinkerton*.

### 3. Marco

**Accomplice to arson.** Marco began as an innocent driver, believing Theo was picking up "equipment." Accomplice liability requires aid with the intent that the crime be committed, and mere presence is not enough. But once Theo told him the plan, Marco agreed to "keep watch and honk if anyone comes," said "Make it quick," stood lookout, and was promised $500. Acting as a lookout is classic aid, and his words and the payment show he intended the burning to succeed. Marco will argue he was merely present and frightened, but he actively watched the street at Theo's request. **Marco is likely guilty of arson as an accomplice.**

**Conspiracy.** The same facts show a tacit agreement with Theo to commit arson, with an overt act. **Likely guilty of conspiracy.**

**Murder of Luis.** As an accomplice to the arson and a co-felon, Marco is liable under the felony-murder rule for a death caused by Theo in furtherance of the arson, even though he did not know Luis was inside and did not light the fire. Under the common-law natural-and-probable-consequences doctrine, death is also a foreseeable result of setting a building on fire at night. **Marco is likely guilty of felony murder.**

**Withdrawal.** An accomplice escapes liability only by withdrawing before the crime becomes unstoppable, by repudiating encouragement, neutralizing aid, or timely warning police. Marco shouted "I'm out!" only after the fire was lit and spreading, and his anonymous 911 call came ten minutes later. The arson was already complete, and Luis's death flowed from the fire already burning. **Withdrawal fails as to the arson and Luis's death**, although the call may help him at sentencing. Withdrawal is also no defense to the conspiracy itself once the agreement and an overt act exist. His announced withdrawal, communicated to Theo, may also cut off *Pinkerton* liability for later crimes (if he joined the larger conspiracy with Grace, notice to Theo alone may not suffice), and the attempt on Ruth two days later was not foreseeable to him in any event. **Marco is not liable for the attempted murder of Ruth.**

**Conclusion (Marco):** accomplice to arson, conspiracy, and felony murder of Luis; no liability for the attempt on Ruth.

**Common mistakes**
1. Assuming arson at common law. A restaurant owned by the defendant is neither a dwelling nor "of another"; use the statute.
2. Forgetting that attempted murder needs intent to kill. Felony-murder or depraved-heart reasoning cannot support an attempt.
3. Treating Grace's "Good" comment as making her an accomplice. Approval after the fact is not aid or encouragement.
4. Accepting Marco's "I'm out!" as a withdrawal. It came after the fire was burning.
5. Discussing California's SB 1437 felony-murder limits without being asked. The call does not ask for California law and the facts supply the governing statutes; mention California only in one optional line.
