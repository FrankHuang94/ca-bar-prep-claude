# E-CRM-003 — Coast Motors

## Facts

Victor Lane owns Coast Motors, a used-car dealership. Since 2021 Nadia Brooks has been its bookkeeper. Her duties included depositing customer payments into the dealership's bank account and paying approved vendor invoices by online transfer from that account. Any check over $2,500 required Victor's signature.

In January 2026 Nadia transferred $6,000 from the dealership's account to her personal account to pay overdue rent. She entered it in the ledger as "advance — N.B." and told no one. Nadia says she planned to repay it from her March bonus. She also says Victor owed her about $6,000 in unpaid overtime; she had complained twice, and Victor had replied that he would "look into it."

In February, Nadia prepared three invoices of $3,000 each from "Bay Tire Supply," a business name she had registered, for tires that were never delivered. Relying on the invoices, Victor signed three checks payable to Bay Tire Supply. Nadia deposited them into a Bay Tire account that only she controlled.

On March 10 Victor discovered both schemes and fired Nadia. At 11 p.m. that night, after the dealership had closed, Nadia drove there with a note she had written at home: "Partial payment of my overtime — N." She pushed open a side-office window she knew did not latch and climbed in. She took her personal laptop from her desk, the original timesheets the dealership kept for her, and $1,800 from the petty-cash drawer, and left the note in the drawer. She told police she went in "to get my laptop and my proof of what Victor owes me."

On February 20, a man calling himself "Owen Reyes" showed salesman Ben Ortiz a driver's license in that name and asked to test-drive a 2021 pickup priced at $18,000. Ben gave him the keys for a 30-minute solo drive. The man, actually Owen Ruiz, never returned. The license was fake.

On March 2 Ben spotted the pickup in a mall parking lot. He opened it with the dealership's spare key and sat in the driver's seat to drive it back. Owen ran up, pressed a hard object inside his jacket pocket against the window and shouted, "Get out of my truck or you're dead." Ben, believing Owen had a gun, got out, and Owen drove away. The object was a cell phone.

On March 5, at 10 p.m. in a grocery-store parking lot, Owen sold the pickup to Kai Lund, an auto mechanic, for $2,500 in cash. Owen said he had "lost the title" and would mail it. The vehicle identification number plate on the dashboard had been scratched. Kai asked, "Is this hot?" Owen answered, "No, it's my cousin's — he's moving overseas." Kai paid and drove the truck home. Police recovered it from Kai's garage on March 12.

## Call of the Question

1. What theft-related crimes, if any, has Nadia committed, and what defenses may she raise? Discuss.
2. What crimes, if any, have Owen and Kai committed, and what defenses may they raise? Discuss.

## Model Answer

The call does not ask for California law, so the common-law crimes are analyzed, with modern statutes noted where they change the result.

### 1. Nadia

**A. Embezzlement: the $6,000 transfer**

Embezzlement is the fraudulent conversion of the property of another by a person in lawful possession of it. It differs from larceny, which requires a trespassory taking from someone else's possession; an employee with mere custody who takes property commits larceny.

- *Lawful possession.* Nadia was not a clerk handling cash under supervision. She had independent authority to deposit funds and make vendor transfers from the account, so the funds were entrusted to her possession, not just her custody. (Bank funds are also intangible, which embezzlement statutes cover and common-law larceny did not.)
- *Property of another.* The money belonged to Coast Motors.
- *Conversion.* Moving $6,000 into her own account to pay her rent is a serious interference with the owner's rights: she spent the money.
- *Intent to defraud.* She concealed the transfer, told no one and used a cryptic ledger entry.

**Embezzlement is established unless a defense applies.**

**B. Nadia's defenses to the transfer**

*Intent to repay.* An intent to return the *exact* property can negate embezzlement, but an intent to repay equivalent money later is no defense unless the employee was authorized to use the funds. Nadia had no authority to make herself loans, and she meant to repay different money from a future bonus. **No defense.**

*Claim of right.* A good-faith belief that one is entitled to the property negates the intent to defraud (and the intent to steal). Nadia will argue she honestly believed Victor owed her about $6,000 in overtime, the same amount she took. Because embezzlement requires a specific intent, an honest belief need not be reasonable. Similarly, a mistake about a legal concept that is part of the crime (whether the money was "property of another" as against her wage claim) can negate the mental state.

The prosecution will respond that her own conduct shows she did *not* believe the money was hers: she called it an "advance," planned to "repay" it, and hid it. A person who believes she is collecting what she is owed does not plan to pay it back. The overtime claim was also disputed and unliquidated; Victor promised only to "look into it." Many jurisdictions limit claim of right to specific property or to liquidated debts. **The defense likely fails; Nadia is guilty of embezzlement.**

**C. The Bay Tire checks: false pretenses**

False pretenses is obtaining *title* to another's property by a knowing false representation of a past or present material fact, made with intent to defraud, on which the victim relies in passing title. If the victim passes only possession, the crime is larceny by trick.

- *False representation of fact.* Each invoice represented that tires had been delivered, a past fact that was false.
- *Knowledge and intent.* Nadia created the invoices and registered the fictitious business.
- *Reliance.* Victor signed "relying on the invoices."
- *Title passed.* Victor intended the payee, Bay Tire Supply, to own the money; he did not hand it over for a limited purpose. Nadia, as the person behind Bay Tire, obtained title.

This is not embezzlement: the $9,000 was never entrusted to Nadia's possession, because checks over $2,500 required Victor's signature, and she obtained the funds only through his deceived decision. Nor is it larceny by trick, because title, not mere possession, passed. **Nadia committed false pretenses (three counts, $9,000).**

**D. Burglary: the night entry**

Common-law burglary is the breaking and entering of the *dwelling* of another at nighttime with intent to commit a felony therein. Modern statutes reach entry into any building at any time with intent to commit a felony or theft inside.

- *Common law.* Pushing open a closed, unlatched window is a breaking, climbing in is an entry, and 11 p.m. is nighttime. But a car dealership's office is not a place where anyone regularly sleeps, so it is not a dwelling. **No common-law burglary.**
- *Modern statute.* The dealership is a building, Nadia had been fired that day and had no license to enter after hours, and she entered. The issue is **intent at the time of entry**.
  - *Laptop.* Retrieving her own laptop is not theft; an intent to recover one's own property cannot support burglary.
  - *Timesheets.* She intended to take the dealership's original records to use as "proof." The prosecution will argue that removing the employer's originals with no plan to return them is an intent to deprive. Nadia will argue they recorded her own work and she wanted them only as evidence, a claim of right.
  - *Cash.* The decisive fact is the note, written *at home before she drove over*: "Partial payment of my overtime." It shows that when she climbed through the window she already intended to take money. The question is whether that intent was an intent to *steal*. If her claim of right negates larcenous intent, it also negates burglary's intent element.

As explained in E below, the claim-of-right argument for the cash is weak. **Nadia is likely guilty of burglary under a modern statute**, but not common-law burglary.

**E. Larceny: the petty cash, the timesheets and the laptop**

Larceny is the trespassory taking and carrying away of the tangible personal property of another with intent to permanently deprive. The $1,800 cash and the original timesheets are tangible property of Coast Motors, taken without consent and carried out. The laptop is Nadia's own property, so taking it is not larceny.

*Claim of right to satisfy a debt.* Some jurisdictions recognize claim of right when a person takes money in good faith to satisfy a debt she believes is owed; many limit the defense to specific property she owns or to liquidated, undisputed debts. Nadia's overtime claim was disputed and unliquidated. Moreover, the $6,000 she already transferred matched her own estimate of the overtime owed, so it is hard to believe she honestly thought another $1,800 was due. The note shows openness, which supports good faith, but it does not make an unliquidated claim a debt she could collect by self-help. **The cash taking is likely larceny.** The timesheets are closer: if Nadia meant only to preserve evidence of her claim, the jury could find no intent to permanently deprive.

**F. Consolidation, grading and merger**

Modern codes consolidate larceny, embezzlement and false pretenses into a single theft offense graded by value, though the prosecution must still prove one recognized form of theft. Here the amounts ($6,000; $9,000; $1,800) would each be felony-grade theft in most States. Burglary does not merge with the theft committed inside, so Nadia may be convicted of both.

### 2. Owen and Kai

**A. Owen: larceny by trick (February 20)**

Larceny by trick is larceny in which the defendant obtains *possession*, but not title, by a knowing misrepresentation. Ben handed over the keys for a 30-minute test drive; the dealership never intended to transfer ownership. Owen used a fake license and false name, which shows he intended at the moment of taking never to return the truck. Consent obtained by fraud does not defeat the trespass. He drove away (carrying away) and later sold the truck (intent to permanently deprive). Because title never passed, this is not false pretenses. **Owen committed larceny by trick of the $18,000 pickup.**

**B. Owen: robbery of Ben (March 2)**

Robbery is larceny from the person or presence of the victim accomplished by force or by intimidation, meaning a threat of immediate bodily harm.

- *Property of another in another's possession.* Owen will argue he could not "rob" a truck he already had. But Ben, the dealership's employee, had retaken possession: he was sitting in the driver's seat with the dealership's key, about to drive it back. The truck was again in the dealership's possession through Ben, and Owen, a thief, had no right to it.
- *Presence.* Ben was inside the truck; it was within his immediate control.
- *Intimidation.* "Get out of my truck or you're dead," with a hard object pressed to the window, was a threat of immediate death. Ben actually and reasonably believed Owen had a gun, and he surrendered the truck because of that fear.
- *Fake weapon.* Simple robbery requires only intimidation, so the fact that the "gun" was a phone does not matter. Whether Owen committed *armed* robbery depends on the jurisdiction: some statutes require a real weapon; others treat an object the victim reasonably believes is a weapon as enough.
- *Larceny elements.* Taking and carrying away with intent to permanently deprive are satisfied: Owen drove off and sold the truck three days later.
- *Claim of right.* Owen's "my truck" is no defense; he knew he obtained it by fraud.

This is not extortion, which involves a threat of *future* harm. **Owen committed robbery**; the included larceny and assault merge into it.

**C. Kai: receiving stolen property (March 5)**

Receiving stolen property requires (1) receiving possession or control, (2) of property that is stolen, (3) knowing (or believing) it is stolen, (4) with intent to permanently deprive the owner.

- *Receipt.* Kai paid and drove the truck to his garage.
- *Stolen.* Property obtained by larceny by trick and robbery is stolen property, and it was still stolen when Kai received it; police had not recovered it.
- *Knowledge.* This is the contested element. Suspicion is not knowledge. Kai will say he asked directly, "Is this hot?", was told it belonged to a cousin moving overseas, and believed the answer. Because knowledge is a subjective mental state, an honest mistake of fact negates it even if unreasonable.

The prosecution will argue that the circumstances show actual knowledge or belief: a price of $2,500 for an $18,000 truck (about 14% of its value); no title; a cash sale at 10 p.m. in a parking lot; a scratched VIN plate; and Kai's expertise as a mechanic, which makes the meaning of a defaced VIN obvious to him. His question shows he already suspected theft. Many courts and the MPC treat knowledge as satisfied when the defendant is aware of a high probability that a fact exists, unless he actually believes it does not (willful blindness). A jury may conclude that Kai asked only so he could say he asked.

- *Intent to deprive.* Keeping the truck as his own satisfies this element.

**Kai is likely guilty of receiving stolen property**, although his honest-belief defense presents a jury question.

**Scoring on the 40–100 scale** (`00-exam-intel/grader-expectations.md` §4):
- **75+:** each taking classified correctly (embezzlement / false pretenses / larceny / larceny by trick / robbery) with the possession-title reasoning; claim of right developed with the "advance" facts; burglary intent tied to the pre-written note; Kai's knowledge argued both ways.
- **65:** right crimes and conclusions, but the custody/possession or title/possession distinctions are asserted rather than explained, or the robbery possession point is missed.
- **55:** calls the Bay Tire scheme embezzlement or the test drive false pretenses, or finds common-law burglary of the dealership.
- **45 or below:** labels everything "theft" without elements.

**Common mistakes**
1. Treating intent to repay as a defense to embezzlement. Only intent to return the exact property counts.
2. Confusing false pretenses (title passes) with larceny by trick (possession only).
3. Calling a commercial building a "dwelling" for common-law burglary.
4. Treating Kai's question "Is this hot?" as proof of innocence. Suspicion is not knowledge, but the surrounding facts may prove knowledge or willful blindness.
5. Forgetting that intimidation by a fake weapon is enough for simple robbery.

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: PRC criminal law separates theft (盗窃), fraud (诈骗) and embezzlement by employees (职务侵占); the US common law draws its lines by how the defendant got the property (trespass, entrustment, or deception passing title) (approx.).
