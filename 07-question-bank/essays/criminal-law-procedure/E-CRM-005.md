# E-CRM-005 — "This is for Eli"

## Facts

At about 8 p.m. on Saturday, June 6, 2026, Drew Hale's 16-year-old son Eli came home with a bloody face. He told Drew that Marco Vance, 30, the manager of the city skate park, had punched him repeatedly after an argument over a confiscated skateboard. Eli's nose and two ribs were broken. Drew drove Eli to the hospital.

At 8:40 p.m., while doctors treated Eli, Drew called his friend Cal Burke and said, "Marco did this to my boy." Cal answered, "Guys like that only learn one way. Pick me up — I'll bring something." When Drew arrived, Cal got in with an aluminum baseball bat and a pint of whiskey. As they drove 25 minutes to Marco's apartment, Drew drank about half the whiskey. Cal handed him the bat and said, "Break his arms. Make him feel what Eli felt."

At 9:30 p.m., Drew knocked on Marco's door while Cal waited at the bottom of the steps. When Marco opened the door, Drew said, "This is for Eli," and swung the bat, striking Marco's raised forearm. As Marco fell, Drew swung once more and hit him on the side of the head. Cal shouted, "Again!" Drew dropped the bat, and the two men left.

Marco suffered a broken arm and a skull fracture. Surgery went well, and his doctors expected a full recovery. On June 9, a nurse misread Marco's chart and injected ten times the prescribed dose of a sedative. Marco died within hours. The medical examiner found that the overdose was the immediate cause of death and that Marco was receiving sedation only because of his head injury. The hospital's internal review called the error "a serious and unusual departure from standard nursing practice."

After leaving Marco's apartment on June 6, Drew drove while Cal sat in the passenger seat. Drew had been convicted of driving under the influence in 2022 and had completed a court-ordered course on the dangers of drunk driving. Cal, who knew Drew had been drinking, said, "Floor it — we need to get out of here." Drew drove 70 mph on a residential street with a 30-mph limit, ran a red light, and struck and killed Paige Lin, who was walking in a marked crosswalk. Drew's blood-alcohol concentration an hour later was 0.14%.

When arrested, Drew told police, "I never meant to kill anybody. I just lost it when I saw Eli's face." Drew had no history of violence.

## Call of the Question

1. Of what homicide offenses, if any, can Drew be convicted for the deaths of Marco and Paige? Discuss.
2. Of what homicide offenses, if any, can Cal be convicted? Discuss.

## Model Answer

**Framework.** Murder is the unlawful killing of a human being with malice aforethought: intent to kill, intent to inflict serious bodily injury, depraved-heart recklessness, or felony murder. An unlawful killing without malice is manslaughter.

### 1. Drew

#### A. The death of Marco

**Causation.** The defendant's act must be both the actual (but-for) cause and the proximate cause of death. Foreseeable, dependent intervening causes, such as ordinary medical negligence in treating the injury, do not break the chain; an unforeseeable, independent cause does.

- *Actual cause.* But for the blow to the head, Marco would not have been hospitalized or sedated, and would not have received the overdose. Drew is an actual cause.
- *Proximate cause.* Drew will argue that the overdose was a superseding cause: the head injury was no longer life-threatening, doctors expected a full recovery, and the hospital called the error "serious and unusual." Some courts treat gross medical negligence that is the *sole* cause of death as superseding.
- The prosecution will respond that medical treatment, including negligent treatment, is a normal and foreseeable response to a skull fracture; the sedative was given only because of the injury Drew inflicted; and medication errors, while serious, are a known risk of hospital care. The intervening cause was dependent on Drew's act, not an independent event like a fire or a stranger's attack.

On balance, most courts would hold Drew to be the proximate cause, because the error occurred in treating the very injury he inflicted. **Causation is likely satisfied.** If a jury found the nurse's conduct so extraordinary that it alone caused the death, Drew would be guilty of aggravated battery (and attempted murder only if he intended to kill), not homicide.

**Malice.**
- *Intent to kill.* A jury may infer intent to kill from the intentional use of a deadly weapon aimed at a vital part of the body. An aluminum bat swung at a man's head is a deadly weapon used against a vital area. But the inference is permissive, and the other facts point away from intent to kill: the plan Cal voiced was to "break his arms"; the first blow struck the forearm; the head blow came as Marco fell; Drew swung only twice and stopped, ignoring Cal's "Again!"; and Drew said he "never meant to kill anybody." Intent to kill is doubtful.
- *Intent to inflict serious bodily injury.* Malice also exists if the defendant intended to cause serious bodily injury and death resulted. Drew went to the door armed with a bat, announced "This is for Eli," and swung at Marco hard enough to break his arm and fracture his skull. At a minimum he intended serious bodily injury. **Malice is present; absent mitigation, the killing is murder.**

**First-degree murder: premeditation and deliberation.** First-degree murder requires a willful, deliberate and premeditated killing: an intent to kill formed after reflection, even if brief. The prosecution will point to planning (a 25-minute drive, a bat obtained in advance), motive (revenge for Eli) and a manner suggesting purpose ("This is for Eli"). But premeditation must concern a *killing*, and the plan was to injure ("break his arms"); the head blow came in the moment as Marco fell. Drew had also been drinking. **First-degree murder is unlikely; the killing is at most second-degree murder.**

**Voluntary manslaughter: heat of passion.** A killing that would otherwise be murder (intent to kill or to inflict serious bodily injury) is reduced to voluntary manslaughter if the defendant acted in a sudden heat of passion caused by adequate provocation, before a reasonable person would have cooled off and before he actually cooled off.

- *Adequate provocation.* A serious battery on a close family member is a recognized category of provocation in many jurisdictions; Eli, 16, had his nose and ribs broken. The prosecution will argue that Drew did not *witness* the beating but learned of it from Eli, and that words alone are not adequate provocation. Drew will respond that he saw his son's bloody face and broken nose, which is more than words, and that informational words reporting a provoking event are treated differently from mere insults in some courts.
- *Actual passion.* "I just lost it when I saw Eli's face" supports actual passion.
- *Cooling time.* This is Drew's weakest element. Ninety minutes passed between Eli's arrival and the attack. Drew spent time at the hospital, made a phone call, picked up Cal and drove 25 minutes. A reasonable person would likely have cooled, and the deliberate steps (calling for help, arming himself) suggest a calculated revenge rather than a sudden passion. Intoxication does not help, because the reasonable person is sober.
- *MPC alternative.* Under the MPC, a killing is manslaughter if committed under extreme mental or emotional disturbance for which there is a reasonable explanation, judged from the actor's situation as he believed it to be, with no fixed cooling-off rule. A father who has just seen his son's injuries has a stronger claim under this broader test.

**Conclusion:** Under the common law, the cooling time likely defeats mitigation; under the MPC, a jury could find extreme emotional disturbance and return manslaughter.

**Voluntary intoxication.** Voluntary intoxication can negate a specific intent, including premeditation and deliberation or the actual formation of an intent to kill, but it is no defense to depraved-heart or reckless killings, and it does not supply provocation. Drew's 0.14% blood-alcohol level supports the conclusion that he did not premeditate. But his conduct shows a functioning, purposeful mind (he drove, knocked, announced his reason and aimed his swings), so intoxication will not negate his intent to inflict serious injury.

**Felony murder.** Aggravated battery or assault with a deadly weapon cannot serve as the predicate felony because it is not independent of the killing; it merges. **No felony murder.**

**Conclusion on Marco:** Drew is likely guilty of **second-degree murder** (intent to inflict serious bodily injury). A jury that accepts provocation (more likely under the MPC) could return **voluntary manslaughter**.

#### B. The death of Paige

**Causation.** Drew's car struck Paige in a crosswalk; he is the actual and proximate cause.

**Depraved-heart murder.** A killing is murder if the defendant consciously disregarded an extremely high and unjustifiable risk of death, showing extreme indifference to human life. Drew drove 70 mph in a 30-mph residential zone, ran a red light, and had a blood-alcohol level of 0.14%. Most importantly, his 2022 DUI conviction and court-ordered course gave him actual knowledge of the deadly risk of drunk driving. That subjective awareness distinguishes this from ordinary drunk driving. Voluntary intoxication is no defense to depraved-heart malice. Drew will argue he was fleeing in panic, not indifferent to life, but panic does not reduce the risk he chose to create.

**Involuntary manslaughter (alternative).** If the jury finds he did not actually appreciate the risk, the killing is involuntary manslaughter: criminal negligence, a gross deviation from reasonable care creating a substantial risk of death. Misdemeanor (unlawful-act) manslaughter is a further alternative, since speeding and running a red light are unlawful acts that proximately caused the death; many States also have vehicular-manslaughter statutes.

**Felony murder.** A killing during immediate flight from a felony, before the felon reaches a place of temporary safety, is within the felony-murder rule, and Drew killed Paige while fleeing the attack on Marco. But the only felony is the aggravated battery of Marco, an assault-type felony that merges and cannot serve as the predicate. **No felony murder; Paige's death turns on depraved-heart malice.**

**Conclusion on Paige:** Drew is likely guilty of **second-degree (depraved-heart) murder**, or at least **involuntary manslaughter**.

### 2. Cal

#### A. Marco's death

**Accomplice liability.** A person is liable as an accomplice, and punished as a principal, if he aided, counseled or encouraged the crime with the intent to aid and the intent that the crime be committed. Mere presence is not enough.

- *Act.* Cal did far more than stand by: he volunteered to "bring something," supplied the bat and the whiskey, told Drew to "break his arms," and shouted "Again!" during the attack.
- *Intent.* Cal intended Drew to inflict serious injury ("Make him feel what Eli felt"). For a crime defined by a result, an accomplice must have the mental state required for that result. Cal shared the intent to inflict serious bodily injury, which is a form of malice; nothing shows he intended a killing.
- *Causation.* Cal's aid helped produce the blow that caused the death, and the causation analysis above applies equally to him.

**Cal is liable as an accomplice to the homicide with intent-to-injure malice: second-degree murder.**

**Provocation is personal.** Drew's heat-of-passion argument depends on his own reaction to his son's injuries. Cal is not Eli's father, was calm enough to plan and gather a weapon, and acted from a different state of mind. Most modern courts and the MPC allow an accomplice to be convicted of a different, even greater, degree of homicide than the principal when their mental states differ. **Even if Drew is convicted of voluntary manslaughter, Cal may be convicted of murder.**

**Scope of liability.** Even if a jury found that Cal intended only a simple beating, the common-law **natural and probable consequences** doctrine would make him liable for any further crime that was a foreseeable result of the intended one; a death from a bat beating is foreseeable. The MPC rejects that doctrine and holds an accomplice liable for a result only if he had the culpability required for it; Cal's intent to inflict serious injury (or at least his recklessness as to death) would support murder or manslaughter on that basis. (California does not allow the natural and probable consequences doctrine to support a murder conviction, Pen. Code § 188(a)(3), but the call does not ask for California law.)

#### B. Paige's death

Cal did not drive, but he supplied the whiskey, knew Drew had been drinking, and urged him to "Floor it." Accomplice liability can extend to unintended homicides when the accomplice intentionally encourages the dangerous conduct (here, fast drunk driving) and himself has the culpability required for the result. Cal will argue that he did not intend anyone's death and that complicity in an unintentional crime is conceptually awkward; some courts are reluctant to impose it. But many courts and the MPC accept it where the accomplice knowingly promotes reckless conduct that causes death.

Cal was aware that Drew was drunk and urged him to speed on a residential street, a conscious disregard of a substantial risk. That supports **involuntary manslaughter**. Depraved-heart murder is less likely for Cal, because nothing shows he knew of Drew's prior DUI or appreciated an extremely high risk of death to the same degree.

**Conclusion:** Cal is likely guilty of **second-degree murder** of Marco as an accomplice and **involuntary manslaughter** of Paige.

- **75+:** separate analysis for each death; causation argued both ways with the medical-negligence rule; malice theories distinguished and premeditation rejected with reasons; cooling time treated as the decisive provocation element, with the MPC alternative; Cal's liability built from his acts and mental state, including the point that provocation is personal.
- **65:** reaches sound conclusions but merges intent to kill and intent to injure, or omits the MPC alternative or the complicity-in-recklessness point.
- **55:** treats the nurse's error as automatically superseding (or ignores causation), or makes Drew guilty of first-degree murder merely because he drove to Marco's home.
- **45 or below:** labels each death "murder" or "manslaughter" without malice analysis.

**Common mistakes**
1. Skipping causation because the defendant obviously hurt the victim. When death follows a medical error, causation is a major issue.
2. Treating any planning as premeditation of a *killing*. Planning to injure is not premeditation to kill.
3. Using a drunk defendant's perspective in the provocation test. The reasonable person is sober.
4. Assuming the accomplice gets the principal's mitigation. Heat of passion is personal.
5. Saying voluntary intoxication reduces depraved-heart murder. It does not negate conscious disregard of risk.
