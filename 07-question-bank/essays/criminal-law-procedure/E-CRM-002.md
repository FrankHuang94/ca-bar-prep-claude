# E-CRM-002 — The late-night taillight stop

## Facts

At 11:40 p.m. on a Saturday, Officer Kim stopped a rental car on the interstate because its left taillight was out. Dani Ortiz was driving alone. She produced a valid driver's license and a rental agreement in her name. Kim noticed a strong smell of air freshener, two cell phones on the passenger seat, and that Dani's hands shook as she handed over her documents. Dani said she was driving home from visiting her sister in Valley City. A recent police bulletin had described Valley City as a "source city" for methamphetamine; Valley City is the largest city in the region, with about two million residents.

At 11:52 p.m., Kim handed Dani a written warning for the taillight and returned her license. He then said, "Before you go, I need you to wait for a K-9 unit. It's close." Dani asked whether she could leave. Kim said, "Not yet." The K-9 unit arrived at 12:05 a.m. The dog, which is certified and has a strong record of accurate alerts, walked around the car and alerted at the trunk. Officers opened the trunk, found a locked suitcase, forced it open and found two pounds of methamphetamine. They arrested Dani, handcuffed her and placed her in the back of the patrol car.

Kim then picked up one of the phones from the passenger seat. It was unlocked. He scrolled through the text messages and read one from that afternoon: "Got the 2 lbs. Back by midnight." He bagged both phones.

The next morning, Detective Ruiz applied for a warrant to search both phones. His affidavit described the dog's alert, the methamphetamine in the trunk, the two phones, and the text message Kim had read. Ruiz stated in the affidavit that drug couriers commonly use phones to arrange deliveries. A magistrate issued the warrant, and a forensic search recovered dozens of texts arranging purchases from a supplier named "Gus."

At 1:30 a.m., at the station, Ruiz read Dani her Miranda rights from a printed card. Dani said she understood them. Ruiz asked, "Who did you get the meth from?" Dani replied, "I think maybe I should talk to a lawyer?" Ruiz said, "That's your call. But this is your one chance to help yourself before the DA decides how hard to come down on you." After a few seconds, Dani said, "Fine. I picked it up from Gus, but I didn't know it was that much."

Two days later, Dani was arraigned on a charge of possession of methamphetamine for sale, and a public defender was appointed. Ruiz then arranged for Kyle, a jail inmate who had been paid for information in past cases, to be moved into Dani's cell. Ruiz told Kyle, "Don't ask her questions; just keep your ears open." Over the next week, Kyle repeatedly asked Dani about her "supplier" and "how you got caught," and offered to help her find a better lawyer if she trusted him. Dani told Kyle that she had made four earlier trips to buy methamphetamine from Gus, and that in June she had stolen a car from a rental lot in Valley City. Dani was later charged, in a separate case, with the June car theft.

The case is pending in a California superior court. Dani moves to suppress: (1) the methamphetamine; (2) the text messages, both the one Kim read and those found under the warrant; (3) her statement at the station; and (4) her statements to Kyle, in both the drug case and the car-theft case.

## Call of the Question

2. Should the court suppress the text messages? Discuss.
3. Should the court suppress Dani's statement at the station? Discuss.
4. Should the court suppress Dani's statements to Kyle in the drug prosecution and in the car-theft prosecution? Discuss.

## Model Answer

**Governing law.** All the evidence was obtained by police, so there is government action. The Fourth, Fifth and Sixth Amendments apply to the States through the Fourteenth. Because the case is in a California court, Truth-in-Evidence (Cal. Const. art. I, § 28(f)(2)) means evidence is excluded only where the federal Constitution requires it, so the federal analysis below controls.

### 1. The methamphetamine

**Standing.** Fourth Amendment rights are personal. A driver in sole, lawful possession of a rental car has a reasonable expectation of privacy in it (*Byrd*), and Dani was the named renter and the only occupant. The locked suitcase was in her car, and nothing suggests it belonged to anyone else. Dani also may challenge her own seizure. **She has standing.**

**The initial stop.** A traffic stop is a seizure that requires reasonable suspicion or probable cause of a violation; the officer's motives are irrelevant (*Whren*). The broken taillight was a violation Kim personally observed. **The stop was lawful at its inception.**

**Prolonging the stop.** A stop may last no longer than needed to complete its mission: checking the license and registration, addressing the violation and attending to safety. Police may not prolong it for a dog sniff, even briefly, without independent reasonable suspicion (*Rodriguez*). The mission was complete at 11:52 p.m., when Kim issued the warning and returned the license. He then held Dani for 13 more minutes, refusing her request to leave, solely to bring the dog. That extension is lawful only if Kim had reasonable suspicion of drug activity: specific, articulable facts, viewed in their totality, that criminal activity was afoot (*Terry*; *Sokolow*).

The prosecution will point to five facts: the strong air freshener (a possible masking agent), two phones (often used by couriers), Dani's shaking hands, a rental car, and travel from a "source city" late at night. Taken together, the State argues, an experienced officer could reasonably suspect drug courier activity, and *Sokolow* teaches that individually innocent facts can add up to reasonable suspicion.

Dani will respond that each fact is common among innocent travelers. Many people carry a work phone and a personal phone; air freshener is common in rental cars; drivers stopped by police at night are often nervous; and nervousness alone is weak. Valley City is the region's largest city, with two million residents, so traveling from there says almost nothing about an individual. Dani also gave a plausible reason for her trip, and her documents were in order. Kim himself did not act on any suspicion until after he had completed the stop, which suggests the dog was a hunch rather than a response to articulable facts.

This is a close call, and courts differ on similar clusters of facts. But because the most specific facts (the air freshener and the phones) have common innocent explanations and the "source city" label applies to the region's largest city, **the 13-minute extension was likely not supported by reasonable suspicion and violated the Fourth Amendment.** (If the court found reasonable suspicion, a 13-minute wait for a nearby dog would be a reasonably diligent investigation.)

**The dog alert and the search of the suitcase.** A dog sniff during a lawful stop is not a search (*Caballes*). An alert by a reliable, certified dog gives probable cause (*Florida v. Harris*). Under the automobile exception, police with probable cause to believe a vehicle contains contraband may search it without a warrant, including any container that could hold the object of the search, even a locked suitcase in the trunk (*Ross*; *Acevedo*). So **if the detention had been lawful, the search was valid.**

**Exclusion.** Evidence derived from an unlawful seizure is excluded as fruit of the poisonous tree unless the prosecution shows an independent source, inevitable discovery or attenuation (*Wong Sun*). The methamphetamine was found only because Dani was unlawfully held for the dog. There was no independent source, nothing shows the drugs would inevitably have been found, and no intervening event broke the chain: the alert came minutes into the illegal detention. The good-faith exception does not apply because Kim acted without a warrant or binding authority. **The methamphetamine should likely be suppressed.**

### 2. The text messages

**The text Kim read at the scene.** Searching the digital contents of a cell phone seized incident to arrest generally requires a warrant (*Riley*). After Dani was handcuffed in the patrol car, Kim picked up the unlocked phone and read her texts. Officer safety did not require it, since data cannot be a weapon, and there was no risk of destruction once the phone was in police hands; police could secure it and seek a warrant, as they later did. The State may argue that the automobile exception, rather than search incident to arrest, allowed the search because the phone was in the car. But *Riley*'s reasoning, that a phone's contents differ in kind from physical containers, makes that argument unlikely to succeed. **The scroll was an unlawful warrantless search, and the "Got the 2 lbs." text must be suppressed.**

**The texts found under the warrant.** Evidence obtained under a later valid warrant is admissible under the independent-source doctrine if, after excising the illegally obtained information from the affidavit, the remaining information still establishes probable cause, and the decision to seek the warrant was not prompted by the illegal search (*Murray*).

- *Excising Kim's text.* Without the quoted message, the affidavit still describes a dog alert, two pounds of methamphetamine, two phones and the detective's statement that couriers use phones to arrange deliveries. That would ordinarily establish probable cause to search the phones, and police would likely have sought a warrant in any drug-courier arrest, so the scroll probably did not prompt the application.
- *But the rest of the affidavit is also tainted.* The alert and the methamphetamine are themselves fruits of the unlawful prolonged detention (call 1). If they are excised too, nothing supports probable cause. The warrant is therefore derived from the illegal seizure, and the independent-source doctrine cannot save it.
- *Good faith.* The State will argue the officers reasonably relied on a magistrate's warrant (*Leon*). But *Leon* addresses a magistrate's error, not police illegality used to obtain the warrant, and most courts hold that the good-faith exception does not save a warrant that rests on evidence from an earlier unlawful search or seizure.

**If the detention was unlawful, the warrant texts are suppressed as derivative evidence.** If the court found reasonable suspicion for the detention, the texts found under the warrant would be admissible under *Murray*, though the scroll text would still be excluded.

### 3. The station statement

**Fruit of an unlawful arrest.** Dani's arrest rested entirely on the methamphetamine found during the illegal detention. A confession that follows an illegal arrest is admissible only if the taint is attenuated, judged by the time elapsed, intervening circumstances and the flagrancy of the misconduct; Miranda warnings alone do not purge the taint (*Brown v. Illinois*). The statement came about 90 minutes after the arrest, with no release, appearance before a magistrate or other intervening event. **If the detention was unlawful, the statement is likely suppressed on this ground.** The Fifth Amendment analysis matters if the court disagrees.

**Miranda: custody, interrogation, warnings and waiver.** Miranda applies to custodial interrogation. Dani was under arrest at the station (custody), and "Who did you get the meth from?" is express questioning (interrogation). Ruiz gave complete warnings from a printed card, and Dani said she understood them. A waiver may be implied when a suspect who understands her rights makes an uncoerced statement (*Thompkins*). The question is whether her remark about a lawyer changed things.

**Request for counsel.** If a suspect in custody *unambiguously* requests counsel, all interrogation must stop until a lawyer is present or the suspect reinitiates (*Edwards*). But the request must be clear enough that a reasonable officer would understand it as a request for an attorney; police need not stop, or even ask clarifying questions, when the statement is ambiguous (*Davis*).

Dani will argue that "I think maybe I should talk to a lawyer?" conveyed a desire for counsel, and that Ruiz understood it, because he answered "That's your call" and then pressed her to talk. The State will respond that her words are almost identical to the statement held ambiguous in *Davis* ("Maybe I should talk to a lawyer"): "I think maybe," phrased as a question, expresses uncertainty rather than a request. Under *Davis*, **the remark was likely too equivocal to invoke the right to counsel**, so Ruiz could continue, and Dani's later answer was an implied waiver.

**Voluntariness.** Independently, a statement is inadmissible if it was involuntary under the totality of the circumstances. Ruiz's remark that this was Dani's "one chance to help yourself before the DA decides how hard to come down on you" implies that cooperation could bring leniency. A vague suggestion that cooperation may help, made to an adult after warnings, in a short interview without threats or deprivation, generally does not overbear the will. **The statement was likely voluntary.**

**Conclusion.** Under Miranda alone, the statement would be admissible. Because it is likely the fruit of an unlawful detention and arrest, **it should likely be suppressed from the case-in-chief.** If it is suppressed only on Fourth Amendment or Miranda grounds but was voluntary, it may still be used to impeach Dani if she testifies inconsistently (*Harris*; *Havens*).

### 4. The statements to Kyle

**Miranda.** Miranda does not apply when a suspect speaks to someone she does not know is a government agent, because the coercive pressure of police interrogation is absent (*Illinois v. Perkins*). Dani thought Kyle was a fellow inmate. **No Miranda violation.**

**Sixth Amendment.** The right to counsel attaches when formal adversary proceedings begin, here at the arraignment on the drug charge. After that, the government may not deliberately elicit statements about the charged offense outside counsel's presence (*Massiah*).

- *Government agent.* Kyle had been paid for information before, and Ruiz arranged to put him in Dani's cell for that purpose. He was acting for the State.
- *Deliberate elicitation.* The State will argue that Ruiz told Kyle to "just keep your ears open," like the passive "listening post" permitted in *Kuhlmann v. Wilson*. But Kyle did far more than listen: he repeatedly asked about her "supplier" and "how you got caught" and cultivated her trust by offering to find her a lawyer. As in *United States v. Henry*, the government is responsible for an informant's active questioning that it set in motion by placing a paid informant in the cell, whatever its instruction.

**The Sixth Amendment was violated as to the charged drug offense.**

**Which case?** The right is offense-specific (*Texas v. Cobb*). Statements deliberately elicited after attachment are inadmissible at the trial of the pending charge, but statements about other offenses, for which the right had not attached, may be used in prosecuting those offenses (*Maine v. Moulton*).

- *Drug prosecution.* Dani's statements about her supplier and her four earlier trips to buy from Gus pertain to the pending possession-for-sale charge and are offered in that case. **They must be suppressed in the drug prosecution's case-in-chief.** They may still be used to impeach her if she testifies (*Kansas v. Ventris*).
- *Car-theft prosecution.* When Kyle elicited the statements, Dani had not been charged with the June car theft, and the car theft is a different offense under *Blockburger*. Her right to counsel had not attached for it, and Miranda does not apply. **Her statement about the car theft is admissible in the car-theft case.**

**Common mistakes**
1. Starting with the automobile exception. The decisive problem is the 13-minute detention after the stop's mission ended.
2. Treating a later warrant as curing everything. *Murray* asks whether the *untainted* facts support probable cause.
3. Treating any mention of a lawyer as an *Edwards* invocation. *Davis* requires an unambiguous request.
4. Applying Miranda to a jailhouse informant. The issue is the Sixth Amendment, which is offense-specific.
5. Writing a separate California exclusionary analysis. Truth-in-Evidence makes the federal rule control; one sentence is enough.
