# E-CRM-004 — The Quick Fuel robbery

## Facts

At 10:30 p.m. on February 2, 2026, a man robbed the Quick Fuel gas station in Linden at gunpoint. The clerk, Mei Tan, faced him across the counter under bright lights for about 45 seconds while he demanded cash. He ran north on Elm Street toward Linden Elementary School. Mei told police the robber was a white man about 25, about 5'10", thin, wearing a black hoodie, with a star tattoo on the left side of his neck. A customer, Rosa Vega, saw the man for "two or three seconds" as he ran past the door; she described him only as "a white guy in a dark hoodie."

At 10:50 p.m. Officer Grant stopped Theo Mills, 24, a thin white man about 5'10", walking on Elm Street six blocks north of the station. Theo wore a gray sweatshirt, had a star tattoo on the left side of his neck and had $340 in cash in his pocket. Grant handcuffed him and radioed for Mei. At 11:05 p.m. another officer drove Mei to the spot. Theo stood alone beside the patrol car, handcuffed and lit by its spotlight. Officer Grant told Mei, "We think we got him — he had cash on him." Mei said, "That's him. I'm pretty sure. The tattoo is the same."

At the station at 11:40 p.m., Detective Ford sat down with the still-handcuffed Theo in an interview room. Without giving Miranda warnings, Ford said, "The gun — where is it? That school's right there; kids will be on that playground at 8 a.m." Theo said, "I tossed it in the dumpster behind the school." Officers found a revolver there at 12:15 a.m. Ford then asked, still without warnings, "So you did the Quick Fuel job?" Theo said, "Yeah. I needed rent."

Ford left for 15 minutes. He returned to the same room, read Theo complete Miranda warnings, and said, "Like you already told me, you did the job. Let's just get the details down." Theo signed a waiver form and gave a detailed confession. The department's interrogation manual advises detectives to "secure an admission before formal advisement when practical." Ford then asked about a pharmacy burglary two weeks earlier. Theo said, "I'm done talking. I want a lawyer." Ford ended the interview.

On February 3 Theo was arraigned on a robbery charge, and a public defender was appointed. He remained in jail. On February 4, Detective Kerr of the burglary unit, who did not know Theo had asked for a lawyer, visited him in jail, read him Miranda warnings and asked about the pharmacy burglary. Theo signed a waiver and admitted the burglary.

On February 10, without notifying Theo's lawyer, police held a six-person lineup for Mei. Theo was the only participant with a visible neck tattoo. Mei picked him immediately. On February 12, a detective showed Rosa six photographs, including Theo's booking photo, which showed his tattoo. The detective said, "Take your time. The guy we arrested is in here — the one with the tattoo." Rosa paused and said, "I guess it's him."

Theo has been charged with robbery and burglary.

## Call of the Question

1. Theo moves to suppress Mei's identifications of him at the scene, at the lineup and in court, and Rosa's identification of him. How should the court rule? Discuss.
2. Theo moves to suppress the gun and each of his statements. How should the court rule? Discuss.

## Model Answer

### 1. The identifications

**A. Mei's show-up at the scene (due process)**

The show-up took place before any formal charge, so there was no Sixth Amendment right to counsel (*Kirby*); only due process applies. An identification procedure violates due process if the police arranged a procedure that was unnecessarily suggestive and that created a substantial likelihood of misidentification. Even a suggestive identification is admissible if it is reliable under the totality of the circumstances, considering the witness's opportunity to view, degree of attention, accuracy of the prior description, level of certainty, and the time between the crime and the identification (*Neil v. Biggers*; *Manson v. Brathwaite*).

*Police-arranged and suggestive.* The police brought Mei to Theo. A show-up of a single handcuffed suspect beside a patrol car under a spotlight is inherently suggestive, and Officer Grant made it worse by saying, "We think we got him — he had cash on him."

*Necessity.* The State will argue that a prompt show-up 35 minutes after a robbery, near the scene, is justified because it lets police quickly confirm or release a suspect while the witness's memory is fresh. That justifies a show-up, but not the officer's comment, which was unnecessary.

*Reliability.*
- *Opportunity to view:* about 45 seconds, face to face across a counter under bright lights. Strong.
- *Degree of attention:* Mei was the person being robbed and was focused on the robber. Theo will argue that a gun draws attention away from a face, but she noticed specific details such as the tattoo's side and shape.
- *Accuracy of description:* age, height, build and a star tattoo on the left side of the neck all matched. The clothing did not: black hoodie versus gray sweatshirt, though a robber may discard a top.
- *Certainty:* "That's him. I'm pretty sure." Fairly confident, slightly hedged.
- *Time:* 35 minutes.

**Conclusion:** The procedure was unnecessarily suggestive because of the comment, but Mei's identification is reliable under *Biggers*. **The show-up identification is admissible**; Theo may attack its weight on cross-examination.

**B. The February 10 lineup (Sixth Amendment)**

The Sixth Amendment right to counsel attaches when adversary judicial proceedings begin, such as arraignment, and applies at every critical stage after that, including a post-charge, in-person lineup (*Wade*; *Gilbert*; *Kirby*). Testimony that the witness identified the defendant at an uncounseled post-charge lineup must be excluded.

Theo was arraigned on the robbery charge on February 3 and had appointed counsel. The live lineup on February 10 was a critical stage, and police did not notify his lawyer; Theo did not waive counsel. **Mei's lineup identification must be excluded.**

The lineup was also suggestive: Theo was the only participant with a visible neck tattoo, the very feature Mei had described. But suggestiveness alone does not require exclusion under due process; given Mei's strong opportunity to view, the identification would likely survive *Manson*'s reliability test, so the Sixth Amendment violation is the ground for exclusion.

**C. Mei's in-court identification**

After an uncounseled lineup, an in-court identification is allowed only if the prosecution proves by clear and convincing evidence that it rests on an independent source, the witness's own observation during the crime, rather than the tainted lineup. Mei watched the robber for 45 seconds at close range in good light, gave a detailed and accurate description, and identified Theo within 35 minutes, before the lineup (in a show-up that is itself admissible). **The prosecution can likely show an independent source; Mei may identify Theo in court.**

**D. Rosa's photo array**

*Counsel.* There is no Sixth Amendment right to counsel at a photographic identification, even after charges are filed (*Ash*). Rosa's identification is tested only under due process.

*Suggestiveness.* The detective told Rosa that "the guy we arrested is in here — the one with the tattoo," although Rosa had never mentioned a tattoo, and Theo's photo was the one that showed one. That comment told Rosa whom to pick. It was police-arranged and unnecessary.

*Reliability.* Rosa saw the man for "two or three seconds" as he ran by; she gave only a generic description ("a white guy in a dark hoodie"); her selection was hesitant ("I guess it's him"); and it came ten days later. Every *Biggers* factor weighs against reliability.

**Conclusion:** **Rosa's photo identification must be excluded.** Any in-court identification by Rosa would need a reliable basis independent of the tainted array; given her brief glimpse, the court should exclude it as well.

### 2. The gun and the statements

**Voluntariness (all statements).** A confession is involuntary if police coercion overbore the suspect's will under the totality of the circumstances (*Connelly*). Theo was questioned late at night while handcuffed, but there were no threats, promises, violence or prolonged questioning. The reference to children on the playground was a statement of fact about public danger, not a threat to Theo. **All of Theo's statements were voluntary.** That matters because voluntary statements taken in violation of Miranda may still be used to impeach him and to find physical evidence.

**A. Statement 1: "I tossed it in the dumpster"**

*Custody and interrogation.* Miranda warnings are required before custodial interrogation. Theo was handcuffed in a station interview room after an arrest; a reasonable person would not feel free to leave, so he was in custody. "The gun — where is it?" is express questioning. No warnings were given.

*Public-safety exception.* Police may ask questions reasonably prompted by an objective, immediate concern for public safety without warnings, and the answers are admissible (*New York v. Quarles*).

The State will argue that a loaded gun discarded somewhere along a route that passes an elementary school was a real public danger: anyone could find it overnight, and children would arrive at 8 a.m. Ford's question was limited to locating the gun, not to gathering evidence of guilt.

Theo will respond that *Quarles* involved an immediate danger, a gun hidden moments earlier in an open supermarket, whereas here more than an hour had passed, the questioning took place at the station, and the children would not arrive for eight hours, enough time to give warnings or search the route. Ford's mention of the school may also have been designed to elicit an answer.

On balance, the location of a discarded gun near a school was unknown and the risk was ongoing, so an objectively reasonable officer could see a need to find it promptly. **Statement 1 likely falls within the public-safety exception and is admissible.**

**B. The gun**

If Statement 1 is admissible under *Quarles*, the gun is too. Even if it is not, physical evidence derived from a *voluntary* statement taken without Miranda warnings is not suppressed (*United States v. Patane*). Theo's statement was voluntary. **The gun is admissible.**

**C. Statement 2: "Yeah. I needed rent."**

"So you did the Quick Fuel job?" had nothing to do with public safety; it sought an admission of guilt. Theo was in custody, the question was interrogation, and no warnings had been given. **Statement 2 is inadmissible in the prosecution's case-in-chief.** Because it was voluntary, it may be used to impeach Theo if he testifies inconsistently (*Harris v. New York*).

**D. Statement 3: the warned confession**

When police obtain an unwarned admission and then give warnings and obtain a second statement, the second statement is generally admissible if the first was voluntary (*Oregon v. Elstad*). But if police deliberately use a question-first, warn-later strategy to undermine the warnings, the warned statement is excluded unless curative measures were taken, such as a substantial break in time and circumstances or a warning that the first statement cannot be used (*Missouri v. Seibert*; Justice Kennedy's concurrence).

- *Deliberate strategy.* The department's manual tells detectives to "secure an admission before formal advisement when practical," and Ford followed it, obtaining an admission and only then giving warnings.
- *No curative measures.* The break was 15 minutes; the room, the detective and the subject were the same; and Ford did not tell Theo that his earlier answer could not be used. Instead he said, "Like you already told me, you did the job," using the unwarned admission to make the warnings seem pointless.

The State will argue that Ford gave complete warnings and Theo signed a written waiver, as in *Elstad*. But in *Elstad* the initial failure was a good-faith mistake and the later warnings gave a genuine choice. Here, under both the *Seibert* plurality's effectiveness test and the Kennedy deliberateness test, the midstream warnings could not function effectively. **Statement 3 is inadmissible in the case-in-chief**, though usable for impeachment because it was voluntary.

**E. Statement 4: the burglary admission on February 4**

*Edwards.* If a suspect in custody unambiguously requests a lawyer, all police-initiated interrogation must stop until counsel is present, unless the suspect himself reinitiates communication (*Edwards v. Arizona*). The rule covers questioning about *any* offense (*Arizona v. Roberson*) and protects the suspect until he has been out of custody for 14 days (*Maryland v. Shatzer*).

- *Invocation.* "I'm done talking. I want a lawyer" is unambiguous; it invoked both silence and counsel.
- *Continuous custody.* Theo remained in jail from the invocation in the early hours of February 3 through February 4, so there was no break in custody, let alone 14 days.
- *Police-initiated.* Detective Kerr came to Theo; Theo did not reinitiate.
- *Different crime; different officer.* *Roberson* holds that questioning about a separate offense is still barred, and an officer's ignorance of the invocation does not matter; the obligation binds the police as a whole.
- *Fresh warnings and waiver.* A waiver obtained through police-initiated interrogation after an invocation of counsel is invalid under *Edwards*, even after new warnings.

**Statement 4 is inadmissible in the case-in-chief** (it may be used for impeachment if voluntary, which it was).

*Sixth Amendment.* Theo's Sixth Amendment right attached on February 3, but only for the charged robbery; the right is offense-specific (*Texas v. Cobb*). The pharmacy burglary had not been charged, so questioning about it did not violate the Sixth Amendment. The exclusion rests on *Edwards*.

**Common mistakes**
1. Claiming a right to counsel at a photo array or a pre-charge lineup. Only post-charge, live identifications trigger the Sixth Amendment.
2. Stopping at "suggestive." Under *Manson*, reliability is the linchpin; a suggestive but reliable identification is admissible.
3. Excluding physical evidence derived from a voluntary unwarned statement. *Patane* admits it.
4. Thinking a new officer and a new crime avoid *Edwards*. *Roberson* bars police-initiated questioning on any crime while the suspect remains in custody.
5. Relying on the Sixth Amendment for the burglary statement. The right is offense-specific; the burglary had not been charged.

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: the PRC Criminal Procedure Law excludes confessions obtained by torture or other illegal methods, but has no Miranda-style warning requirement or rule that interrogation must stop when a suspect asks for a lawyer (approx.).

**Accuracy notes.** Rules follow R-CRM-093 to -096, -098 to -100, -102, -106 and -107. *Seibert* produced a plurality (effectiveness of midstream warnings) and Justice Kennedy's concurrence (deliberate strategy plus no curative measures); lower courts differ on which controls, and the answer applies both. The point that a prompt on-scene show-up can be justified by the need to confirm or dispel suspicion quickly reflects widespread lower-court practice rather than a specific Supreme Court holding. Whether *Quarles* covers questioning at the station more than an hour later is a judgment call; an answer that excludes Statement 1 but admits the gun under *Patane* should receive full credit. *Patane* has no majority opinion (a plurality plus a two-Justice concurrence in the judgment); its result is the settled rule stated in R-CRM-100.
