## Facts

Golden Hour Candle Co., Inc. ("Golden Hour"), a California corporation with its principal place of business in Los Angeles, sells luxury candles in amber glass jars with a hexagonal brass lid and a "sunset" gradient label. Theo Marsh, Golden Hour's former head of product design, lives in Pasadena, California. When Theo left Golden Hour in March 2025, he took copies of Golden Hour's confidential mold drawings for the jars and lids.

Theo then became a consultant to Lumen & Wick, Inc. ("Lumen"), an Oregon corporation with its principal place of business and its 12-person design team in Portland, Oregon. Theo worked mostly from Pasadena, but in mid-2025 he twice traveled to Portland, delivered the mold drawings, and trained Lumen's designers. In September 2025, Lumen launched "Amber Hour" candles in jars, lids and labels nearly identical to Golden Hour's. The jars are made from those molds by Lumen's contract manufacturer in Tijuana, Mexico, which keeps its production records there, and are shipped to Lumen's Portland warehouse. Lumen sells them nationwide, including through 40 retail stores in Los Angeles County, where several customers have told Golden Hour's staff that they bought Amber Hour candles thinking they were Golden Hour's.

On January 12, 2026, Golden Hour sued Lumen and Theo in Los Angeles County Superior Court. The complaint asserts:
1. trade-dress infringement under the federal Lanham Act, against Lumen;
2. unfair competition under California Business and Professions Code § 17200, against Lumen, based on the same packaging;
3. misappropriation of trade secrets (the mold drawings) under California's Uniform Trade Secrets Act, against Theo and Lumen; and
4. breach of contract against Lumen for $48,000 that Lumen has not paid for surplus candle wax Golden Hour sold it in 2024.

It seeks $2 million in damages on the first three claims.

Lumen was personally served through its registered agent on January 20. On February 13, before Theo had been served, Lumen filed a notice of removal in the United States District Court for the Central District of California, which embraces Los Angeles County, citing federal-question jurisdiction. Lumen did not seek Theo's consent. Theo was served on February 25.

On March 2, Lumen filed (a) a motion under 28 U.S.C. § 1406(a) to dismiss or transfer for improper venue, arguing that it resides in Oregon and that the candles were designed and made outside California; (b) alternatively, a motion to transfer the case to the District of Oregon under 28 U.S.C. § 1404(a), pointing to its Portland witnesses and records; and (c) a motion to dismiss for forum non conveniens in favor of the courts of Mexico, where the molds and the manufacturer's records are located. Golden Hour's five key witnesses work in Los Angeles. Golden Hour has offered evidence that Mexican courts would apply Mexican law to the dispute, under which its recovery would likely be smaller.

On March 30, Golden Hour moved to remand the entire action. It argues that (i) Theo is a California citizen, so the case could not be removed; (ii) Lumen removed without Theo's consent; (iii) the state-law claims predominate and belong in state court; and (iv) at the very least, the wax contract claim must be returned to state court.

## Call of the Question

1. Was the action properly removed, and how should the court rule on Golden Hour's motion to remand? Discuss.
2. How should the court rule on Lumen's motion to dismiss or transfer for improper venue, its alternative motion to transfer the case to the District of Oregon, and its motion to dismiss in favor of a Mexican forum? Discuss.

## Model Answer

### 1. Removal and remand

**A. Original jurisdiction over the Lanham Act claim.** A defendant may remove any civil action brought in state court over which the federal district courts have original jurisdiction, to the district and division embracing the place where it is pending (28 U.S.C. § 1441(a)). District courts have original jurisdiction over claims arising under federal law, judged by the well-pleaded complaint (§ 1331). Golden Hour's first claim is created by a federal statute, the Lanham Act, and appears on the face of its complaint, so it arises under federal law (see also § 1338). State courts have concurrent jurisdiction over trademark and trade-dress claims, which is why Golden Hour could file in superior court, but concurrent jurisdiction does not prevent removal. **The Lanham Act claim made the action removable.**

**B. Supplemental jurisdiction over the state-law claims.** In an action within original jurisdiction, the court has supplemental jurisdiction over all other claims so related that they form part of the same case or controversy, meaning they derive from a common nucleus of operative fact that one would ordinarily expect to be tried together. This includes claims that join additional parties (§ 1367(a); *Gibbs*).
- *Unfair-competition claim (§ 17200) against Lumen.* It rests on the same look-alike packaging and the same consumer confusion as the Lanham Act claim. It plainly shares a common nucleus.
- *Trade-secret claim against Theo and Lumen.* Golden Hour will argue that the misappropriated mold drawings are how Lumen produced the look-alike jars and lids. The same launch, the same products and the same witnesses (Theo and Lumen's designers) are involved. Lumen might argue that trade-secret misappropriation turns on different facts (secrecy, Theo's departure, use of the drawings) from consumer confusion. But a common nucleus does not require identical elements, and a plaintiff would ordinarily expect to try the theft of the designs and the copying of the resulting packaging together. The claim is within § 1367(a).
- *Adding Theo.* Theo is a new party against whom no federal claim is pleaded. Section 1367(a) expressly covers claims involving the joinder of additional parties. The § 1367(b) restrictions on claims against parties joined under Rules 19 and 20 apply only when jurisdiction rests *solely* on diversity. Here jurisdiction rests on a federal question, so § 1367(b) is irrelevant even though Theo and Golden Hour are both California citizens.

**Conclusion:** the court has supplemental jurisdiction over claims 2 and 3, including the claim against Theo.

**C. The wax contract claim.** The $48,000 claim concerns a 2024 sale of wax, a separate transaction with no factual overlap with the packaging or the drawings. It is not part of the same case or controversy, so § 1367(a) does not reach it. Diversity cannot support it either. Diversity must be complete across the action, and Golden Hour and Theo are both California citizens; the claim is also well below $75,000 on its own. When a removed action includes a federal-question claim together with a claim not within the court's original or supplemental jurisdiction, the defendant may remove the whole action, but the court **must sever the unrelated claim and remand it** to state court (§ 1441(c)). **Golden Hour's request (iv) should be granted:** the wax claim goes back to superior court, while the rest stays.

**D. Removal procedure.**
- *Timeliness.* A notice of removal must be filed within 30 days after formal service of the initial pleading (§ 1446(b); *Murphy Bros.*). Lumen was served on January 20 and removed on February 13, 24 days later. It was timely.
- *Unanimity.* In a § 1441(a) removal, all defendants who have been *properly joined and served* must join in or consent (§ 1446(b)(2)(A)). Theo had not been served when Lumen removed, so his consent was not required; once served, he had his own 30 days in which to act. Moreover, when § 1441(c) applies, as it does here because of the wax claim, only defendants against whom the federal claim is asserted must consent (§ 1441(c)(2)). The Lanham Act claim is asserted only against Lumen. **Golden Hour's argument (ii) fails.**
- *Forum-defendant rule.* A case removable *solely* on the basis of diversity may not be removed if a properly joined and served defendant is a citizen of the forum State (§ 1441(b)(2)). Golden Hour's argument (i) fails twice: Lumen removed on the basis of a federal question, not diversity, and Theo had not been served when Lumen removed. A defendant's California citizenship does not bar a federal-question removal.

**E. Golden Hour's motion to remand.**
- *Timing.* A motion to remand for any defect other than lack of subject-matter jurisdiction must be made within 30 days after the notice of removal is filed (§ 1447(c)). The notice was filed February 13; the 30 days ended in mid-March; Golden Hour moved on March 30. Its procedural objections (the forum-defendant rule and unanimity) were therefore **waived even if they had merit**. A lack of subject-matter jurisdiction can be raised at any time, which is why the wax claim's remand remains available.
- *Discretion to decline supplemental jurisdiction (§ 1367(c)).* A court may decline supplemental jurisdiction if a state claim raises a novel or complex issue of state law, substantially predominates over the federal claim, all federal claims have been dismissed, or exceptional circumstances exist. Golden Hour argues that two of its three remaining claims are state-law claims and that the trade-secret claim carries much of the damages. Lumen will respond that the Lanham Act claim is very much alive; that § 17200 and trade-secret claims raise no novel issues; and that splitting a single course of copying between two courts would waste resources and risk inconsistent results. Economy, convenience and fairness favor keeping the related claims together, and the state claims do not *substantially* predominate.

**Conclusion:** removal was proper. The court should deny the motion to remand as to claims 1–3, which it will keep, and sever and remand the wax contract claim (claim 4).

### 2. Lumen's venue motions

**A. Section 1406(a): improper venue.** Section 1406(a) applies only when venue is improper. Lumen argues that venue fails under the general venue statute, § 1391(b), because it resides in Oregon and the products were designed and made elsewhere. But § 1391 does not govern removed cases. A removed action goes to "the district court ... for the district and division embracing the place where such action is pending" (§ 1441(a)), and that placement is proper venue by definition (*Polizzi*). Los Angeles County lies in the Central District of California, so venue is proper there regardless of § 1391. Lumen's objection was timely, but it fails on the merits. **The § 1406(a) motion should be denied.**

**B. Section 1404(a): transfer to the District of Oregon.** For the convenience of parties and witnesses and in the interest of justice, a court may transfer a case to any district where it might have been brought, or to which all parties consent. "Might have been brought" requires that venue and personal jurisdiction over every defendant would have existed in the transferee district without the defendants' consent (*Hoffman v. Blaski*).

*1. Could the action have been brought in Oregon?*
- **Venue.** Section 1391(b)(1) is unavailable because the defendants do not all reside in one State (Lumen resides in Oregon; Theo, a natural person, resides where he is domiciled, California). But § 1391(b)(2) allows venue where a substantial part of the events occurred. Lumen designed and launched Amber Hour in Portland, and Theo delivered the drawings and trained the designers there. Venue would lie in Oregon.
- **Personal jurisdiction.** Lumen is at home in Oregon. Theo is a California domiciliary, so Oregon needs specific jurisdiction: purposeful contacts from which the claim arises or to which it relates. Theo twice traveled to Portland to deliver the misappropriated drawings and train Lumen's staff. Those deliberate in-forum acts are the misappropriation itself, so specific jurisdiction is satisfied.

The District of Oregon is therefore a proper transferee district.

*2. Balancing the factors.* The court weighs private factors (access to proof, availability and cost of witnesses) and public factors (local interest, familiarity with the governing law, court congestion), and gives weight to the plaintiff's choice of forum.
- **Favoring Oregon.** Lumen's 12 designers and its business records are in Portland. The design and launch decisions were made there.
- **Favoring Los Angeles.** Golden Hour's five key witnesses work in Los Angeles; Theo lives in Pasadena; and the evidence of consumer confusion comes from Los Angeles County stores and customers, the most likely non-party witnesses. Courts generally give the convenience of non-party witnesses more weight than that of a party's own employees, whom the party can produce. Records are largely electronic. On the public side, Los Angeles has a strong interest in protecting a local company and local consumers from confusion, and two of the three claims arise under California statutes, which a California federal court is more familiar with applying.
- **Plaintiff's choice.** Golden Hour's home forum receives deference. Lumen may note that Golden Hour originally chose state court, but it chose Los Angeles, and that geographic choice remains.

*Law after transfer.* If the court did transfer, the Oregon court would apply the law, including the choice-of-law rules, that the California court would have applied (*Van Dusen*). Transfer would therefore change only convenience, not the governing law.

**Conclusion:** the convenience factors are at most evenly split, and a transfer would only shift inconvenience from Lumen to Golden Hour. **The § 1404(a) motion should be denied.**

**C. Forum non conveniens (Mexico).** A court may dismiss when an adequate alternative forum exists, typically abroad, and the private and public interest factors strongly favor trial there. A plaintiff's choice of its home forum receives strong deference, and the fact that the foreign forum's law is less favorable to the plaintiff does not defeat dismissal unless the remedy there is so inadequate that it is no remedy at all (*Gulf Oil*; *Piper*).
- *Adequate alternative forum.* Lumen would have to show that both defendants are amenable to suit in Mexico. Nothing suggests that Theo, a Californian, could be sued there. Courts often condition dismissal on the defendants' consent to jurisdiction and waiver of limitations defenses. Golden Hour's evidence that Mexican law would yield a smaller recovery is not decisive under *Piper*, but it does not need to be.
- *Private factors.* Only the manufacturer's production records and the molds are in Mexico. The central evidence is in the United States: the design and copying (Portland and Pasadena), the misappropriation (Theo), and consumer confusion (Los Angeles). Production records can be obtained through discovery and translated.
- *Public factors.* The claims arise under United States and California statutes protecting a California company and California consumers. Mexico's interest in a contract manufacturer's role is marginal.
- *Deference.* Golden Hour is a domestic plaintiff suing in its home forum, which receives the strongest deference.

**Conclusion:** the factors do not come close to strongly favoring Mexico. **The forum non conveniens motion should be denied.**

**Common mistakes**
1. Applying the forum-defendant rule (§ 1441(b)(2)) to a federal-question removal. It applies only when removal rests solely on diversity.
2. Using § 1367(b) in a federal-question case. Its limits apply only when jurisdiction rests solely on diversity.
3. Testing venue in a removed case under § 1391. Removal fixes venue in the district embracing the state court.
4. Skipping the "might have been brought" step of § 1404(a), especially personal jurisdiction over every defendant in the transferee district.
5. Treating a less favorable foreign law as automatically defeating forum non conveniens, or ignoring the strong deference owed a domestic plaintiff suing at home.

**Accuracy notes.** The answer does not rely on *Royal Canin U.S.A., Inc. v. Wullschleger* (2025) (post-removal amendment dropping federal claims; repo V-035) because Golden Hour has not amended. The § 1441(c)(2) consent rule and § 1446(b)(2)(A) "properly joined and served" requirement are statutory text consistent with R-CIV-013 and R-CIV-014. The weight given to non-party witnesses in § 1404(a) balancing is a common judicial practice, stated as such rather than as a rule.
