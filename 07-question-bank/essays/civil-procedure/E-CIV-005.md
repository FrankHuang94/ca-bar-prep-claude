## Facts

Priya Natarajan owns a home in San Jose, California. In 2024 she hired Summit Ridge Builders, Inc. ("Summit"), a California corporation, to build a second-story addition for $410,000 under a written contract that states: "The parties waive trial by jury in any dispute arising under this agreement." Summit subcontracted the roofing work to Apex Roofing Co. ("Apex").

Summit finished in December 2024. In January 2025, a solar company Priya hired separately installed panels on the new roof. That spring the roof leaked badly. Priya withheld the final $85,000, blaming Summit's roofing. In March 2025, Summit's president, Carl Voss, posted on a public online forum for contractors: "Homeowner Priya Natarajan of San Jose is a deadbeat who stiffs every contractor she hires. Avoid her."

**Suit 1.** In April 2025, Summit sued Priya in Santa Clara County Superior Court for breach of contract, seeking the $85,000. Priya answered and filed a cross-complaint against Summit with two counts: (1) breach of contract, alleging that defective roofing caused $140,000 in water damage to her home; and (2) promissory estoppel, based on Carl's oral promise during construction to "fix any leak for free for ten years." Priya did not mention Carl's forum post in her pleadings.

Priya demanded a jury. Summit moved to strike the demand based on the waiver clause. The court denied the motion and seated a 12-person jury. Over Summit's objection, the court also submitted the promissory-estoppel count to the jury. On a special verdict, the jury found that (1) Priya breached the contract by withholding $85,000; (2) the leaks were not caused by defective roofing work but by roof penetrations made by Priya's solar installer; and (3) Priya should not recover on promissory estoppel. On each question, the same nine jurors voted yes and the same three voted no. Judgment was entered for Summit on August 4, 2026, awarding it $85,000 and denying Priya any recovery on her cross-complaint. Priya filed a timely notice of appeal, which is still pending.

**Suit 2.** In October 2026, Priya filed a new action in Santa Clara County Superior Court asserting (1) negligence against Summit for the same $140,000 of water damage; (2) negligence against Apex, which was not a party to Suit 1, for the same damage; and (3) defamation against Summit based on Carl's March 2025 forum post.

Summit argues that the judgment in Suit 1 bars both claims against it. Apex argues that the Suit 1 jury's finding that the leaks were not caused by the roofing work prevents Priya from relitigating causation against it.

## Call of the Question

1. Answer according to California law and note any difference under federal law: (a) Was Priya entitled to a jury trial in Suit 1 despite the contract's jury-waiver clause, and on which claims? (b) Was the 9–3 verdict valid?
2. Answer according to California law and compare federal law: How should the court in Suit 2 rule on (a) Summit's defenses to Priya's negligence and defamation claims, and (b) Apex's issue-preclusion defense?

## Model Answer

Both suits are in California superior court, so California procedure governs. The Seventh Amendment does not bind state courts; the jury right comes from the California Constitution.

### 1. Suit 1: the jury

**A. The right to a jury trial.** Trial by jury is an inviolate right in California (Cal. Const. art. I, § 16). It extends to actions that were triable by jury at common law when the California Constitution was adopted in 1850. Whether an action is legal or equitable depends on its gist (*C & K Engineering*; *Grafton*). The federal test is similar in structure but looks to English practice in 1791 and focuses on whether the remedy sought is legal or equitable (*Terry*).

Summit's claim for the $85,000 contract balance and Priya's cross-claim for $140,000 in contract damages are actions for money damages for breach of contract, the classic common-law action. Both are legal, and **both carry a jury right.**

**B. The waiver clause.** Under *Grafton Partners L.P. v. Superior Court* (Cal. 2005), a pre-dispute contractual waiver of the right to a civil jury is unenforceable in California. A party may waive a jury only by the methods the Legislature has provided in CCP § 631, such as failing to appear, failing to demand a jury, failing to deposit jury fees on time, or consenting in writing or orally in open court after the case is pending.
- *Summit's argument.* Priya, a sophisticated homeowner, signed a clear clause and should be held to her bargain.
- *Priya's response.* The clause was signed before any dispute existed, which is exactly the kind of waiver *Grafton* refuses to enforce. Nothing suggests she waived the jury by any § 631 method; she demanded a jury.

**Conclusion:** the trial court correctly denied Summit's motion to strike. Priya was entitled to a jury on the contract claims. (Federal courts generally enforce a contractual jury waiver that was knowing and voluntary, but in a California court *Grafton* controls.)

**C. The promissory-estoppel count.** Promissory estoppel is an equitable doctrine, and in California it carries no right to a jury trial (*C & K Engineering Contractors v. Amber Steel Co.* (Cal. 1978)). The count rests on Carl's oral promise to "fix any leak for free," enforced without consideration because of reliance. That claim is equitable in its gist. The judge, not the jury, should have decided it. Summit objected, so the issue is preserved. At most, the jury's finding on that count could be treated as advisory, and the judge must make his own findings. Because the jury separately found that the leaks were caused by the solar installer, not by the roofing work, the error is likely harmless: the promise to fix leaks in Summit's work would not reach leaks Summit did not cause. Federal law is less settled. Because the Seventh Amendment test turns chiefly on the remedy sought (*Terry*), a federal court could treat a promissory-estoppel claim for money damages as legal and try it to a jury; if the claim is treated as equitable, any common factual issues are tried to the jury first (*Beacon Theatres*).

**D. The 9–3 verdict.** In a California civil case, **three-fourths of the jury may render a verdict**, and the jury consists of 12 persons unless the parties agree in open court to fewer (Cal. Const. art. I, § 16). Nine of 12 is exactly three-fourths. The same nine jurors agreed on every question, so there is no question of shifting majorities. **The verdict is valid.** In federal court, by contrast, a civil verdict must be unanimous unless the parties stipulate otherwise (FRCP 48(b)), so a 9–3 split would be a hung jury.

### 2. Suit 2: the preclusion defenses

**A. Summit's defense to the negligence claim: claim preclusion under the primary-rights theory.** California defines a cause of action by the **primary right** invaded, not by the legal theory or the transaction. One injury to one primary right gives rise to one cause of action, however many theories or remedies are available. A final judgment on the merits bars a later suit between the same parties or their privies on the same primary right, including theories never raised (*Slater v. Blackwood*; *Mycogen*; *Boeken*).
- *Same primary right.* In Suit 1, Priya's cross-complaint sought $140,000 for water damage to her home caused, she claimed, by defective roofing. In Suit 2 she seeks the same $140,000 for the same water damage, relabeled "negligence." The harm is identical: injury to her property interest in the house from the leaking roof. Priya will argue that a tort duty of care is different from a contract duty. But in California the primary right is defined by the harm suffered, not by the theory. A contract judgment followed by a tort suit for the same harm involves one primary right.
- *Same parties.* Priya and Summit were adverse parties on her cross-complaint, which was a claim she chose to litigate.
- *Final judgment on the merits.* The jury decided her claim on the merits after trial. Whether the judgment is *final* is addressed in Part C.

*Federal comparison.* Under the federal transactional test, a judgment bars every claim arising from the same transaction or series of connected transactions (Restatement (Second) of Judgments § 24). The negligence claim arises from the same construction job and the same leaks, so it would be barred under that test as well.

**Conclusion:** once the Suit 1 judgment becomes final, the negligence claim against Summit is barred.

**B. Summit's defense to the defamation claim.**

*Limitations (not yet raised).* Summit relies only on the Suit 1 judgment, but an action for libel or slander must be brought within one year (CCP § 340(c)). Carl posted in March 2025 and Priya sued in October 2026, so Summit should also plead the statute of limitations, which would independently defeat the claim.

*1. Claim preclusion.* The defamation claim seeks redress for injury to Priya's reputation from Carl's March 2025 post, published on Summit's behalf by its president. That is a different harm from water damage to her house, so it is a **different primary right**, and California's claim preclusion does not bar it, even though it arose out of the same dispute. Under the federal transactional test the result is less clear. The post is a separate event, but it concerns the same payment dispute, and a court weighing whether the facts form a convenient trial unit could go either way.

*2. Compulsory cross-complaint (CCP § 426.30).* Summit has a stronger statutory defense. A defendant who answers a complaint must plead in a cross-complaint any **related cause of action**, one arising out of the same transaction, occurrence or series of transactions or occurrences as the plaintiff's claim, that it has against the plaintiff when it serves its answer. Otherwise it may not assert that claim against the plaintiff in any later action. California courts read "related" broadly, asking whether the claims are logically related.
- *Existing at the time of answer.* Carl posted in March 2025; Summit sued in April 2025; Priya answered afterward. The defamation claim existed when she answered.
- *Against the plaintiff.* The post was made by Summit's president about Summit's dispute, so the claim runs against Summit, the Suit 1 plaintiff.
- *Related?* Summit will argue that the post is about the very nonpayment Summit sued over: whether Priya "stiffs" contractors overlaps directly with whether she was justified in withholding the $85,000, which would be central to any truth defense. Priya will respond that defamation is a distinct occurrence (a public statement weeks after the dispute arose) concerning her reputation, not the contract. Given the logical connection between the statement and the payment dispute, a court would likely treat it as related.
- *Exceptions and relief.* None of the § 426.30 exceptions applies: the court had personal jurisdiction over Priya, and she answered. A party who omits a related claim may seek leave to file it in good faith while the first action is pending (§ 426.50), but Priya never did so, and after a judgment entered on a jury verdict that path is effectively closed.

*Federal comparison.* Rule 13(a) similarly makes a counterclaim arising from the same transaction or occurrence compulsory and bars it if omitted. The logical-relationship test would likely produce the same result.

**Conclusion:** the defamation claim survives primary-rights claim preclusion but is **likely barred by § 426.30**. Summit will argue that the pending appeal does not matter, because the statute bars an omitted related claim "in any other action" based on the failure to plead it, not on the finality of the judgment. Priya will answer that if the Suit 1 judgment is reversed and the case retried, she could still seek leave to file the claim there (§ 426.50), so the bar should not be applied while the appeal is pending. Because the point is unsettled, the prudent ruling is the same as for the negligence claim: stay the defamation claim pending the appeal rather than dismiss it now.

**C. Finality: the pending appeal.** Unlike the federal rule, a California trial-court judgment is **not final for preclusion purposes while an appeal is pending** or until the time to appeal has expired (CCP § 1049; *Samara v. Matar* (Cal. 2018)). Priya's appeal of the Suit 1 judgment is pending. Summit's claim-preclusion defense to the negligence claim is therefore **premature**. The court should not dismiss the claim now. Instead it may stay Suit 2 until the appeal is resolved. If the judgment is affirmed, the bar will apply; if it is reversed, there is no final judgment to bar the claim.

*Federal comparison.* Under the federal majority rule, a judgment is final for preclusion when entered, despite a pending appeal. A federal court would apply claim preclusion now. (If the first judgment had come from a California state court, a federal court would still have to give it only the effect California courts would give it, under 28 U.S.C. § 1738.)

**D. Apex's issue-preclusion defense.** In California, issue preclusion applies when (1) the issue is identical to one decided in a prior proceeding; (2) it was actually litigated; (3) it was necessarily decided; (4) the prior decision is final and on the merits; and (5) the party against whom preclusion is sought was a party, or in privity with a party, to the prior proceeding. It also must serve the doctrine's public-policy purposes (*Lucido*). Mutuality is not required, so a non-party may use it defensively (*Bernhard v. Bank of America*).
- *Identical issue.* In Suit 1 the jury decided whether the leaks were caused by defective roofing work. Apex performed that roofing work as Summit's subcontractor. In Suit 2, Priya's negligence claim against Apex depends on proving that Apex's roofing caused the same leaks. The issue is identical.
- *Actually litigated and necessarily decided.* Causation was contested at trial and resolved by an express special-verdict finding that the solar installer's penetrations caused the leaks. That finding was necessary to the judgment rejecting Priya's cross-complaint.
- *Party.* Priya, the party against whom preclusion is asserted, was a party to Suit 1 and had every incentive to litigate causation fully, with $140,000 at stake.
- *Non-mutual defensive use.* Apex was not a party, but California abandoned mutuality, and defensive use by a new defendant against a plaintiff who has already lost the issue is the most accepted form. Using it serves judicial economy and avoids inconsistent judgments, so public policy favors it. Priya may argue that a 9–3 verdict is a weak basis for preclusion, but a valid California verdict is a full determination.
- *Final judgment.* This element fails for now. Because Priya's appeal is pending, the Suit 1 judgment is not final in California.

*Federal comparison.* Federal law likewise permits defensive non-mutual issue preclusion (*Blonder-Tongue*), and under the federal finality rule Apex could invoke it now.

**Conclusion:** Apex's defense is **premature** but likely to succeed once the appeal is resolved against Priya. The court should stay the claim against Apex pending the appeal rather than reject the defense outright.

**Common mistakes**
1. Enforcing a pre-dispute jury waiver in a California court. *Grafton* holds it unenforceable.
2. Giving a jury on promissory estoppel. In California it is equitable (*C & K Engineering*).
3. Treating the negligence claim as a new cause of action because the theory changed. California's primary right is defined by the harm.
4. Stopping at "different primary right" for the defamation claim. Section 426.30 can bar a related claim even when claim preclusion would not.
5. Applying the federal finality rule in California. A California judgment on appeal is not yet final for preclusion.

**Accuracy notes.** The answer does not rely on whether personal injury and property damage from one act are separate primary rights in tort (repo V-976; the case once cited for it, *Holmes*, is a contract case, V-155 resolved) or on California's order of trial in mixed legal and equitable cases (V-158). The remark that federal courts treat contractual jury waivers differently is deliberately general and is not a graded point. The "logical relationship" reading of § 426.30's "related cause of action" is the broad construction California courts use, stated as a test for argument; a grader should accept either conclusion on relatedness if reasoned. Whether § 426.30's bar applies before the first judgment is final is not addressed by R-CIV-109; the answer presents both arguments and recommends a stay, and either reasoned conclusion should earn credit. The limitations point (CCP § 340(c)) is noted for completeness and is not separately graded.
