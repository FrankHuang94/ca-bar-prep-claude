# E-CIV-002 — The leaking solar roof

## Facts

Priya Desai owns a house in Fresno County, California. In March 2024 she signed a written contract with BrightPath Solar, Inc., a California corporation, to install rooftop solar panels for $32,000. She paid $24,000 up front; the remaining $8,000 was due "upon final inspection," which took place in May 2024. BrightPath's final invoice, which Priya received that month, included the line: "Roof penetrations and flashing — Apex Roofing (subcontractor) — $2,100." Priya looked only at the invoice total and assumed BrightPath's own crew had done all the work on her roof. She withheld the $8,000 because the system's monitoring app showed output about 30% below the minimum stated in the contract.

In January 2025, heavy rain leaked through the roof around the panel mounts, damaging ceilings and floors. Priya reported the leak three weeks later, after mold appeared. Her repair costs, including $18,000 for mold remediation, total $70,000.

On February 3, 2025, Priya filed a complaint in Fresno County Superior Court against "BrightPath Solar, Inc. and Does 1 through 10." It alleged three causes of action, each against "Defendants, and each of them":
- **Breach of contract:** "Plaintiff and BrightPath entered into an agreement under which BrightPath agreed to install a solar energy system at plaintiff's home. BrightPath breached the agreement, causing damages in excess of $35,000."
- **Negligence:** "In April 2024, Defendants negligently installed the roof penetrations and flashing for the solar system, causing rainwater to enter plaintiff's home in January 2025 and damage its ceilings, walls and floors."
- **Fraud:** "BrightPath fraudulently induced plaintiff to enter the agreement by making false statements about the system, on which plaintiff relied to her detriment."

After meeting and conferring with Priya's counsel, BrightPath filed a timely demurrer to all three causes of action. On the same day, it served and filed a separate motion for sanctions under Code of Civil Procedure section 128.7, arguing that the fraud cause of action was frivolous.

In August 2025, BrightPath produced in discovery its subcontract with Apex Roofing, Inc. and an internal email from its installer stating: "Apex skipped the flashing sealant on the rear array." On September 15, 2025, Priya filed an amendment substituting Apex for Doe 1 in the negligence cause of action. Apex first learned of the lawsuit when it was served later that month. Assume that the limitations period on Priya's negligence claim against Apex expired before September 15, 2025, unless the amendment relates back.

On October 1, 2025, BrightPath answered. Its lawyer overlooked the unpaid $8,000 and filed no cross-complaint. In January 2026, BrightPath filed a separate action against Priya in the same superior court to recover the $8,000.

In March 2026, BrightPath served and filed, 60 days before the hearing date, a motion for "summary adjudication that plaintiff cannot recover the $18,000 she spent on mold remediation," supported by a separate statement and a declaration that mold grows only when water damage goes untreated for more than two weeks.

## Call of the Question

Answer questions 1, 3 and 4 according to California law.

1. How should the court rule on BrightPath's demurrer and on its motion for sanctions? Discuss.
2. Does Priya's substitution of Apex for Doe 1 relate back to the date of her original complaint? Would the result differ under the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure? Discuss.
3. How should the court rule on BrightPath's motion for summary adjudication? Discuss.
4. Can BrightPath recover the $8,000 in its separate action? Discuss.

## Model Answer

### 1. The demurrer and the sanctions motion

**California pleading and the demurrer.** A California complaint must contain a statement of the facts constituting each cause of action in ordinary and concise language (CCP § 425.10). This is fact pleading: the plaintiff must allege the ultimate facts supporting every element, which is more than federal notice pleading requires under FRCP 8(a) and *Twombly*/*Iqbal*. A defendant tests the complaint by demurrer, limited to the face of the pleading and judicially noticeable matter, filed within 30 days of service after meeting and conferring (CCP §§ 430.10, 430.30, 430.41). A general demurrer for failure to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action is never waived; special demurrers, such as uncertainty or failure to state whether a contract is written, oral or implied, are waived if not raised. BrightPath met and conferred and demurred on time.

**Fraud: general demurrer.** Fraud requires a misrepresentation, knowledge of falsity, intent to induce reliance, justifiable reliance and damage, and California requires fraud to be pleaded with particularity: the complaint must allege facts showing how, when, where, to whom and by what means the representations were made (*Lazar v. Superior Court*). Priya alleges only that BrightPath made "false statements about the system" on which she relied. She does not say what was said, by whom, when, or why it was false or known to be false. Priya will argue that the court should read the pleading liberally, since the output shortfall shows something was misrepresented. But the specificity rule exists because fraud charges are serious, and her allegation is a bare conclusion. (A federal court would reach the same result under FRCP 9(b).) **The general demurrer to the fraud cause of action should be sustained.** Because Priya could likely add specific facts, such as a sales representative's promised output figure, the court should grant **leave to amend**, which California courts give liberally when there is a reasonable possibility of cure (*Blank v. Kirwan*).

**Contract: special demurrer.** In an action on a contract, a defendant may specially demur if it cannot be ascertained from the pleading whether the contract is written, oral or implied by conduct (CCP § 430.10). Priya alleges only that the parties "entered into an agreement." She does not say that it was written, although it was. BrightPath raised the ground on time, so it is not waived. **The special demurrer should be sustained with leave to amend**, an easy cure: Priya need only allege the written contract and attach it or plead its terms. Her allegation of breach is also thin, since she does not say *how* BrightPath breached (for example, the output shortfall or the leaks), which supports requiring an amendment.

**Negligence: demurrer overruled.** BrightPath may argue that "negligently installed" is a conclusion. But the count identifies the act (installing roof penetrations and flashing), the time (April 2024), and how it caused harm (rain entering the home in January 2025 and damaging ceilings and floors). Those are the ultimate facts; evidentiary detail is not required. **The demurrer to the negligence count should be overruled.**

**Sanctions under CCP § 128.7.** By presenting a pleading, an attorney certifies after reasonable inquiry that its legal contentions are warranted and its factual contentions have, or are likely to have, evidentiary support. A sanctions motion must be made separately and must first be **served** on the opposing party; it may be filed only if the challenged paper is not withdrawn or corrected within 21 days after service (§ 128.7(c)(1)). BrightPath served and filed its motion on the same day, denying Priya the safe harbor in which she could have withdrawn or amended the fraud count. The motion also targets a claim that is under-pleaded rather than clearly frivolous: an output 30% below the contracted minimum gives some factual basis for a misrepresentation theory. **The court should deny the sanctions motion** for failure to comply with the safe harbor. The federal Rule 11(c)(2) procedure is the same.

### 2. Relation back of the Doe amendment

**California: CCP § 474.** A plaintiff who is genuinely ignorant of a defendant's identity when she files may sue that defendant under a fictitious "Doe" name and later substitute the true name. The amendment relates back to the original filing date if (1) the original complaint charged the Doe defendants with the cause of action, (2) the amended complaint rests on the same general set of facts and the same injury, and (3) the plaintiff was actually ignorant, when she filed, of the defendant's identity *or of the facts giving rise to its liability*.

- *Doe defendants charged.* The negligence count is pleaded against "Defendants, and each of them," which includes Does 1 through 10.
- *Same general facts.* The amendment substitutes Apex into the same negligence count about the same flashing and the same January 2025 leak.
- *Genuine ignorance.* This is the contested element. Apex will argue that Priya was not ignorant: the May 2024 invoice, which she had nine months before filing, named "Apex Roofing (subcontractor)" for "Roof penetrations and flashing," the very work her complaint says was negligent. Priya will respond that ignorance under § 474 includes ignorance of the facts that make a known person liable, not just ignorance of a name. She believed BrightPath's own crew did the roof work, and the invoice line did not tell her that Apex's work, rather than BrightPath's mounting of the panels, caused the leak. She learned that only from the August 2025 email ("Apex skipped the flashing sealant"). Courts generally ask what the plaintiff actually knew when she filed, not what she should have known, and Priya substituted Apex within about a month after learning the facts. Apex will reply that some courts refuse § 474 relief to a plaintiff who once had the information and simply failed to review documents readily available in her own files, and the invoice was exactly such a document.

Because Priya never registered Apex's role, and the invoice disclosed only that Apex did some of the roof work, not that its work was defective or caused the leak, a court applying the actual-knowledge test will likely find that she was genuinely ignorant of the facts giving rise to Apex's liability. **The amendment likely relates back**, but this is a close question: a court that treats the invoice as readily available information Priya was obliged to review could deny relation back.

**Under the Federal Rules.** FRCP 15(c)(1) allows an amendment to relate back if (A) the law that provides the limitations period allows relation back, (B) the amendment arises from the same conduct, transaction or occurrence, or (C) it changes the party against whom the claim is asserted, and within the Rule 4(m) period (90 days after filing) the new party received notice of the action so that it will not be prejudiced and knew or should have known that it would have been sued but for a mistake concerning the proper party's identity (*Krupski*).

- *Rule 15(c)(1)(C).* The claim arises from the same occurrence. But Apex first learned of the suit when served in September 2025, more than seven months after the February 3 filing, well outside the 90-day window. In addition, many federal courts hold that naming a "Doe" because the plaintiff does not know who is liable is a lack of knowledge, not a "mistake" about identity. **Relation back under 15(c)(1)(C) would fail.**
- *Rule 15(c)(1)(A).* If the federal court were applying a California limitations period, for example in a diversity case, it could look to California's relation-back law, including § 474, through this provision.

**So the result would likely differ** in a federal court applying federal law alone: California's Doe practice is much more forgiving to plaintiffs than Rule 15(c)(1)(C).

### 3. The motion for summary adjudication

**Standard.** Summary judgment is granted if all the papers show no triable issue as to any material fact and the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law (CCP § 437c(c)). A defendant meets its initial burden by showing that an element cannot be established or that a complete defense exists (§ 437c(p)(2); *Aguilar*).

**Scope of summary adjudication.** California allows summary adjudication only if the motion completely disposes of a cause of action, an affirmative defense, a claim for punitive damages, or an issue of duty (§ 437c(f)(1)). BrightPath asks the court to rule that Priya "cannot recover the $18,000 she spent on mold remediation." That is one item of damages within the negligence cause of action. Granting it would leave the negligence claim, and the rest of her $70,000 in damages, for trial. BrightPath may argue that efficiency favors resolving the mold issue now, but the statute does not allow piecemeal adjudication of damage items. **The motion is improper and should be denied.**

**Procedure and merits.** The motion also fails procedurally: a motion for summary judgment or adjudication must be served at least 81 days before the hearing, and BrightPath gave only 60. On the merits, whether Priya acted reasonably in reporting the leak three weeks later, and whether the delay rather than the leak itself caused the mold, are disputed questions of fact about mitigation and causation; the expert declaration would not eliminate a triable issue.

**Federal contrast.** Under FRCP 56(a), a party may move for summary judgment on "part of" a claim or defense, so a federal court could entertain a motion directed at one category of damages, and the motion's timing would be governed by Rule 56(b) and the scheduling order rather than an 81-day notice period.

### 4. BrightPath's separate action for $8,000

**Compulsory cross-complaint (CCP § 426.30).** A defendant who files an answer must plead in a cross-complaint any related cause of action it has against the plaintiff at the time it serves the answer; if it does not, it may not assert that claim against the plaintiff in any later action. A cause of action is "related" if it arises out of the same transaction, occurrence or series of transactions or occurrences as the plaintiff's claim (§ 426.10(c)).

- *Answer filed.* BrightPath answered on October 1, 2025.
- *Existing claim.* The $8,000 became due "upon final inspection" in May 2024, long before the answer, so the claim existed when BrightPath answered.
- *Related.* Priya's complaint alleges breach of the installation contract and negligent installation. BrightPath's claim is for the unpaid price under the same contract for the same installation. BrightPath may argue its claim is a simple debt, separate from Priya's complaints about leaks. But both parties' claims arise from the same contract and the same performance, and Priya withheld the $8,000 because of the alleged underperformance, so the issues overlap heavily.
- *No exception.* The court had personal jurisdiction over BrightPath and BrightPath answered, so § 426.30(b) does not apply.

**The separate action is barred**, and Priya should plead § 426.30 as an affirmative defense in it.

**Relief through § 426.50.** BrightPath is not without a remedy. While Priya's action is pending, a party who failed to plead a related cause of action may apply for leave to file a cross-complaint, and the court must grant leave to a party acting in good faith; the statute is liberally construed to avoid forfeiture. BrightPath's lawyer simply "overlooked" the claim, which is the kind of good-faith omission the statute forgives. **BrightPath should dismiss its separate action and move for leave to file a cross-complaint in Priya's case, which the court will very likely grant.** The federal analogue, the compulsory counterclaim rule of FRCP 13(a), would produce the same bar, with amendment under Rule 15 as the cure while the first case is pending.

**Common mistakes**
1. Calling the California attack a "motion to dismiss." In superior court it is a demurrer (general or special).
2. Treating knowledge of a name as automatically defeating § 474. Ignorance of the facts giving rise to liability also qualifies.
3. Granting summary adjudication of a single item of damages. Only whole causes of action, affirmative defenses, punitive-damages claims or duty issues qualify.
4. Forgetting § 426.50. The compulsory cross-complaint bar is harsh, but leave to file must be granted for a good-faith omission while the action is pending.
5. Filing and serving a § 128.7 motion together. The 21-day safe harbor runs from service before filing.
