# E-TRT-005 — The quarry

## Facts

Ridgeview Aggregates, Inc. ("Ridgeview") operates a limestone quarry on 120 acres outside the town of Calder. The quarry opened in 1985, when the area was farmland. Since 2010, houses on one-acre lots have been built along its eastern boundary. The county zones the quarry site for "extractive industry." Ridgeview blasts rock with explosives about twice a week. It uses licensed blasters, monitors ground vibration, and has never before had a blasting accident.

Calder County Code § 8.20 provides: "Any person conducting blasting within 1,000 feet of an occupied dwelling shall (a) deliver written notice of the date and approximate time of the blast to each such dwelling at least 24 hours before the blast, and (b) sound a warning siren for at least one minute immediately before detonation." The ordinance states that its purpose is "to protect residents near blasting operations and their property from injury." Ridgeview ordinarily leaves notices in residents' mailboxes the day before each blast.

On Tuesday, March 3, 2026, Ridgeview opened a new quarry face 700 feet from the house of Nadia Ortiz. Its foreman, who was behind schedule, skipped the written notices; the siren sounded. The blast threw a 20-pound rock over the quarry's 12-foot earthen berm. The rock crashed through the roof of Nadia's garage and destroyed her car, worth $18,000. Nadia always parks in her garage; the car would have been there whether or not notice had been given.

Owen Pratt lives 900 feet from the new face and keeps two show horses in a paddock behind his house. Whenever he receives a blasting notice, he moves the horses into his barn, where they stay calm. On March 3, with no notice, the horses were in the paddock. At the blast, his mare, Juniper, bolted through the paddock fence, fell and broke a leg, and she had to be put down. Juniper was worth $45,000. Owen knew that Ridgeview often blasted on Tuesdays, but says he "relied on the notices."

Ridgeview's back acreage, which it is not currently mining, includes a dirt road leading to an old excavation. At a bend in the road, a 40-foot sheer drop-off sits behind tall brush; it cannot be seen until a person is a few feet from the edge. A chain-link fence around the property has a large gap. Ridgeview's security reports have noted for at least two years that local teenagers use the gap to ride bicycles on the dirt road. A "No Trespassing" sign hangs on the fence near the gap. Ridgeview never repaired the gap or put a barrier or warning near the drop-off; its site manager wrote in an email that "the kids know they're not supposed to be there."

On March 14, Theo Kim, age 14, rode his bicycle through the gap and down the dirt road for the first time. He rounded the bend at speed and went over the drop-off, suffering serious spinal injuries.

## Call of the Question

1. What claims may Nadia and Owen bring against Ridgeview, and what defenses may Ridgeview raise? Discuss.
2. What claims may Theo bring against Ridgeview, and what defenses may Ridgeview raise? Discuss.

## Model Answer

### 1. Nadia and Owen v. Ridgeview

#### Nadia v. Ridgeview

**A. Strict liability: abnormally dangerous activity.** One who carries on an abnormally dangerous activity is strictly liable for harm to the person or property of another resulting from it, even though he exercised the utmost care, but only for the kind of harm whose possibility makes the activity abnormally dangerous (R2d §§ 519, 520). Courts weigh the § 520 factors:

- *(a) High risk and (b) great harm.* Blasting throws rock and debris and can cause concussion damage; a 20-pound rock landing 700 feet away can kill.
- *(c) Risk not eliminable by reasonable care.* Ridgeview used licensed blasters, a 12-foot berm and vibration monitoring, yet the rock still cleared the berm. That shows that care reduces but cannot remove the risk.
- *(d) Not a matter of common usage.* Few people use explosives.
- *(e) Appropriateness to the place.* Ridgeview will argue that the quarry was there first (1985), that the site is zoned for extraction, and that limestone must be blasted where it lies. Nadia will respond that houses have adjoined the quarry for 15 years and that Ridgeview chose to open a new face only 700 feet from her home.
- *(f) Value to the community.* Aggregate production supplies construction materials and jobs, but it does not outweigh the danger of throwing rocks into a residential area.

Courts almost uniformly treat blasting as abnormally dangerous. **Ridgeview is subject to strict liability.**

**B. Scope of the risk and defenses.** A flying rock landing on a neighbor's property is exactly the harm that makes blasting dangerous, and Nadia, a neighbor 700 feet away, is a foreseeable plaintiff. The blast was the actual and proximate cause of the $18,000 loss.

- *Due care and safety record.* Ridgeview's precautions and accident-free history are irrelevant to strict liability.
- *Zoning.* Zoning permission is not a license to injure neighbors; at most it bears on factor (e).
- *Coming to the quarry.* That Nadia's house was built after the quarry opened is not a defense to strict liability; it is at most another factor under (e).
- *Comparative fault.* Comparative fault reduces strict-liability damages in most jurisdictions, but Nadia did nothing unreasonable; parking in her own garage is not fault.

**Ridgeview is liable to Nadia for $18,000.**

**C. Negligence per se and trespass.** Ridgeview violated the notice ordinance, but a statutory violation creates liability only if it caused the harm. Nadia's car would have been in the garage whether or not notice had been given, so the violation was not a but-for cause, and **negligence per se fails as to Nadia.** Nothing suggests that the blast itself was carelessly conducted. Trespass to land requires an intent to enter or substantial certainty that the rock would land on her property, which Ridgeview did not have; an unintended entry is actionable only under negligence or strict-liability rules. Strict liability is Nadia's claim.

#### Owen v. Ridgeview

**D. Strict liability: the "kind of harm" limit.** Ridgeview will argue that Juniper was not struck by rock or shaken by ground concussion. She panicked at the noise and injured herself running through a fence. Courts in the classic cases about ranched animals frightened by distant blasting have refused strict liability because fright of an animal is not the risk that makes blasting abnormally dangerous. Owen will respond that the blast's noise and shock are themselves inherent in blasting, that a horse 900 feet away is a foreseeable victim, and that the ordinance itself treats blasting as a danger to residents' "property." Keeping horses in a rural area is not an abnormally sensitive activity of the kind that defeats strict liability (R2d § 524A).

The point is close, but **most courts would likely deny strict liability** for harm that comes only from an animal's fright. Owen's stronger claim is negligence per se.

**E. Negligence per se.** A statute or ordinance that imposes a duty may set the standard of care if it was designed to protect the class of persons that includes the plaintiff against the type of harm the plaintiff suffered. Under the majority rule an unexcused violation is negligence per se, establishing duty and breach; causation and damages must still be proved.

- *Clear standard.* Section 8.20(a) requires written notice to each dwelling within 1,000 feet at least 24 hours before a blast.
- *Protected class.* Owen lives 900 feet from the new face, so he is within the class the ordinance protects.
- *Type of harm.* The ordinance protects "residents near blasting operations *and their property* from injury." Ridgeview will argue that the notice rule targets human safety from debris, not the management of livestock. But advance notice exists precisely so that residents can protect themselves and their property from the blast's effects, for example by bringing animals inside. A horse injured in a panic caused by the blast is property injured by the blast, the very harm the notice was meant to let residents prevent.
- *Violation and excuse.* A violation is excused if compliance was impossible or would create greater danger, or in an emergency not of the actor's making. Being behind schedule is a matter of convenience, not an excuse. Sounding the siren complied with paragraph (b) but does not cure the violation of paragraph (a); one minute of warning is not enough to move horses.

In California, an unexcused violation of a statute meeting these conditions raises a **presumption of negligence** (Evid. Code § 669), which Ridgeview could rebut only by showing it did what a reasonably prudent person who wanted to comply would have done. It cannot, because its foreman deliberately skipped the notices. Independently, Ridgeview departed from its own customary practice of notifying residents, which is evidence of ordinary negligence. **Ridgeview breached its duty to Owen.**

**F. Causation, comparative fault and damages.**
- *Actual cause.* But for the missing notice, Owen would have moved the horses into the barn, as he always did, and Juniper would not have bolted.
- *Proximate cause.* An animal hurt because its owner had no chance to secure it is within the scope of the risk created by failing to give notice.
- *Comparative fault.* Ridgeview will argue that Owen knew it often blasted on Tuesdays and should have stabled the horses anyway. Owen reasonably relied on Ridgeview's consistent practice of giving notice, so any fault is small. Under pure comparative negligence (the MBE default and California's rule under *Li*), any fault assigned to Owen reduces, but does not bar, his recovery.

**Owen likely recovers $45,000, reduced by any percentage of fault the jury assigns to him.**

### 2. Theo v. Ridgeview

**A. Duty to trespassers under the common-law categories.** A possessor owes an undiscovered trespasser only a duty to refrain from willful or wanton conduct. To discovered or anticipated trespassers, including people who constantly intrude on a limited area, the possessor must use reasonable care in its activities and must warn of or make safe known, man-made conditions that involve a risk of death or serious bodily harm and that trespassers are unlikely to discover.

- *Status.* Theo entered through a gap past a "No Trespassing" sign, so he was a trespasser.
- *Anticipated trespassers.* Ridgeview's own security reports showed for two years that teenagers used this gap and this road. That is constant intrusion on a limited area. Ridgeview will argue that Theo had never been there before, but the duty runs to the anticipated class of people using that route, and Theo was one of them.
- *Condition.* The drop-off is an artificial excavation, known to Ridgeview, and a 40-foot sheer drop threatens death or serious injury. Because it sits behind brush at a bend and cannot be seen until a few feet away, a rider is unlikely to discover it in time.
- *Breach.* A fence repair, a barrier or a warning sign would have cost little. Ridgeview did nothing, and its manager's email ("the kids know they're not supposed to be there") shows a conscious choice not to act, not an oversight.
- *Causation.* A barrier or warning would likely have stopped Theo before the edge.

**Ridgeview likely breached its duty to Theo as an anticipated trespasser.**

**B. Attractive nuisance.** A possessor is liable to trespassing children for an artificial condition if it knows children are likely to trespass there, the condition poses an unreasonable risk of death or serious harm, the children because of their youth do not discover or appreciate the risk, the burden of eliminating it is slight compared with the risk, and the possessor fails to use reasonable care (R2d § 339).

Most elements are met: Ridgeview knew of the teenagers, the condition was artificial and deadly, and a barrier was cheap. The weak element is youth. A 14-year-old understands that cliffs are dangerous and that trespassing is forbidden, and courts seldom extend the doctrine to teenagers. Theo failed to see the danger because it was hidden, not because he was too young to appreciate it. **Attractive nuisance is doubtful, but Theo does not need it**, because the anticipated-trespasser rule already imposes a duty.

**C. California: *Rowland* and Civil Code § 846.** California has abolished the trespasser, licensee and invitee categories. Under Civil Code § 1714 and *Rowland v. Christian*, a possessor owes every entrant reasonable care in managing its property, and the entrant's status is only one factor. A known, hidden 40-foot drop on a road regularly used by teenagers, which could be guarded cheaply, is a strong case of unreasonable management.

California's recreational-use statute, however, changes the analysis. Under Civil Code § 846, an owner owes no duty to keep its land safe for, or to warn, persons who enter for a recreational purpose, unless (1) it willfully or maliciously failed to guard or warn against a dangerous condition, (2) the entrant paid consideration, or (3) it expressly invited the entrant. Riding a bicycle for fun is recreational riding, so § 846 applies, and Theo must prove a willful failure to guard or warn.

Ridgeview will argue that it posted a "No Trespassing" sign and at most failed to act, which is not willful misconduct. Theo will respond that Ridgeview knew of this specific hidden hazard, knew from two years of security reports that teenagers rode the road, and deliberately chose not to repair the fence or post a warning because "the kids know." That is a conscious failure to guard against a known danger. **Theo likely fits the willful-failure exception, though § 846 makes his California case harder than his common-law case.**

**D. Theo's fault and damages.** A child is held to the standard of a reasonable child of like age, intelligence and experience. Riding a bicycle is not an adult activity, so that standard applies. A reasonable 14-year-old riding an unfamiliar dirt road past a "No Trespassing" sign should slow down at a blind bend, so the jury may assign Theo some fault. Under pure comparative negligence, that fault reduces his recovery but does not bar it. Assumption of risk fails, because Theo did not know of the drop-off. Theo recovers his medical expenses, lost earning capacity and pain and suffering from his spinal injuries, reduced by his share of fault.

**Common mistakes**
1. Letting Ridgeview's precautions defeat strict liability. Due care is irrelevant to an abnormally dangerous activity.
2. Using negligence per se for Nadia. Her loss would have happened with notice, so the violation did not cause it.
3. Ignoring the "kind of harm" limit for the frightened horse.
4. Treating Theo as an undiscovered trespasser when Ridgeview's reports showed constant use of the route.
5. Stating *Rowland* as the end of the California analysis without § 846 recreational-use immunity and its willful-failure exception.
