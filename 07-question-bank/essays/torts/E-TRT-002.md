# E-TRT-002 — The "Tip-Safe" patio heater

## Facts

Hearth & Field Co. manufactures the WarmSpire, a seven-foot propane patio heater. The box and the company's advertisements state in large print: "TIP-SAFE™ — automatic shutoff the instant it tips." Each heater contains a tilt switch designed to cut off the gas if the heater leans more than 15 degrees.

The WarmSpire has a 15-inch-diameter base, which makes it light and easy to move. Most competing heaters use a 22-inch weighted base, which adds about $25 to the cost and makes a heater harder to tip but heavier to move. In the year before Leo's purchase, Hearth & Field received 14 reports of WarmSpire heaters tipping over when bumped or in wind; it made no design change. The 28-page owner's manual says on page 14, in ordinary type: "For outdoor use only. Do not use under a roof, awning or covered patio. Keep 3 feet from combustibles." No warning appears on the heater itself.

Leo Grant bought a WarmSpire for $399 from Outdoor Depot, a national home-improvement chain, which sold it in the manufacturer's sealed box. Leo chose it over a cheaper model because of the "TIP-SAFE" claim on the box. He did not read the manual. He set the heater up on his back patio under a pergola with a solid plastic roof, about two feet from a wooden railing. Many homeowners use patio heaters on covered patios.

One evening, Leo was sitting on the patio with his friend Mia when Leo's 70-pound dog bumped the heater and it toppled over. The gas did not shut off. The burner flame ignited the wooden railing, and the fire spread to the plastic roof, which melted and dripped onto Leo, causing second-degree burns to his arms and shoulders. Mia burned her hand badly while trying to smother the flames with a cushion. The fire destroyed the railing and Leo's patio furniture ($8,000) and the heater itself ($399).

After the fire, an engineer examined the heater and found that its tilt switch had a cracked internal contact that kept the switch from closing the valve. The engineer will testify that the crack was present when the switch left the factory and that 99.8% of switches tested from the same production batch worked properly. The engineer will also testify that a 22-inch weighted base would not have tipped from the dog's bump.

Leo has sued Hearth & Field and Outdoor Depot. Mia has sued both companies.

2. What claims may Leo bring against Outdoor Depot? Discuss.
3. May Mia recover against Hearth & Field and Outdoor Depot? Discuss.

## Model Answer

### 1. Leo v. Hearth & Field

#### A. Strict products liability

A commercial seller is strictly liable for physical harm caused by a product that was defective when it left the seller's control, if the product reached the user without substantial change (*Greenman*; R2d § 402A). Hearth & Field manufactures and sells WarmSpire heaters commercially, and the heater reached Leo in a sealed box, unaltered. The question is defect.

**Manufacturing defect.** A product has a manufacturing defect when it departs from its intended design, differing from the other units in its line. The tilt switch was designed to cut off gas when the heater tipped. Leo's switch had a cracked internal contact, present when it left the factory, while 99.8% of switches from the same batch worked properly. That is a classic departure from design. Hearth & Field may argue the crack could have occurred later, for example from the fall itself, but the engineer will testify it existed at the factory, and in any event the malfunction doctrine allows a defect to be inferred when a product fails in a way that ordinarily results from a defect. **The heater had a manufacturing defect.** Care in manufacturing is irrelevant in strict liability.

**Design defect.** A product is defectively designed if (1) it failed to perform as safely as an ordinary consumer would expect when used in an intended or reasonably foreseeable way, or (2) the risks of the design outweigh its benefits, typically shown by a feasible safer alternative (*Barker v. Lull Engineering*; R3d PL § 2(b)).

- *Consumer expectations.* An ordinary consumer, especially one sold a heater advertised as "TIP-SAFE," expects a patio heater not to fall over when brushed by a pet and not to keep burning if it does. Tipping is within everyday experience, so this test applies (*Soule* limits it to cases where consumers can form safety expectations). **The base likely fails this test.**
- *Risk-benefit.* The gravity of the danger is great (an open propane flame on the ground), and its likelihood is shown by 14 tip-over reports in a year. A 22-inch weighted base is feasible, used by most competitors, costs about $25, and would have prevented this tip-over. Hearth & Field will point to the benefits of its design: a lighter, more portable heater, plus the tilt switch as a backup safety feature. But portability is a modest benefit compared with the fire risk, and relying on a single switch that can fail, as it did here, does not make the base safe. In California, once the plaintiff shows the design proximately caused the injury, the burden shifts to the defendant to prove that the benefits outweigh the risks (*Barker*). **The design is likely defective under both tests.**

**Warning defect.** A product is defective if foreseeable risks, known or knowable at distribution, could have been reduced by reasonable warnings and the omission makes the product not reasonably safe; there is no duty to warn of obvious risks.

- *Adequacy.* The only warnings, not to use under a roof and to keep three feet from combustibles, are buried on page 14 of a 28-page manual in ordinary type; nothing appears on the heater, where users actually look when setting it up. Many homeowners use heaters on covered patios, so the risk of that use was foreseeable. The 14 tip-over reports also gave Hearth & Field knowledge of a tip-over risk that it never warned about.
- *Obviousness.* Hearth & Field will argue that everyone knows a flame should not be placed near wood or under a plastic roof. But users reasonably believe a commercially sold heater is designed to be safe in ordinary patio settings, and the specific hazard here, a tip-over without shutoff, was the very risk the "TIP-SAFE" claim told consumers not to worry about.
- *Causation.* Hearth & Field will argue that Leo never read the manual, so a better warning would not have mattered. Leo will respond that a conspicuous label *on the heater* is different from a manual he had no reason to study, and he would have seen it when assembling and lighting the unit. Many courts presume that an adequate warning would have been heeded.

**The warnings were likely inadequate.**

**Causation.** But for the failed switch, the gas would have shut off when the heater fell; but for the narrow base, the dog's bump would not have toppled it. A pet bumping a heater on a residential patio, and use under a covered patio, are foreseeable, so neither is a superseding cause. **Each defect is an actual and proximate cause.**

#### B. Negligence

A manufacturer is liable in negligence if it failed to use reasonable care in the design, manufacture, inspection or labeling of a product and foreseeably injured someone. Hearth & Field received 14 tip-over reports and made no design change and added no warning, and it apparently did not catch a cracked safety switch in testing. A jury could find that a reasonable manufacturer would have redesigned the base, added an on-product warning, or improved switch inspection. **Negligence is likely provable**, though strict liability is easier because it requires no proof of fault.

#### C. Warranty and misrepresentation

- *Express warranty (UCC § 2-313).* An affirmation of fact about the goods that becomes part of the basis of the bargain creates an express warranty. "TIP-SAFE — automatic shutoff the instant it tips" is a specific factual promise, and Leo chose the heater because of it. The heater did not shut off. **Breach.** Leo must give the seller reasonable notice of breach after discovering it (§ 2-607(3)).
- *Implied warranty of merchantability (§ 2-314).* A heater that tips easily and keeps burning is not fit for its ordinary purpose. **Breach.** Hearth & Field may argue lack of vertical privity, because Leo bought from Outdoor Depot; some states still require privity for implied-warranty claims, though the express warranty on the box was addressed to buyers like Leo.
- *Strict liability for public misrepresentation (R2d § 402B).* A seller that publicly misrepresents a material fact about a product's character or quality is liable for physical harm caused by justifiable reliance, even without fraud or negligence. The "TIP-SAFE" claim was material and false as to Leo's unit, and Leo relied on it in choosing the heater and using it around his dog. **Liability likely.**

#### D. Defenses

- *Comparative fault.* Comparative fault applies to strict products liability and reduces, rather than bars, recovery (*Daly*). Leo placed the heater under a plastic roof and two feet from a wooden railing, contrary to the manual, and never read the manual. A jury will likely assign him some fault, particularly for the spread of fire to the roof.
- *Misuse.* Misuse is a defense only if unforeseeable. Using a patio heater on a covered patio is common, as the facts state, and having a pet nearby is ordinary. **Not a complete defense.**
- *Assumption of risk.* This requires that Leo knew of and voluntarily encountered the specific risk. He did not know the heater could tip and keep burning; he believed the opposite. **The defense fails.**

#### E. Damages

Leo may recover for his burns (medical expenses, pain and suffering) and for damage to *other* property, the railing and furniture ($8,000). Loss of the heater itself ($399) is purely economic loss to the product, recoverable in warranty but not in strict liability or negligence (*Seely*). Punitive damages require malice, oppression or fraud, or conscious disregard of safety (in California, by clear and convincing evidence under Civ. Code § 3294). Continuing to sell a heater marketed as "TIP-SAFE" after 14 tip-over reports gives Leo a plausible argument, though whether a corporate managing agent consciously disregarded the danger is a jury question.

### 2. Leo v. Outdoor Depot

**Strict liability.** Every commercial seller in the chain of distribution, including a retailer, is strictly liable for a defective product, even if it sold the product in a sealed box and could not have discovered the defect (*Vandermark*). Outdoor Depot sold the defective WarmSpire, so **it is strictly liable** on the same manufacturing, design and warning theories.

**Warranty.** As a merchant seller of heaters in direct privity with Leo, Outdoor Depot gave the implied warranty of merchantability, which the heater breached. Whether it also adopted the "TIP-SAFE" express warranty printed on the box it sold is arguable; the claim was part of the package Leo bought from it.

**Negligence.** A retailer usually has no duty to inspect sealed goods unless it has reason to know of the danger. Nothing shows Outdoor Depot knew of the tip-over reports, so **a negligence claim likely fails.**

**Indemnity.** Outdoor Depot may obtain indemnity from Hearth & Field, the manufacturer responsible for the defects.

### 3. Mia's claims

**Strict liability.** Strict products liability protects not only users and consumers but also foreseeable bystanders injured by a defective product (*Elmore*). A guest seated on the patio next to the heater is a foreseeable bystander.

**Rescue.** Mia was not burned by the initial fall; she burned her hand trying to smother the fire. Danger invites rescue: a defendant whose product or negligence creates a peril is liable to a person injured in a reasonable attempt to rescue, because a rescue is a foreseeable response, not a superseding cause. Smothering flames with a cushion was a reasonable response to a fire spreading on a patio. If the jury found Mia acted carelessly, her recovery would be reduced, not barred.

**Warranty.** Under UCC § 2-318 (Alternative A), a seller's warranty extends to any natural person who is a guest in the buyer's home and may reasonably be expected to use, consume or be affected by the goods and who is personally injured. Mia was a guest at Leo's home sitting beside the heater, so **the warranties extend to her.** (California's Commercial Code appears not to include § 2318, so a California court would apply case-law privity rules instead [VERIFY V-970].)

**Leo's fault.** Leo's negligence does not reduce Mia's own recovery; only her own fault would. Under joint and several liability (the MBE default), Mia may collect all her damages from either company, which may then seek contribution from Leo if he was negligent. In California, the companies remain jointly and severally liable for Mia's economic damages, but each defendant's liability for her non-economic damages is several only, in proportion to its own fault, so they would not pay the share of those damages assigned to Leo (Civ. Code § 1431.2). **Mia may recover from both Hearth & Field and Outdoor Depot.**

**Common mistakes**
1. Treating Leo's failure to read the manual as ending the warning claim. Ask whether an *on-product* warning would have reached him.
2. Calling covered-patio use "misuse" that bars recovery. Foreseeable misuse is no defense; it goes to comparative fault.
3. Awarding the $399 heater in strict liability. Damage to the product itself is economic loss (warranty only).
4. Excusing the retailer because the box was sealed. Retailers are strictly liable and seek indemnity upstream.
5. Treating Mia's rescue as a superseding cause. Danger invites rescue.
