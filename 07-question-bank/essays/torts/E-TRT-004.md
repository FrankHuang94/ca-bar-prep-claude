# E-TRT-004 — The self-checkout glitch

## Facts

VoltMart operates a large electronics store in the town of Ashford. Under a written "Security Services Agreement," VoltMart engaged Apex Protective Services, Inc. ("Apex") to staff the store with loss-prevention officers. The agreement states that Apex is an independent contractor; that Apex hires, trains, schedules and disciplines its officers; and that VoltMart's store manager "may assign officers to posts and communicate VoltMart's loss-prevention priorities." Apex officers wear navy uniforms with a shoulder patch reading "VoltMart Security."

In 2025, VoltMart's regional office received three written complaints that Apex officers had used excessive force on customers at other VoltMart stores. It renewed the agreement in January 2026 without asking Apex about them. At a staff meeting in February, the Ashford store manager, Ross, told the Apex officers: "Shrinkage is killing us. Nobody walks out with our merchandise—do whatever it takes."

On March 6, 2026, Leo Park, age 19, bought several items at a self-checkout station, including $250 headphones. The scanner beeped for the headphones, but because of a software fault the item did not register and did not appear on his receipt. Leo paid the total shown and put everything in his backpack. Kurt, an Apex officer watching on a monitor, saw Leo put the headphones in the backpack and concluded that he had not paid for them.

Kurt followed Leo into the parking lot. About 40 feet from the door, he grabbed Leo's arm and backpack strap and said: "You're coming back inside, or I call the cops and you go to jail." Leo protested but did not resist. Leo's friend Pia, waiting by her car about 30 feet away at dusk, saw a large man in a dark uniform pulling Leo by the arm. She ran over and yanked Kurt's arm, shouting "Let him go!" Kurt shoved her away with one arm; she fell and broke her wrist. Kurt says he thought she was attacking him. Pia's medical bills total $9,000.

Kurt walked Leo to a windowless back office, handcuffed him to a chair and searched the backpack, finding the headphones and the receipt. When Leo began recording on his phone, Kurt took the phone, deleted the video and put the phone in his pocket.

Twenty minutes later, Ross reviewed the self-checkout video and told Kurt: "He scanned them—it's our scanner glitch. Let him go." Ross then returned to the sales floor. Kurt replied that Leo had "attitude" and kept him handcuffed another 70 minutes. At one point he raised his fist close to Leo's face and said, "Thieves like you deserve a beating." Kurt then released Leo and returned his phone. No police were ever called.

Since the incident, Leo has had repeated panic attacks and has paid $3,000 for counseling.

## Call of the Question

1. What intentional tort claims may Leo bring against Kurt, and what privileges may Kurt assert? Discuss.
2. What claims may Pia and Kurt assert against each other, and what defenses may each raise? Discuss.
3. Are Apex and VoltMart liable for Kurt's conduct? Discuss.

## Model Answer

### 1. Leo v. Kurt

**A. Battery.** A defendant is liable for battery if he acts intending to cause a harmful or offensive contact with the plaintiff's person, and such contact results; the person includes things closely connected to it. Kurt grabbed Leo's arm and the backpack strap on his shoulder and later handcuffed him to a chair. Each contact was intended and would offend a reasonable sense of dignity. **The prima facie case is met**; the question is privilege (below).

**B. Assault.** Assault requires an act intended to cause a harmful or offensive contact or the apprehension of one, which puts the plaintiff in reasonable apprehension of an imminent contact. The parking-lot threat ("or I call the cops and you go to jail") threatened future arrest, not imminent contact. But raising a fist close to the face of a handcuffed man while saying "Thieves like you deserve a beating" created a reasonable apprehension of an imminent blow. Kurt will argue that he never meant to strike. Intent to cause apprehension is enough, and Leo, handcuffed to a chair, had every reason to believe the blow was coming. **This is an assault.**

**C. False imprisonment.** A defendant is liable if he intends to confine the plaintiff within boundaries he fixes, the plaintiff is confined with no reasonable means of escape, and the plaintiff is aware of the confinement or harmed by it. Kurt held Leo by the arm, told him he would go to jail if he did not come back, walked him to a windowless office and handcuffed him to a chair for about 90 minutes. Leo was conscious of it throughout. **The prima facie case is met**, subject to privilege.

**D. Privileges.**

*Recapture of chattels.* A person whose chattel was wrongfully taken may use reasonable force to recapture it in fresh pursuit, but the actor bears the risk of a mistake about the taking. Leo had paid for the headphones; the scanner failed. **Recapture gives Kurt no privilege.**

*Shopkeeper's privilege.* A shopkeeper (and its agent) who reasonably believes a person has stolen merchandise may detain that person for a reasonable time, in a reasonable manner, on or near the premises, to investigate. A reasonable mistake is protected.

- *Reasonable belief.* Kurt saw Leo put the headphones in his backpack, and the system showed no charge for them. Leo will argue that a self-checkout customer bagging his items is normal and Kurt should have checked the transaction first. But seeing an expensive item go into a bag apparently unpaid is a reasonable basis for suspicion. **The belief was likely reasonable.**
- *On or near the premises.* Forty feet from the door, in the store's parking lot, is near the premises.
- *Reasonable manner.* Taking Leo's arm to stop him was probably reasonable. Handcuffing a 19-year-old who "did not resist" to a chair is harder to justify, because restraints were not needed to investigate. This first 20 minutes is arguable, but the force likely exceeded what was reasonable.
- *Reasonable time; end of the privilege.* The privilege lasts only as long as needed to investigate. Once Ross told Kurt that the video showed Leo scanning the headphones, the suspicion was dispelled and the privilege ended. Keeping Leo handcuffed another 70 minutes because he had "attitude" served no investigative purpose.

**At most the first 20 minutes were privileged (and the handcuffing even then is doubtful); the last 70 minutes were false imprisonment and battery, and the fist-raising was an unprivileged assault.**

**E. The phone: trespass to chattels or conversion?** Trespass to chattels is intentional intermeddling with or dispossession of another's chattel that causes actual harm, including deprivation of use for a substantial time or damage. Conversion is an interference so serious that the defendant may fairly be made to pay the chattel's full value, judged by the extent and duration of control, intent and harm.

Kurt took the phone, deleted Leo's video and kept the phone for the rest of the detention, more than an hour. Deleting the recording damaged the phone's contents and deprived Leo of evidence, which is actual harm, and the shopkeeper's privilege does not cover seizing and erasing a customer's property. But Kurt returned the phone in working order after little more than an hour. **This is trespass to chattels, not conversion**, so Leo recovers his actual loss rather than the phone's full value.

**F. Intentional infliction of emotional distress.** A defendant who by extreme and outrageous conduct intentionally or recklessly causes severe emotional distress is liable. Outrageousness is more likely where the defendant abuses a position of authority. Kurt, a uniformed security officer, kept a young man handcuffed for 70 minutes after learning he was innocent, threatened him with a beating, and destroyed his video of the encounter, all out of spite. That is a deliberate abuse of power, not a mere insult. Kurt will argue that a detention lasting about an hour, with no injury, is not beyond all bounds of decency. Leo's repeated panic attacks and the $3,000 in counseling show severe distress. **IIED is a close call that likely succeeds** on these facts.

### 2. Pia and Kurt

**A. Pia v. Kurt: battery and self-defense.** Kurt intentionally shoved Pia, causing harmful contact, so the prima facie battery is clear. Kurt claims self-defense: a person may use reasonable force against force he reasonably believes is about to be used against him, and deadly force only against a threat of death or serious bodily harm.

- *Reasonable belief.* Pia ran up and yanked Kurt's arm, so Kurt reasonably believed he was being attacked.
- *Proportional force.* A single one-arm shove is nondeadly force proportional to an arm-yank. The broken wrist resulted from the fall, but the test is the reasonableness of the force, not the severity of an unexpected injury.

Pia will argue that a large trained officer could have simply pulled free. But the privilege does not require the least possible force, only reasonable force. **Kurt is likely privileged and not liable to Pia.**

**B. Kurt v. Pia: battery and defense of others.** Pia intentionally grabbed Kurt, a battery. She claims defense of others: a person may use reasonable force to defend a third person when she reasonably believes that person would be privileged to defend himself.

- *Majority rule.* A reasonable mistake protects the intervenor. At dusk, from 30 feet away, Pia saw a large man pulling her friend by the arm. Kurt will argue that his uniform and "VoltMart Security" patch should have told her this was a lawful detention. Whether a reasonable person would have seen the patch in poor light, in a few seconds, is a jury question. A brief tug to break the hold was proportional.
- *Minority ("steps into the shoes") rule.* The intervenor has no privilege if the person defended was not actually privileged to resist. At that moment Kurt's detention was likely privileged under the shopkeeper's privilege, so Leo could not resist, and Pia's privilege would fail.

Pia's honest mistake matters only through the privilege: it is not a "negligence" defense, and most states do not apportion fault in an intentional-tort claim. **Under the majority rule Pia is probably privileged; even if not, Kurt's damages are nominal.**

### 3. Liability of Apex and VoltMart

**A. Apex: respondeat superior.** An employer is vicariously liable for its employee's torts committed within the scope of employment: conduct of the kind he was hired to perform, within authorized time and space limits, and motivated at least in part by a purpose to serve the employer. Intentional torts are within scope where force is part of the job or the tort arises from job-related friction. In California the test is whether the tort was engendered by, or a foreseeable outgrowth of, the employment.

Kurt is Apex's employee, since Apex hires, trains, schedules and disciplines him. Stopping, detaining and searching suspected shoplifters is exactly what he was hired to do, on the client's premises and during his shift. Apex will argue that the extra 70 minutes, the threat and the video deletion were spiteful personal acts ("attitude") that served no employer purpose. But they grew directly out of a detention Kurt was conducting for his employer, the deletion arguably protected the employer from a damaging video, and abuse of detention authority is a foreseeable risk of loss-prevention work. This is unlike *Lisa M.*, where a hospital ultrasound technician's sexual assault on a patient during an examination was held outside the scope of employment because it arose from his personal motives, not from any event or condition of the work. **Apex is likely liable for all of Kurt's torts.**

**B. VoltMart: the independent-contractor rule and its exceptions.** One who hires an independent contractor is generally not vicariously liable for its negligence or torts. Exceptions apply to a nondelegable duty (such as a possessor's duty to keep premises open to the public reasonably safe), inherently dangerous work, and apparent agency.

- *Status.* The agreement calls Apex an independent contractor, and Apex controls hiring, training and discipline. VoltMart's manager can assign posts and set priorities, but that is control over results, not the manner of the work. The label is not controlling, but Apex is likely an independent contractor.
- *Nondelegable duty.* Leo was VoltMart's business invitee, and he was detained in VoltMart's own back office. A store that uses security officers to stop and detain its customers cannot escape responsibility for their safe treatment by hiring an outside firm, and loss-prevention work involving force carries a peculiar risk to customers. **This exception likely applies.**
- *Apparent agency.* Apex officers wear "VoltMart Security" patches, so VoltMart held them out as its own staff, and customers reasonably submit to them as the store's agents. Leo's reliance is shown by his compliance ("did not resist").

**VoltMart is likely vicariously liable.**

**C. VoltMart's direct liability.** An employer or principal is liable for its own negligence in selecting or retaining a person it knew or should have known posed the particular risk that caused the harm. VoltMart renewed Apex's contract after three written complaints of excessive force without any inquiry, which is the very risk that occurred here. Ross's instruction, "do whatever it takes," encouraged aggressive detentions. Ross also knew Leo was innocent and that he was still handcuffed in VoltMart's own office, yet he walked away without making sure Leo was released. **These facts support direct negligence by VoltMart**, even apart from vicarious liability.

**D. Damages.** Leo recovers nominal damages for the intentional torts, compensatory damages for his distress, his $3,000 in counseling, and the loss of use and the deleted video. Punitive damages against Kurt are likely, because he acted with malice in keeping an innocent person restrained out of spite (in California, by clear and convincing evidence, Civ. Code § 3294). Apex and VoltMart are liable for punitive damages only if an officer, director or managing agent authorized, ratified or had advance knowledge and consciously disregarded the risk (§ 3294(b)). A single store manager is probably not a managing agent who sets corporate policy, but the regional office's renewal despite three complaints is a stronger basis for punitive damages against VoltMart.

**Scoring on the 40–100 scale** (`00-exam-intel/grader-expectations.md` §4):
- **75+:** runs each tort's elements, then splits the detention into a privileged first phase and an unprivileged second phase; distinguishes recapture from the shopkeeper's privilege; handles both privileges in the Pia–Kurt fight with the majority/minority split; and analyzes Apex and VoltMart separately, including both vicarious-liability exceptions and direct negligence.
- **65:** finds the torts and the shopkeeper's privilege but treats the whole 90 minutes as either privileged or unprivileged, or analyzes only Apex's liability.
- **55:** misses the privilege analysis or treats Apex's independent-contractor label as ending VoltMart's liability.
- **45 or below:** lists torts without elements or privileges.

**Common mistakes**
1. Treating the shopkeeper's privilege as all-or-nothing. It ends once the suspicion is dispelled.
2. Using the recapture privilege, which does not protect a mistake about the taking.
3. Treating the minority "steps into the shoes" rule as the general rule for defense of others.
4. Accepting the contract's "independent contractor" label as the end of VoltMart's liability.
5. Apportioning comparative fault in an intentional-tort claim (most states do not), instead of deciding the privilege question.

**Accuracy notes.** Rules follow R-TRT-002 to -013, -063, -064, -067 and -114. The answer relies on the common-law shopkeeper's privilege and does not cite California's merchant-detention statute (Pen. Code § 490.5(f)), whose text is flagged V-609 in the rulebook. Courts differ on whether security work is "inherently dangerous" for independent-contractor purposes; the nondelegable-duty and apparent-agency grounds are the stronger ones. Whether a store manager is a "managing agent" under § 3294(b) depends on his policy-making authority (*White v. Ultramar*).
