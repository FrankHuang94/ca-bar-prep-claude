# E-TRT-001 — The hedge at Oak and Fifth

## Facts

GreenScape Landscaping, Inc. employs Ray Torres as a crew leader. Ray drives a GreenScape pickup truck between job sites and must return it, with the crew's equipment, to GreenScape's yard at the end of each day. GreenScape's written policies forbid personal errands in company trucks and any phone use while driving. In the past year Ray received two tickets for using a handheld phone while driving, one of them in the company truck. GreenScape's manager knew of both tickets, gave Ray a verbal warning and kept him in his driving role.

On November 18, 2025, at about 5:35 p.m. (sunset was at 4:50 p.m.), Ray finished a job and set out for the yard. Instead of driving directly there, he drove two miles in the opposite direction to pick up his daughter's birthday cake at a bakery. He then headed back toward the yard. About one mile before he would have rejoined his usual route, he approached the intersection of Oak Street and Fifth Street while reading a text from his wife on his handheld phone. A state statute prohibits holding and operating a handheld phone while driving. Fifth Street has a stop sign at Oak.

Paula Chen, 42, was riding her bicycle on Oak Street toward the intersection. A state statute requires a bicycle operated "during darkness" to display a white front headlight. Paula's headlight battery had died that afternoon, and she decided to ride home without it. Her 16-year-old daughter, Emma, was riding her own bicycle about 30 feet behind her. Streetlights lit the intersection.

At the corner, a six-foot hedge on property owned by Nora Patel blocked the view between Oak and Fifth. A city ordinance requires that hedges within 25 feet of a street corner be kept below three feet "to preserve sight lines for motorists, bicyclists and pedestrians." Three months earlier Nora received a city notice that her hedge violated the ordinance. She did not trim it.

Ray rolled through the stop sign at about 15 miles per hour and struck Paula as she entered the intersection. He told police, "I didn't see her until she hit my bumper. I was checking a text." An accident reconstructionist will testify that:
- the hedge hid Paula from drivers on Fifth Street, and hid Fifth Street traffic from Paula, until she was about 20 feet from the intersection;
- a driver who stopped at the stop line and looked left would have seen Paula in time to wait for her, and would have seen her more easily if she had a headlight; and
- with the hedge trimmed, Paula would have seen the truck approaching and could have stopped.

Paula suffered a fractured pelvis and a traumatic brain injury. Her medical bills are $180,000 and her lost wages $60,000, and she continues to suffer pain and memory problems. Emma saw her mother thrown into the air and land motionless in the street. She has since been diagnosed with post-traumatic stress disorder, suffers panic attacks, and missed two months of school.

## Call of the Question

1. What negligence claims may Paula reasonably assert against Ray, GreenScape and Nora, and what defenses may each raise? Discuss.
2. May Emma recover damages for her emotional distress? Discuss.
3. If the jury finds Paula, Ray, GreenScape and Nora each partly at fault, how should Paula's damages be allocated? Discuss.

## Model Answer

Negligence requires (1) a duty of care, (2) breach of that duty, (3) actual and proximate causation, and (4) damages.

### 1. Paula's claims and the defenses

#### A. Paula v. Ray

**Duty.** Everyone owes a duty of reasonable care to foreseeable plaintiffs within the zone of danger created by his conduct (*Palsgraf*); in California, Civil Code § 1714 imposes a general duty of ordinary care. A driver plainly owes it to cyclists on the road.

**Breach.** Ray must act as a reasonably prudent driver would. Reading a text while approaching an intersection at dusk and rolling through a stop sign at 15 mph falls far below that standard, because the risk of hitting someone is high and the burden of putting the phone down and stopping is trivial.

**Negligence per se.** A statute sets the standard of care if it protects the class of persons that includes the plaintiff from the type of harm suffered. The handheld-phone statute exists to prevent collisions caused by distracted driving, and it protects other road users, including cyclists. The stop-sign requirement serves the same purpose at intersections. Ray violated both, with no excuse: no emergency forced him to read a personal text. **Ray is negligent per se** (in California, the violation creates a presumption of negligence under Evidence Code § 669 that Ray cannot rebut).

**Causation.** Ray's conduct is an actual cause if the harm would not have occurred but for it. The expert will testify that a driver who stopped and looked left would have seen Paula in time to wait. Ray did neither. He is also a proximate cause: hitting a cyclist is exactly the foreseeable harm of distracted driving. **Ray caused Paula's injuries.**

**Defense: Paula's comparative negligence.** A plaintiff's own negligence reduces her recovery by her percentage of fault; under pure comparative negligence (the MBE default and California's rule, *Li v. Yellow Cab*) she recovers even if she is mostly at fault.

- *Negligence per se for Paula.* The headlight statute protects road users, including the cyclist herself, from collisions in the dark. Riding 45 minutes after sunset without a light likely violated it. Paula may argue an excuse: her battery died unexpectedly and the intersection was lit by streetlights. But she *decided* to ride without the light knowing it was dead, so the violation is not excused.
- *Causation.* Ray will argue that a headlight would have made Paula visible. Paula will respond that Ray "didn't see her until she hit my bumper" because he was reading a text, so no headlight would have caught his attention. The expert's testimony that a driver who looked would have seen her "more easily" with a light gives the jury some basis to find partial causation.

**Paula is likely partly at fault, but her share will be small**, because the dominant cause was Ray's failure to look or stop.

#### B. Paula v. GreenScape

**Respondeat superior.** An employer is vicariously liable for torts an employee commits within the scope of employment: conduct of the kind he was hired to perform, within authorized time and space limits, and motivated at least in part by a purpose to serve the employer. A minor deviation (*detour*) stays within the scope; a substantial personal venture (*frolic*) does not. California asks whether the tort was engendered by, or a foreseeable outgrowth of, the employment.

Ray is GreenScape's employee, and returning the company truck and equipment to the yard at day's end was part of his job.

- *GreenScape's argument.* Driving two miles in the *opposite* direction to buy a birthday cake was a purely personal errand that its written policy forbids, so Ray was on a frolic. He had not yet rejoined his usual route when the collision happened, a mile away.
- *Paula's argument.* The errand was short and occurred during the trip the job required. Ray had finished the errand and was heading back toward the yard, with the equipment in the truck, to complete his assignment. Many courts treat an employee as re-entering the scope of employment once he has completed the personal errand and resumes travel toward the employer's business, even before reaching the original route. That an employer forbids an act, such as errands or phone use, does not take it outside the scope if it is done while performing the job. Under California's foreseeability test, an employee who drives a company truck at day's end stopping for a quick personal errand is a foreseeable outgrowth of the employment.

Because Ray was carrying GreenScape's equipment back to its yard and had turned back toward it, **the deviation was likely a detour, and GreenScape is likely vicariously liable**, though a jury could find a frolic.

**Direct negligence: retention, supervision and entrustment.** An employer is directly liable if it retains or entrusts a vehicle to an employee it knows or should know poses a particular risk of the harm that occurred. GreenScape's manager knew Ray had two recent handheld-phone tickets, one in the company truck, yet merely gave a verbal warning and left him driving. The risk that materialized, a phone-distracted crash, is exactly the risk GreenScape knew about. GreenScape will respond that a warning and a written policy were reasonable responses. But two violations in a year, one in its own truck, called for more, such as monitoring or reassignment. **GreenScape is likely directly negligent**, which also makes it liable even if Ray was on a frolic.

#### C. Paula v. Nora

**Duty.** Courts are divided on whether, at common law, a landowner owes travelers on an adjacent street a duty to keep vegetation from blocking the view at an intersection; many find no such duty absent a statute or ordinance. Two points answer that concern here. First, the sight-line ordinance itself defines the conduct required of corner owners, and under negligence per se an unexcused violation establishes duty and breach. Second, in California, Civil Code § 1714 imposes a general duty of ordinary care in the management of property, departed from only for strong policy reasons (*Rowland*), and a six-foot corner hedge that hides cyclists from drivers foreseeably endangers travelers. **Nora likely owed Paula a duty.**

**Breach and negligence per se.** The ordinance requires corner hedges below three feet "to preserve sight lines for motorists, bicyclists and pedestrians." Paula, a bicyclist, is within the protected class, and an intersection collision caused by an obstructed view is the very harm the ordinance targets. Nora's six-foot hedge violated it, and she had a notice of violation three months earlier, so she cannot claim ignorance or impossibility. **Nora is negligent per se.**

**Actual cause.** Nora will argue that her hedge made no difference because Ray was reading a text and was not looking at all; a trimmed hedge would not have made him see Paula. But the ordinance protects sight lines in *both* directions. The expert will testify that with the hedge trimmed, *Paula* would have seen the truck approaching and could have stopped. That makes the hedge a but-for cause; at minimum, it was a substantial factor combining with Ray's negligence to produce one indivisible injury.

**Proximate cause and intervening cause.** Nora will argue that Ray's texting and running the stop sign were a superseding cause. An intervening act supersedes only if it is unforeseeable and extraordinary. A negligent driver failing to stop at an obstructed intersection is precisely the risk that makes the hedge dangerous; the ordinance exists because drivers and cyclists need to see each other in case one of them errs. **Ray's negligence is not superseding, and Nora is likely liable.**

### 2. Emma's claim for emotional distress

A bystander may recover for serious emotional distress caused by witnessing negligently caused injury to another if (1) she is closely related to the victim, (2) she is present at the scene of the injury-producing event when it occurs and is then aware that it is causing injury to the victim, and (3) she suffers serious emotional distress beyond that of a disinterested witness (*Dillon v. Legg*; *Thing v. La Chusa*). Many jurisdictions use this test; some still require that the bystander be within the zone of danger.

- *Close relationship.* Emma is Paula's daughter.
- *Presence and contemporaneous awareness.* Emma was riding 30 feet behind her mother and watched her get struck, "thrown into the air" and land motionless. She perceived the injury as it happened.
- *Serious distress.* A diagnosis of PTSD, panic attacks and two months out of school is far beyond the reaction of a disinterested witness.
- *Zone of danger.* Even in a zone-of-danger jurisdiction, Emma, cycling 30 feet behind on the same path toward the same intersection, was arguably at risk of being hit herself.

The defendants may argue that Emma's distress should be reduced to reflect Paula's comparative fault, and some courts do reduce a bystander's recovery that way. **Emma may recover** from Ray, GreenScape (vicariously and directly) and Nora, whose negligence caused the event she witnessed.

### 3. Allocation of Paula's damages

**Damages.** Paula may recover economic damages ($180,000 in medical expenses and $60,000 in lost wages, plus future losses proved with reasonable certainty) and non-economic damages for pain, suffering and the memory problems from her brain injury.

**Comparative fault.** Under pure comparative negligence, Paula's total damages are reduced by her own percentage of fault. For example, if the jury assigns Paula 10%, Ray 60%, GreenScape 10% (direct negligence) and Nora 20%, Paula recovers 90% of her damages.

**Joint and several liability.** When several defendants' negligence produces a single, indivisible injury, as Paula's injuries are, each is jointly and severally liable for the whole of the plaintiff's recoverable damages (the traditional rule and the MBE default). Paula may collect the entire 90% from any defendant, and a defendant who pays more than its share may obtain contribution from the others in proportion to fault.

**Vicarious share.** If Ray was within the scope of his employment, GreenScape is liable for Ray's 60% in addition to its own 10%. As between them, GreenScape may seek indemnity from Ray for the vicarious portion.

**California modification (Proposition 51).** In California, each defendant remains jointly and severally liable for **economic** damages, but liability for **non-economic** damages is several only, in proportion to that defendant's fault (Civ. Code § 1431.2). On the hypothetical percentages, Nora would owe all of Paula's recoverable economic damages if the others could not pay, but only 20% of her non-economic damages. GreenScape, as Ray's employer, would bear Ray's share of non-economic damages as well as its own.

**Common mistakes**
1. Ending Nora's analysis at "Ray wasn't looking, so the hedge didn't matter." Sight lines protect the cyclist's view too.
2. Treating any forbidden errand as a frolic. Ask whether the employee had resumed the employer's business.
3. Forgetting direct negligence (retention/entrustment), which survives even if respondeat superior fails.
4. Using contributory negligence as a complete bar. The MBE default and California use pure comparative fault.
5. Applying Proposition 51 to economic damages. Only non-economic damages become several.
