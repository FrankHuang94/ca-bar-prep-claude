# E-TRT-003 — The Bayview Bulletin

## Facts

Gwen Okafor publishes *The Bayview Bulletin*, an online weekly newsletter about local politics with about 6,000 paid subscribers. In 2026 Victor Lam, owner of Lam's Kitchen, a popular restaurant at 220 Grove Avenue, ran for the Bayview City Council on a promise to "run the city like a business." Gwen supported his opponent.

On September 3, a volunteer for Victor's opponent told Gwen that "Lam's" had been cited for rat droppings. Before writing her story, Gwen downloaded the county health department's public inspection list. It showed a citation for rodent droppings at "Lam's Noodle House, 14 Pier Street," a restaurant with a different owner, and a clean inspection for Lam's Kitchen. In her notes Gwen wrote: "Same family? Check." She did not check. Her deadline was the next morning. Lam's Noodle House has no connection to Victor or his family.

On September 5 the Bulletin's lead story, under Gwen's byline, stated: "Health inspectors found rat droppings in Victor Lam's restaurant kitchen last month." It ended: "Lam is a crook who should never be trusted with the city budget." Next to the story Gwen ran a photograph of Victor in swim trunks, holding a glass of wine beside the pool in his backyard, captioned "Lam relaxes at home." Gwen had taken the photograph with a camera drone that she flew about 30 feet above the backyard, which is enclosed by an eight-foot privacy fence and cannot be seen from the street or from neighboring yards.

The same issue reported: "Lam's wife, Mei, has been treated at Harborview Recovery Center for alcohol addiction since spring." The statement was true. Gwen learned it from a friend who works as a receptionist at the clinic. Mei has never taken part in Victor's campaign or appeared in public with him during it. The article said the information showed that "Lam can't manage his own household, let alone a city."

On September 12, at a candidates' forum in the public library attended by about 80 residents, Gwen rose during the question period and said: "Victor's restaurant manager, Dana Wu, was fired from her last job at the Marina Grill for stealing from the cash register. Is that who he'd put in charge of city money?" The forum was not recorded. Gwen's only source was a former Marina Grill server who, Gwen knew, had quarreled with Dana. In fact, the Marina Grill had investigated a cash shortage in 2024, found no evidence against Dana, and Dana resigned two months later to join Lam's Kitchen. Dana has never sought publicity and has no role in the campaign. A week after the forum, a catering company whose owner attended the forum withdrew a part-time job offer to Dana paying $15,000 a year.

On September 15 Gwen began running a paid social-media advertisement for the Bulletin showing Victor's photograph with the words "Victor Lam reads the Bulletin. Shouldn't you?" Victor has never subscribed and never agreed to the use of his picture. Subscriptions rose 15% that month.

Victor lost the election. His restaurant's revenue fell 20% after September 5. Mei says she was humiliated and has stopped leaving her house.

## Call of the Question

1. What tort claims may Victor bring against Gwen, and what defenses may Gwen raise? Discuss.
2. What tort claims may Dana bring against Gwen, and what defenses may Gwen raise? Discuss.
3. What tort claims may Mei bring against Gwen, and what defenses may Gwen raise? Discuss.

## Model Answer

### 1. Victor v. Gwen

**A. Defamation: the rat-droppings statement.** A defendant is liable for defamation if she publishes to a third person a false and defamatory statement of fact of and concerning the plaintiff, causing damage. A statement is defamatory if it tends to harm the plaintiff's reputation in the community.

- *Defamatory fact.* "Health inspectors found rat droppings in Victor Lam's restaurant kitchen" accuses a restaurant owner of unsanitary conditions and is verifiable against inspection records. It is a statement of fact, not opinion.
- *Of and concerning.* The story names Victor and "Victor Lam's restaurant." The existence of a different "Lam's" restaurant does not help Gwen, because her words point to Victor.
- *Publication.* The story went to about 6,000 subscribers.
- *Falsity.* The county list shows a clean inspection for Lam's Kitchen; the citation was at an unrelated restaurant.
- *Form and damages.* A written newsletter is libel. At common law general damages are presumed, and in California libel that is defamatory on its face needs no proof of special damages (Civ. Code § 45a). Victor's 20% drop in revenue is also provable special damage.

The rat-droppings story also supports a **false-light** claim (publicity placing Victor in a false light that would be highly offensive to a reasonable person), but it rests on the same falsehood and is subject to the same constitutional limits, so it adds little. The drone photograph is accurate, so it does not itself create a false light; its taking and use are analyzed under intrusion and appropriation below.

**B. Constitutional overlay.** A public official or public figure must prove by clear and convincing evidence that the statement was false and was made with actual malice: knowledge of its falsity or reckless disregard for the truth, meaning the defendant actually had serious doubts about it (*New York Times v. Sullivan*; *St. Amant*).

- *Status.* A candidate for public office is treated like a public official for statements bearing on his fitness for office. Victor also made his business record the center of his campaign ("run the city like a business"), so he is at least a limited-purpose public figure on that subject.
- *Public concern.* Restaurant sanitation and a candidate's management are matters of public concern.
- *Actual malice.* Gwen will argue that she honestly believed the restaurants were connected and was at most negligent under deadline pressure. She will add that a failure to investigate is not actual malice, and that her political hostility to Victor is not "malice" in the constitutional sense.

Victor's evidence is stronger than a failure to investigate. Gwen's only source was a volunteer for Victor's opponent. More importantly, before publishing, Gwen held the official record, which named a different restaurant at a different address and showed a clean inspection for Victor's restaurant. Her own note, "Same family? Check.", shows that she recognized the doubt, and she published anyway without checking. Publishing in the face of documents contradicting the story, while consciously leaving the doubt unresolved, supports a finding that she entertained serious doubts. **Victor can likely prove actual malice by clear and convincing evidence.**

*Damages.* With actual malice proved, Victor may recover presumed damages, his provable lost profits, and punitive damages (in California, on clear and convincing proof of malice, oppression or fraud, Civ. Code § 3294). Losing the election is too speculative a loss to attribute to one article.

**C. "Lam is a crook."** There is no separate privilege for opinion; a statement is actionable only if it asserts or implies a provably false fact, and rhetorical hyperbole is protected (*Milkovich*). Victor will argue that "crook" implies criminal dishonesty. But it is the closing line of a partisan attack piece in a political newsletter, nothing in the article suggests an actual crime, and readers would take "should never be trusted with the city budget" as invective. **The "crook" line is likely protected hyperbole**, although it may aggravate damages for the actionable rat story.

**D. Intrusion upon seclusion (the drone).** A defendant who intentionally intrudes, physically or otherwise, on another's solitude or private affairs is liable if the intrusion would be highly offensive to a reasonable person. No publication is required.

- *Intentional intrusion.* Gwen deliberately flew a camera drone 30 feet above Victor's backyard to photograph him.
- *Reasonable expectation of privacy.* The yard is surrounded by an eight-foot fence and cannot be seen from the street or the neighbors' yards. Gwen will argue that airspace is public and that anyone overhead could see in. A reasonable person, however, expects privacy in a fenced backyard against a low-hovering camera, which is far more intrusive than a distant aircraft.
- *Highly offensive.* Photographing a man in swimwear at home for use in a political attack is highly offensive.

Gwen's argument that a candidate's life is newsworthy is no defense: the tort is the intrusion itself, not the publication. California's constitutional privacy right (Cal. Const. art. I, § 1) points the same way. **Victor likely prevails on intrusion.**

**E. Appropriation; Civil Code § 3344.** A defendant who uses the plaintiff's name or likeness for her own advantage, usually commercial, without consent is liable. Civil Code § 3344 also gives a remedy for the knowing use of another's name or photograph in advertising without consent, measured by actual damages and the profits attributable to the use, or a statutory minimum.

Gwen used Victor's photo and name in a paid advertisement to sell subscriptions, without consent, and subscriptions rose 15%. She will invoke the news and public-affairs exemption, arguing that Victor is a candidate she covers. But the ad does not report or reproduce news; it falsely claims that Victor reads and implicitly endorses the Bulletin. Exploiting a false endorsement is commercial use, not news. **Victor likely recovers under the common law and § 3344**, including profits attributable to the ad.

### 2. Dana v. Gwen

**A. Slander per se.** Spoken defamation is slander, which requires proof of special damages unless it falls within a per se category, such as imputing a serious crime or misconduct in the plaintiff's business or profession (Civ. Code § 46).

- *Defamatory fact; per se.* "Fired from her last job... for stealing from the cash register" imputes theft and dishonesty in Dana's occupation. It is stated as fact. The rhetorical question that follows does not turn it into opinion.
- *Of and concerning; publication.* Gwen named Dana to about 80 people. The forum was not recorded, so the statement is slander, not libel.
- *Falsity.* Dana was investigated, cleared and later resigned. Gwen may argue substantial truth because there was a cash-shortage investigation. But "fired for stealing" carries a far worse sting than "investigated and cleared," so the statement is not substantially true.

**B. Constitutional overlay.**
- *Status.* Dana is a private figure. She never sought publicity and has no role in the campaign, and working for a candidate does not make her one. She did not voluntarily enter any public controversy; Gwen pulled her into it.
- *Concern.* Gwen will argue that a candidate's choice of managers bears on his fitness. Dana will respond that her employment history is a private matter. Courts look at content, form and context. The content concerned Dana's private employment, but the context was a public election forum and the statement was tied to Victor's judgment, so it is likely a matter of public concern, though the point is close.
- *If public concern (Gertz).* Dana must prove falsity and at least negligence (California requires negligence, *Brown v. Kelly Broadcasting*), and without actual malice she may recover only actual injury. Gwen relied on a single source she knew had quarreled with Dana and called neither Dana nor the Marina Grill, which is plainly negligent. Actual malice is harder to show: knowing that her source was biased suggests reckless disregard, but there is no direct evidence that Gwen doubted the story. Dana's actual injury includes the $15,000 job offer withdrawn by a forum attendee a week later, plus reputational harm and distress. Presumed and punitive damages require actual malice.
- *If private concern (Dun & Bradstreet).* The common law applies: slander per se supports presumed damages, and punitive damages are available without actual malice.

**C. Defenses.** Truth fails, and the statement is fact, not opinion. Gwen may claim a qualified privilege for statements made in good faith on a matter of common interest (Civ. Code § 47(c) covers communications without malice to an interested person). A general audience at an open forum is not the kind of narrow, shared interest the privilege protects. Even if it applied, a qualified privilege is lost by abuse, and repeating a known adversary's accusation without any verification is at least reckless. No absolute privilege applies, because a candidates' forum is not an official proceeding. **Dana likely prevails**, recovering at least her $15,000 lost job offer and other actual injury.

### 3. Mei v. Gwen

**A. Public disclosure of private facts.** A defendant who gives publicity to a matter concerning another's private life is liable if the matter is not of legitimate public concern and its publication would be highly offensive to a reasonable person. Truth is not a defense.

- *Publicity.* The report went to about 6,000 subscribers online.
- *Private fact.* Addiction treatment is confidential medical information that Gwen learned from a clinic receptionist, not from any public record.
- *Highly offensive.* Disclosure of addiction treatment would offend any reasonable person.
- *Newsworthiness.* Gwen will argue that a candidate's household bears on his fitness ("can't manage his own household"). Courts weigh the social value of the fact, the depth of the intrusion into private matters, and the extent to which the plaintiff voluntarily entered public life. Mei never campaigned or appeared publicly with Victor, the link between her medical treatment and his fitness is thin, and the intrusion into her health is deep.

The First Amendment protects truthful publication of lawfully obtained public-record information on matters of public significance, but this fact came from a breach of clinic confidentiality, not a public record, and has little public significance. **Mei likely prevails** and recovers damages for humiliation and distress.

**B. Other theories and anti-SLAPP.** Defamation and false light fail because the statement was true and not misleading. Intentional infliction of emotional distress requires extreme and outrageous conduct and severe distress. Mei's distress is severe (she has stopped leaving her house), but publishing a true fact in a political article, though cruel, is probably not beyond all bounds of decency, so IIED likely fails.

As to all three plaintiffs, Gwen may file an anti-SLAPP motion to strike (CCP § 425.16), because her statements concern a public issue made in a public forum. Each plaintiff must then show a probability of prevailing; on the analysis above, Victor (libel, intrusion, appropriation), Dana and Mei can likely do so.

- **45 or below:** discusses only common-law defamation, with no constitutional overlay and no privacy torts.

**Common mistakes**
1. Equating actual malice with ill will. It means knowledge of falsity or serious subjective doubt.
2. Concluding that Gwen's failure to check proves actual malice by itself. The stronger point is that she held contradicting documents and noted the doubt.
3. Treating Dana as a public figure because her employer was a candidate.
4. Applying newsworthiness to the intrusion claim, or truth to the disclosure claim.
5. Treating "crook" as automatically actionable (or automatically protected) without the *Milkovich* analysis.

**Accuracy notes.** Rules follow R-TRT-096 to R-TRT-104. The candidate-as-public-official point and the subjective "serious doubts" test are settled Supreme Court doctrine. California requires negligence for private-figure plaintiffs (*Brown v. Kelly Broadcasting* (1989) 48 Cal.3d 711). The answer avoids the retraction statute (Civ. Code § 48a, flagged V-606) because coverage of online newsletters is not confirmed.
