## Facts

Noor and Elliot married in California in June 2014. On June 1, 2025, Elliot told Noor the marriage was over and moved out. The date of separation is not disputed. Noor filed for dissolution in July 2025.

**Coastline Solar.** In 2010 Elliot founded Coastline Solar, Inc., a residential solar-installation company, and he owned all of its stock when he married. An appraiser values the business at $500,000 as of the wedding. Throughout the marriage Elliot worked about 50 hours a week as Coastline's president. He paid himself a salary of $140,000 a year, and Coastline also paid about $25,000 a year of family expenses, namely the family's health insurance and the lease on Noor's car. The parties' experts agree that (1) a fair salary for the president of a comparable company was $180,000 a year; (2) a fair annual return on the capital in a business of this kind was 8%, simple; and (3) Coastline was worth $2,600,000 at separation and is worth the same today. In 2018 Elliot designed and launched a battery-storage division that now produces 30% of Coastline's revenue. In 2021, however, the state tripled its rebates for home solar systems, and solar installers throughout the region, including competitors whose management did not change, roughly doubled in value over the next three years.

**Noor's education.** When they married, Noor was a dental hygienist. From 2016 to 2020 she attended dental school. Community earnings paid $160,000 of her tuition, and she borrowed another $120,000 in student loans in her own name. From 2021 to 2025 the spouses paid $30,000 on those loans from community funds; $90,000 is still owed. From 2021 Noor practiced as a dentist, earning about $250,000 a year, nearly all of which went into the spouses' joint accounts and paid for a larger home and the children's private school. In 2017 community funds also paid $25,000 for a battery-storage engineering certification course that Elliot took before launching Coastline's new division.

**The accident.** In March 2023 a delivery truck ran a red light and struck Noor's car, crushing her right hand. She can now work only three days a week, and her income has fallen to about $140,000 a year. In February 2024 the trucking company's insurer paid $240,000 to settle her claim; the release does not allocate the payment among her losses. Noor deposited $40,000 of it in the spouses' joint checking account, where it was spent on family living expenses, and $200,000 in a new savings account in her name alone, which she has not touched. Before the settlement, community funds had paid $35,000 of her medical bills.

**The pedestrian.** On a weekday afternoon in March 2025, Elliot finished a sales meeting at a customer's home and drove his own pickup toward a golf course, where he planned to play nine holes with a college friend who occasionally referred customers to Coastline. On the way, while reading a text message, he struck a pedestrian, Rowan. In April 2026 Rowan obtained a $550,000 judgment against Elliot alone. Elliot's liability insurer paid its $250,000 limit, leaving $300,000 unpaid. Rowan now seeks to collect the balance by levying on Elliot's Coastline stock, on the $200,000 settlement account, and on a condominium that Noor inherited from her mother in 2019 and has always held in her own name.

## Call of the Question

Answer according to California law.

1. What are Noor's and Elliot's rights in (a) Coastline Solar, (b) the $200,000 settlement account, and (c) the community funds spent on Noor's dental education and the remaining student loans?
2. What property may Rowan reach to satisfy the unpaid $300,000, and how should that liability be treated when the court divides the parties' property?

## Model Answer

### Introduction

California is a community property state. Property acquired by a spouse during marriage while domiciled in California is presumed to be community property (CP) (Fam. Code § 760). Property owned before marriage, property acquired by gift or inheritance, the rents, issues and profits of separate property (SP), and earnings after the date of separation (DOS) are SP (§§ 770, 771). The DOS is June 1, 2025, so the marital community ran for 11 years (June 2014 to June 2025). At dissolution the court divides the community estate equally and confirms each spouse's SP (§ 2550).

### 1(a). Coastline Solar

**Character at acquisition.** Elliot founded Coastline in 2010 and owned all of its stock at the wedding, so the business was his SP, worth $500,000 at marriage (§ 770).

**Community labor requires apportionment.** A spouse's skill, effort and labor during marriage belong to the community (§ 760). When community labor contributes to the growth of an SP business, the increase must be apportioned between the separate estate, as a return on capital, and the community, as a return on labor (*Pereira v. Pereira*; *Van Camp v. Van Camp*). Elliot worked about 50 hours a week as president for 11 years, and the business grew by $2,100,000. Apportionment is required.

**Pereira.** The SP owner receives the capital at marriage plus a fair return for the years of marriage; the rest is CP.
- SP = $500,000 + (8% × $500,000 × 11) = $500,000 + $440,000 = **$940,000**
- CP = $2,600,000 − $940,000 = **$1,660,000**

**Van Camp.** The community receives the reasonable value of the spouse's services, less what the community already received from the business in salary and family expenses; the rest is SP.
- Fair value of services: $180,000 × 11 = $1,980,000
- Received by the community: ($140,000 salary + $25,000 family expenses) × 11 = $1,815,000
- CP = **$165,000**; SP = $2,600,000 − $165,000 = **$2,435,000**

The $25,000 a year of health insurance and car lease counts as compensation because it was a benefit the community took from the business.

**Which method?** The court chooses the method that achieves substantial justice: Pereira when growth is chiefly due to the spouse's personal efforts, Van Camp when it is chiefly due to the capital or outside conditions; neither formula is mandatory (*Beam v. Bank of America*; *In re Marriage of Dekker*). Whether a court may apply Pereira to one period and Van Camp to another is not settled [VERIFY V-900], so a party may argue for a split, but the safer course on the exam is to choose one method and mention the other.
- *Noor (Pereira).* Elliot worked 50-hour weeks and personally created the battery-storage division, which now produces 30% of revenue. That growth is the product of his skill and effort.
- *Elliot (Van Camp).* Much of the growth came from an outside event: the 2021 tripling of state rebates, after which every installer in the region roughly doubled in value, including competitors whose management did not change. That is the market-driven growth Van Camp addresses. Elliot also paid the community close to full value for his work ($165,000 against a fair $180,000 a year), so the community was not shortchanged.
- *Likely result.* Both drivers are real. Noor could argue in the alternative for Pereira for 2014–2021, when growth came mainly from Elliot's efforts and the new division, and Van Camp for 2021–2025, when the rebate drove the market; but that split is an argument, not settled law [VERIFY V-900], and it would require a 2021 valuation, which this record lacks. Because the largest single jump tracks the rebate and the community drew nearly market compensation throughout, the court will likely lean toward **Van Camp**, though the battery division gives Noor a real argument for Pereira. On pure Van Camp the CP interest is $165,000.

**Division.** Elliot will likely keep the stock, and Noor will receive half of the community interest through an offset or equalizing payment: **$82,500 under Van Camp** (or $830,000 if the court used Pereira).

### 1(b). The $200,000 settlement account

**Character.** Personal-injury damages are CP if the cause of action arose during marriage (§ 780). They are the injured spouse's SP if the claim arose after a judgment of dissolution or legal separation, while the injured spouse was living separate from the other spouse, or against the other spouse (§ 781). Noor was injured in March 2023 by a third party while the spouses were married and living together. The settlement is therefore **CP**.

**Division (§ 2603).** Community personal-injury damages are assigned to the injured spouse at division unless the court, considering the spouses' economic condition and needs, the time since the recovery or the accrual of the claim, and all other facts, determines that justice requires another disposition; even then, at least one-half must go to the injured spouse. Damages that have been commingled with other community property lose this special treatment.

**Commingled portion.** The $40,000 deposited in the joint checking account was commingled and spent on family living expenses. It no longer exists, so there is nothing to divide and no reimbursement claim. The $200,000 has stayed in a separate account in Noor's name and was never commingled, so § 2603 applies to it (with its interest).

**Justice factors.**
- *Elliot's arguments.* The release is unallocated. Part of the payment replaced Noor's lost earnings from March 2023 to the June 2025 DOS, which would have been CP, and the community paid $35,000 of her medical bills.
- *Noor's arguments.* Her hand injury is permanent and has cut her income from about $250,000 to about $140,000 a year, so her future needs are greater. Elliot leaves the marriage with a large SP interest in Coastline ($940,000 even under Pereira, about $2.4 million under Van Camp). The recovery came only about two years before trial.
- *Likely result.* The statute's default favors the injured spouse, and Noor's needs and Elliot's comparative wealth point the same way. **The $200,000 will likely be assigned entirely to Noor.** At most, a court might give Elliot a modest share reflecting the community's medical payments; Noor must receive at least $100,000.

### 1(c). Noor's education and student loans

**Rule.** A professional degree or enhanced earning capacity is not property. Instead, the community is reimbursed for community contributions to a spouse's education or training that substantially enhances that spouse's earning capacity, with interest at the legal rate from the end of each calendar year in which the contributions were made, and loans incurred during marriage for the education are assigned to the educated spouse rather than divided (§ 2641(b); § 2627). This is the community's exclusive remedy (§ 2641(d); *In re Marriage of Sullivan*).

**Application.** Community funds paid $160,000 of tuition (2016–2020) and $30,000 of loan payments (2021–2025), a total of $190,000 plus interest. The education substantially enhanced Noor's earning capacity: she went from hygienist to dentist and roughly $250,000 a year. Absent a reduction, Noor owes the community $190,000 plus interest (Elliot's half is $95,000 plus interest), and the remaining **$90,000 of loans is assigned to Noor**.

**Reductions (§ 2641(c)).** Reimbursement and loan assignment are reduced or modified to the extent they would be unjust, including where:
1. *The community substantially benefited.* There is a rebuttable presumption that the community has **not** substantially benefited from contributions made less than ten years before the proceeding began, and has benefited from contributions made more than ten years before. All contributions here were made within ten years of the July 2025 filing, so the presumption favors Elliot. Noor can rebut it: from 2021 nearly all of her dental income (about $250,000 a year until the 2023 accident, about $140,000 after it) went into joint accounts and paid for a larger home and private school. Elliot will respond that the community enjoyed only about four years of that income and that her injury cut it short. Four years of a dentist's income flowing to the family is a substantial benefit, so the presumption is likely rebutted at least in part.
2. *Offsetting education.* The community paid $25,000 for Elliot's battery-storage certification, which enhanced his earning capacity and Coastline's value. Noor's reimbursement obligation should be offset by at least that amount.
3. *Reduced need for support.* Noor's degree lets her earn $140,000 even after her injury, which reduces any need for spousal support from Elliot. That supports reducing the reimbursement. Elliot will answer that her injury, not her degree, now limits her earnings.

**Conclusion.** The community has a § 2641 claim of $190,000 plus interest, and the loans are presumptively Noor's. Because the presumption of no benefit is likely rebutted and Elliot received offsetting education, the court will likely **substantially reduce** the reimbursement and may also adjust the loan assignment.

### 2. Rowan's judgment

**A. What Rowan may reach**

*When the debt was incurred.* A tort debt is incurred when the tort occurs (§ 903). Elliot struck Rowan in March 2025, before the June 2025 DOS, so the debt was incurred during marriage, even though judgment was entered after separation.

*Community estate.* The community estate is liable for a debt incurred by either spouse before or during marriage, regardless of which spouse manages the property or is a party to the judgment (§ 910). Rowan may therefore reach:
- the **community interest in Coastline** (and Elliot's SP interest, below); and
- the **$200,000 settlement account**. It is CP until the court divides it. Section 2603 governs division between the spouses; it does not shield community personal-injury damages from a community creditor.

*Elliot's SP.* A spouse's SP is liable for his own debts (§ 913(a)), so Rowan may reach Elliot's SP interest in the Coastline stock.

*Noor's SP.* A spouse's SP is not liable for the other spouse's debts, except debts for necessaries (§§ 913(b), 914). Noor's condominium was inherited and has always been held in her name, so it is her SP (§ 770), and a tort judgment is not a necessary. **Rowan cannot reach the condominium.** Noor's post-DOS earnings are also her SP (§ 771) and are not liable.

**B. Order of satisfaction (§ 1000(b))**

A spouse is not liable for the other spouse's torts merely because of the marriage. If the tortfeasor was performing an activity for the benefit of the community, the liability is satisfied first from the community estate and then from his SP; if not, first from his SP and then from the community estate.
- *Elliot's argument (community benefit).* He was driving from a sales meeting, on a workday, to meet a friend who referred customers to Coastline, the business that supported the family. Cultivating referrals benefits the community.
- *Noor's argument (no community benefit).* The work meeting was over. He was driving to play golf, a personal recreation, and the referral connection was incidental. He was also reading a text while driving, conduct that served no community purpose.
- *Likely result.* This is close. Section 1000(b) asks only whether Elliot was performing an activity for the benefit of the community; it does not require that the activity be purely for business, so a court could find that cultivating a referral source on a workday, straight from a sales call, qualifies. But the meeting was over and the immediate purpose of the trip was a round of golf, so the better view is that the activity was **not** for the benefit of the community. On that view Rowan must first satisfy the $300,000 from Elliot's SP, chiefly his SP interest in Coastline (about $2.4 million under Van Camp), which is more than enough, and Noor's settlement account should not be touched. If instead the court found a community benefit, Rowan would look first to community property, including the settlement account, and the debt would be divided as a community debt (C below).

**C. Treatment at division**

At division, a liability that falls under § 1000(b)(2), because it was not incurred in an activity for the community's benefit, is assigned to the spouse whose act caused it, **without offset** against the other spouse's share (§ 2627). On the likely finding above, the entire $300,000 is assigned to Elliot. If it had been a community-benefit tort, it would be a community debt divided under the general rules (§§ 2620–2622).

After division, property Noor receives in the division and her SP are not liable for a debt incurred by Elliot unless the debt was assigned to her (§ 916(a)(2)). If Rowan collects from community property before the division, the court should charge that amount to Elliot's share so that Noor bears none of it.

**Common mistakes**
1. Calling the settlement Noor's SP at the outset. It is CP under § 780; § 2603 only changes how it is divided, and it does not protect the funds from a community creditor.
2. Leaving the $25,000 of family expenses out of the Van Camp computation, or reversing what each formula computes (Pereira computes the SP share; Van Camp computes the CP share).
3. Forgetting the interest on § 2641 reimbursement, or giving § 2640-style reimbursement instead.
4. Applying the ten-year presumption backwards (contributions within ten years are presumed **not** to have benefited the community).
5. Merging the three debt questions: liability to Rowan (§§ 910, 913), order of satisfaction (§ 1000) and allocation between the spouses (§ 2627).
