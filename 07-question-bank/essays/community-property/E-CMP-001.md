## Facts

Ingrid and Paolo married in California in June 2014 and lived in California throughout the marriage. On March 1, 2025, Paolo told Ingrid that he wanted a divorce and moved into an apartment the same day. Ingrid filed a petition for dissolution in April 2025. Throughout the marriage, both spouses deposited their salaries (about $260,000 a year combined) into a joint checking account, from which they paid all household bills.

**The condominium.** In 2011, Paolo bought a condominium for $300,000, paying $60,000 down from his own savings and borrowing $240,000 on a loan in his name alone. Title was taken in "Paolo Russo, a single man." Before the wedding Paolo paid the principal down to $220,000; the condominium was then worth $350,000. The couple lived there throughout the marriage. Mortgage payments from the joint checking account reduced the principal by a further $70,000, to $150,000. In 2016, when Ingrid suggested selling the condominium to buy a larger home, Paolo said, "Forget it. This place is ours now, both of ours." Relying on that statement, Ingrid stopped looking at houses. Nothing was ever put in writing. The parties stipulate that the condominium is now worth $650,000 and that no principal has been paid since separation.

**The lake cabin.** In 2016, the couple bought a cabin for $250,000, taking title as "Paolo Russo and Ingrid Russo, wife and husband, as joint tenants." Of the $50,000 down payment, $30,000 came from a savings account Ingrid had opened before the marriage and into which no community funds were ever deposited; $20,000 came from the joint checking account. When they signed the purchase documents, Paolo said, "This is *our* cabin. If we ever split up, you're not getting your $30,000 back." Ingrid laughed and said, "Fine." The cabin is now worth $400,000, subject to a $150,000 loan balance.

**The brokerage account.** On June 5, 2017, Ingrid deposited an $80,000 inheritance from her grandmother into the joint checking account, which then held $9,000. By June 20, $14,000 of salary had been deposited and $11,000 of household bills paid. On June 20, Ingrid transferred $50,000 from the joint account into a new brokerage account in her sole name and bought shares of a stock index fund. The transfer memo read "Nonna's inheritance — invest," and Ingrid kept a contemporaneous spreadsheet labeled "Inheritance money." The rest of the inheritance was spent from the joint account over the next two years on household expenses and a family trip to Italy. Ingrid has never traded in the brokerage account; with reinvested dividends it is now worth $90,000.

**The SUV.** In 2022, Paolo bought an SUV for $45,000 with funds from the joint checking account and had it titled in Ingrid's name. He gave her the keys with a birthday card signed, "Happy 40th! The keys are yours — enjoy every mile. Love, P." Ingrid drives the SUV daily; Paolo drives a company car. It is now worth $30,000.

**Debts.** In 2023, without telling Ingrid, Paolo borrowed $40,000 from Mesa Bank, signing alone, to buy woodworking equipment for a weekend furniture business. The business earned $3,000, which Paolo deposited into the joint checking account. The equipment is worth $10,000; $32,000 remains owed. In April 2025, Paolo charged $8,000 on a credit card in his sole name for a bed, a sofa and kitchenware for his new apartment.

## Model Answer

**Introduction**

California is a community property state. Property acquired by a married person during the marriage while domiciled in California is presumed to be community property (CP) (Fam. Code § 760). Separate property (SP) includes property owned before marriage, property acquired by gift or inheritance, its rents, issues and profits, and earnings after the date of separation (§§ 770, 771). Character is fixed by the source of funds and the time of acquisition, then tested against presumptions and any acts of the spouses, such as a transmutation, that changed it. The date of separation is the date a complete and final break in the marital relationship occurred, shown by one spouse's expression to the other of the intent to end the marriage and conduct consistent with that intent (§ 70). Here, Paolo told Ingrid on March 1, 2025 that he wanted a divorce and moved out that day, so the date of separation is March 1, 2025. At dissolution the court divides the community estate equally (§ 2550).

### 1. The condominium

**Character at acquisition.** Property owned before marriage is SP (§ 770). Paolo bought the condominium in 2011, three years before the wedding, with his own savings and a loan in his name. It is his SP at inception.

**Did Paolo transmute it into CP?** Since 1985 a transmutation is valid only if made in writing by an express declaration that is made, joined in, consented to or accepted by the spouse whose interest is adversely affected (§ 852(a)). Paolo's 2016 statement, "This place is ours now, both of ours," was oral. Ingrid will argue that the statement was clear and that she relied on it by giving up the search for a larger home. However, *Estate of MacDonald* requires a writing that on its face expressly states a change in ownership, and *In re Marriage of Benson* holds there is no part-performance or estoppel exception to § 852. Ingrid's reliance therefore does not help her. **The condominium remains Paolo's SP.**

**Community contributions: Moore/Marsden.** When community funds reduce the principal of a purchase-money loan on one spouse's SP real property, the community acquires a pro tanto interest. That interest equals the principal it paid plus its share of the appreciation during marriage, the share being the community principal reduction divided by the purchase price (*In re Marriage of Moore*). Appreciation before marriage belongs to the SP owner (*In re Marriage of Marsden*). Only principal counts: interest, taxes and insurance paid by the community are treated as the cost of living in the home (Moore).

Paolo may argue that the community's payments were simply the cost of housing, like rent. Moore rejects that argument for principal, which builds equity. The community paid $70,000 of principal from the joint account of marital salaries, so the formula applies:

| Step | Computation | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Community % | $70,000 CP principal ÷ $300,000 purchase price | 23.33% |
| Appreciation during marriage | $650,000 − $350,000 value at marriage | $300,000 |
| CP interest | $70,000 + (23.33% × $300,000) | **$140,000** |
| Total equity | $650,000 − $150,000 loan | $500,000 |
| SP interest | $500,000 − $140,000. Check: $60,000 down + $20,000 premarital principal + $50,000 premarital appreciation + (76.67% × $300,000 = $230,000) | **$360,000** |

**Conclusion.** The condominium is Paolo's SP, but the community owns a $140,000 interest in it. Ingrid is entitled to half of that interest, $70,000. The court will likely award the condominium to Paolo and offset $70,000 to Ingrid from other assets.

### 2. The lake cabin

**Joint-title presumption.** At dissolution, property acquired during marriage in joint form, including joint tenancy, is presumed CP. The presumption is rebutted only by a clear statement in the deed or a written agreement that the property is SP (§ 2581). The cabin was bought in 2016, during marriage, as "joint tenants," and nothing in the deed or any writing says it is separate. The survivorship feature of joint tenancy is irrelevant at dissolution. **The cabin is CP.**

**Reimbursement of Ingrid's SP down payment (§ 2640).** At dissolution, a spouse is reimbursed for separate contributions to the acquisition of CP, such as a down payment, if they are traced to a separate source, unless the spouse waived the right in writing. The reimbursement carries no interest or appreciation and may not exceed the property's net value.

- *Tracing.* The $30,000 came from an account Ingrid owned before the marriage, into which no community funds were ever deposited. It is cleanly traced to SP.
- *Contribution to acquisition.* A down payment is the classic § 2640 contribution.
- *Waiver.* Paolo will argue that Ingrid agreed to give up reimbursement when she answered "Fine" to his statement that she would not get the $30,000 back, and that her contribution was a gift to the marriage. The old gift presumption of *Marriage of Lucas* has been displaced by § 2640, which requires a **written** waiver. Ingrid's oral, laughing "Fine" is not a writing and is a weak sign of a deliberate waiver in any event.

**Division.** Net equity is $400,000 − $150,000 = $250,000. Ingrid first receives $30,000, leaving $220,000 of CP, or $110,000 to each spouse. The $20,000 community down payment and the community-paid principal create no separate claim, because they are already CP.

**Conclusion.** The cabin is CP. Ingrid receives $140,000 ($30,000 reimbursement plus $110,000) and Paolo receives $110,000 of its net value.

### 3. The brokerage account

**Character of the source.** Property acquired by inheritance is SP (§ 770(a)(2)), so the $80,000 was Ingrid's SP when received. But the brokerage account was bought during marriage from a joint account holding salaries, so it is presumptively CP (§ 760), and title in Ingrid's name does not change that. Ingrid bears the burden of tracing.

**Commingling.** Mixing SP and CP in one account does not change the character of either, but if the SP cannot be traced, the commingled fund is treated as CP (*See v. See*; *In re Marriage of Mix*). Ingrid may not rely on "total recapitulation," that is, showing that more SP went into the account than was spent on separate purposes (See). She must link specific separate dollars to this purchase.

**Direct tracing (Mix).** A spouse traces directly by showing with adequate records that (1) separate funds were in the account when the asset was bought and (2) the spouse intended to use them for it.
- *Availability.* Family expenses are presumed paid from community funds when community funds are available (See). On June 20 the community portion of the account was $9,000 + $14,000 − $11,000 = $12,000, so the $80,000 inheritance was untouched and more than covered the $50,000 transfer.
- *Intent.* The transfer memo, "Nonna's inheritance — invest," and Ingrid's contemporaneous "Inheritance money" spreadsheet show she meant to use her separate funds. These are records made at the time, not a later reconstruction.

Paolo will argue that the account also held $12,000 of community funds, so at least part of the $50,000 could have been community, and that Ingrid's labels are self-serving. However, *Mix* accepts contemporaneous records showing availability and intent, and the memo was written when Ingrid had no reason to anticipate a divorce. **Direct tracing likely succeeds for the full $50,000.**

**Exhaustion (See), the alternative.** Under exhaustion, a purchase is SP if all community funds had been exhausted by family expenses when it was made. Community funds ($12,000) were not exhausted on June 20, so exhaustion cannot prove that the whole purchase was SP. If the court rejected direct tracing, Ingrid would argue that at least $38,000 of the $50,000 must have come from SP, because only $12,000 of community funds was in the account; a court accepting that argument would treat the account as about 76% SP ($38,000/$50,000) and 24% CP.

**The rest of the inheritance.** The other $30,000 was spent on household expenses and a family trip. A spouse who uses SP for family expenses is presumed to have made a gift to the community and is not reimbursed (See). Ingrid has no claim for it.

**Growth.** The rents, issues and profits of SP are SP (§ 770(a)(3)). Ingrid made no trades; the growth to $90,000 came from the market and reinvested dividends, with no community effort to apportion.

**Conclusion.** The $90,000 brokerage account is likely Ingrid's SP in full.

### 4. The SUV

**Presumption and title.** The SUV was bought during marriage with salary funds from the joint account, so it is presumptively CP (§ 760). Ingrid will rely on title in her name. But between spouses, title taken in one spouse's name does not displace the community character of property bought with community funds; the Evidence Code § 662 title presumption gives way to the transmutation statutes (*In re Marriage of Valli*). The question is whether Paolo transmuted his community interest to Ingrid.

**Writing requirement.** The birthday card is a writing signed by Paolo, the adversely affected spouse. But under *MacDonald* the writing must expressly state that ownership or character is being changed, without resort to extrinsic evidence. Ingrid will argue that "The keys are yours" says she owns the car. Paolo will respond that a birthday card about handing over keys speaks of use and enjoyment ("enjoy every mile"), not of changing community ownership, and that reading it as a transmutation requires the surrounding circumstances. Because the card never mentions ownership, separate property or the community, it likely fails the express-declaration test.

**§ 852(c) exception.** No writing is needed for an interspousal gift of clothing, jewelry or other tangible articles of a personal nature used solely or principally by the receiving spouse and not substantial in value in light of the marriage's circumstances. Ingrid uses the SUV principally. But a $45,000 vehicle is not an article of a personal nature like clothing or jewelry, and even on a $260,000 household income it is substantial in value. The exception does not apply.

**Conclusion.** The SUV is CP worth $30,000. The court will likely award it to Ingrid, who uses it, and offset $15,000 to Paolo.

### 5. The debts

**a. Mesa Bank loan**

*Liability to the bank.* The community estate is liable for a debt incurred by either spouse before or during marriage, whether or not the other spouse signed or knew of it (§ 910). A spouse's SP is liable for that spouse's own debts but not for the other spouse's debts, except for necessaries (§ 913). Paolo borrowed in 2023, during marriage and before separation. Mesa Bank may therefore reach all CP, including Ingrid's half interest, and Paolo's SP, such as his SP interest in the condominium. Ingrid's lack of knowledge and failure to sign are irrelevant. Ingrid's SP brokerage account is not reachable, because woodworking equipment is not a necessary of life.

*Allocation at dissolution.* Debts incurred during marriage and before separation are generally divided equally with the community estate (§§ 2620–2622). Under § 2625, however, a debt incurred during marriage that was not incurred for the community's benefit is confirmed to the incurring spouse without offset. Ingrid will argue the loan funded Paolo's hobby, taken out without telling her. Paolo will respond that the furniture business was meant to make money for the family, and that its $3,000 of earnings went into the joint account. Because the venture was income-producing and its profits benefited the community, the loan was likely incurred for community benefit.

*Conclusion.* The $32,000 balance is likely a community debt divided equally, netted against the $10,000 of equipment, which is a community asset (bought on credit during marriage, with nothing showing the bank relied on Paolo's SP). If the court accepted Ingrid's "hobby" characterization, it would confirm the debt to Paolo under § 2625.

**b. The $8,000 credit-card debt**

*Liability to the creditor.* For creditor purposes, "during marriage" excludes the period after separation (§ 910(b)), so the community estate is not liable for Paolo's April 2025 charges merely because the spouses are still married. Paolo is personally liable. Ingrid is liable only if the charges were for Paolo's common necessaries of life while the spouses lived separately (§ 914), and then with a right of reimbursement.

*Allocation.* Debts incurred after separation but before judgment are confirmed to the incurring spouse, except debts for the common necessaries of life of either spouse or the children, which are allocated according to the spouses' needs and abilities to pay when incurred (§ 2623). Paolo will argue that a bed and basic kitchenware for the home he must now live in are necessaries. Ingrid will respond that Paolo chose to leave a fully furnished home and that a sofa is a comfort, not a necessary. A court would likely treat the bed and basic kitchenware as common necessaries and the sofa as Paolo's own debt. Given Paolo's income, the court will likely confirm most or all of the $8,000 to him.

**Common mistakes**
1. Treating the condominium as CP because Ingrid relied on "ours now." There is no estoppel or part-performance exception to § 852 (Benson).
2. Stopping at "the cabin is CP" and forgetting the § 2640 reimbursement, or adding interest or appreciation to it.
3. Using total recapitulation instead of direct tracing or exhaustion, or ignoring the family-expense presumption when computing the community balance.
4. Treating title in Ingrid's name, or the birthday card, as automatically making the SUV her SP; or applying § 852(c) to a car.
5. Mixing creditor liability (§§ 910–914) with allocation between the spouses (§§ 2620–2625).
