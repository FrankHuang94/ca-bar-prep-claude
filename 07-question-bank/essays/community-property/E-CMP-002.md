## Facts

Keiko and Bram married in 2008 in State X, which is not a community property state. Under State X law, property acquired during marriage belongs to the spouse who holds title. In January 2016 they moved to California, where both have been domiciled ever since.

**Brokerage account.** From 2010 to 2015, Bram deposited part of his State X salary into a brokerage account in his sole name. Nothing has been added since the move. The account is now worth $300,000.

**Painting.** In 2012, while the couple lived in State X, Keiko's father gave her a landscape painting for her birthday. It is worth $40,000. Bram says the painting was "acquired in State X during the marriage, like my account."

**The kayak business.** In January 2017, Keiko inherited Tidewater Kayak Rentals, a small rental shop on the California coast, from her aunt. It was then worth $400,000. Keiko ran the business full time. She replaced paper bookings with an online reservation system, opened two more rental locations and started guided tours. In 2019, the state opened a marine park nearby, and tourist traffic in the area roughly doubled. Keiko paid herself $45,000 a year, and the business paid no other family expenses. The parties' experts agree that (1) reasonable compensation for Keiko's work was $95,000 a year; (2) a fair rate of return on a business of this kind was 7% a year; and (3) the business was worth $1,600,000 in January 2026 and its value has not changed since.

**Bram's employment.** In January 2016, Bram began working for the Bayview Water District, which provides a defined-benefit pension. His pension is not yet vested. He plans to retire in January 2036 after 20 years of service. In December 2025, Bayview paid Bram a $30,000 bonus for his performance during calendar year 2025.

**The separation.** On January 1, 2025, after an argument, Bram told Keiko, "I want a divorce," and moved into the guest room. For the rest of 2025 the spouses lived under the same roof. They stopped sharing meals, but they filed a joint 2024 tax return in April 2025, took their children on a two-week summer vacation, and attended couples counseling from September to December 2025. Their salaries continued to go into their joint savings account. On January 10, 2026, Bram moved to an apartment and told Keiko that counseling "had failed." Keiko filed for dissolution in February 2026.

**The gift to Piet.** In March 2025, Bram withdrew $25,000 from the joint savings account, which held only the spouses' salaries, and gave it to his brother, Piet, to help him make a down payment on a house. Bram did not tell Keiko, who learned of the gift in March 2026. Piet still has the house.

## Call of the Question

Answer according to California law.

1. What is the date of separation?
2. What are Keiko's and Bram's rights in (a) the brokerage account, (b) the painting, (c) Tidewater Kayak Rentals, and (d) Bram's pension and his December 2025 bonus?
3. What rights does Keiko have regarding the $25,000 Bram gave to Piet?

## Model Answer

### 1. Date of separation

The date of separation (DOS) is the date a complete and final break in the marital relationship occurred, shown by (1) one spouse's expression to the other of the intent to end the marriage and (2) conduct consistent with that intent (Fam. Code § 70). The court considers all relevant evidence, and spouses may separate while living under the same roof.

**Bram's position: January 1, 2025.** Bram expressed his intent clearly: "I want a divorce." He then moved into the guest room and the spouses stopped sharing meals. Because § 70 allows separation under one roof, Bram will argue that both prongs were met that day. He wants the earlier date because it would make his December 2025 bonus SP and shorten the community period of his pension.

**Keiko's position: January 10, 2026.** Bram's conduct during 2025 was not consistent with a final break. The spouses filed a joint tax return in April 2025, which holds them out as a married couple; took a two-week family vacation; kept pooling their salaries in a joint account; and attended couples counseling for four months. Counseling is an effort to save the marriage, the opposite of a complete and final break. Bram moved out only in January 2026 and said that counseling "had failed," which suggests he regarded the marriage as still alive until then.

**Conclusion.** Bram's January 1 statement satisfies the first prong, but his conduct for the rest of 2025 was largely inconsistent with ending the marriage. The DOS is likely **January 10, 2026**. The rest of this answer uses that date and notes where January 1, 2025 would change the result.

### 2. Characterization and division

**Framework.** Property acquired during marriage while domiciled in California is CP (§ 760). Property acquired before marriage or by gift or inheritance, and its rents, issues and profits, is SP (§ 770). Quasi-community property (QCP) is property acquired by either spouse while domiciled elsewhere that would have been CP had the acquiring spouse been domiciled in California, plus property acquired in exchange for it (§ 125). At dissolution QCP is part of the community estate and is divided equally with the CP (§§ 63, 2550). Because both Keiko and Bram are domiciled in California, there is no question about applying the QCP rules to a spouse who lives elsewhere, and applying them is consistent with due process because California has a strong interest in spouses domiciled here at dissolution (*Addison v. Addison*).

#### (a) The brokerage account

Under State X's title system, the account is Bram's alone, and Bram will argue that its character was fixed under State X law when acquired. But a spouse's earnings during marriage are CP in California (§ 760), so had Bram been domiciled in California from 2010 to 2015, the salary deposits would have been CP. The account is therefore **QCP**, and Bram's title does not change that at dissolution. Its $300,000 value is part of the community estate: **$150,000 to each spouse**, by an equal division or an offsetting award.

#### (b) The painting

Bram argues that anything acquired in State X during the marriage is QCP. But QCP covers only property that *would have been CP* had the acquirer been domiciled in California. A gift to one spouse would have been that spouse's SP in California (§ 770(a)(2)). Keiko's father gave the painting to her alone. **The painting is Keiko's SP**, and she keeps all $40,000 of its value.

#### (c) Tidewater Kayak Rentals

**Character at acquisition.** Keiko inherited the business in 2017, during marriage. Inherited property is SP (§ 770(a)(2)), so the business began as Keiko's SP, worth $400,000.

**Community labor requires apportionment.** A spouse's skill, effort and labor during marriage belong to the community (§ 760). When a spouse's labor during marriage increases the value of an SP business, the increase must be apportioned between the separate estate (a return on the SP capital) and the community (a return on the labor) (*Pereira v. Pereira*; *Van Camp v. Van Camp*). Keiko worked full time in the business for the nine years from January 2017 to the January 2026 DOS.

**Pereira.** The SP owner receives the original capital plus a fair rate of return on it for the period of community effort (here, from the 2017 inheritance to DOS), and the rest is CP. Courts use Pereira when growth is chiefly due to the spouse's personal efforts.
- SP = $400,000 + (7% × $400,000 × 9 years) = $400,000 + $252,000 = **$652,000**
- CP = $1,600,000 − $652,000 = **$948,000**

**Van Camp.** The community receives the reasonable value of the spouse's services less the compensation the community already received, and the rest is SP. Courts use Van Camp when growth is chiefly due to the character of the capital or to market conditions.
- CP = ($95,000 − $45,000) × 9 years = **$450,000**
- SP = $1,600,000 − $450,000 = **$1,150,000**

**Which method?** The court may choose whichever method achieves substantial justice between the spouses; neither formula is mandatory (*Beam v. Bank of America*; *In re Marriage of Dekker*). Whether a court may apply Pereira to one period and Van Camp to another is not settled [VERIFY V-900], so a party may argue for a split, but the safer course on the exam is to choose one method and mention the other.
- *Bram (Pereira).* Keiko's own work drove the growth. She built the online reservation system, opened two new locations and created the guided-tour line. These are the entrepreneurial efforts Pereira rewards. She also paid herself well below market, leaving value in the business that the community earned.
- *Keiko (Van Camp).* Much of the growth came from outside forces. The 2019 opening of the marine park roughly doubled tourist traffic, and a coastal rental shop would have grown with that traffic whoever ran it. That is the market-driven growth Van Camp addresses.
- *Likely result.* Both factors are real. Before 2019, growth came mainly from Keiko's efforts; from 2019, the park was a major driver, though Keiko's new locations and tours let the business capture that traffic. Bram will urge Pereira for the whole period. Keiko could argue for Pereira for 2017–2019 and Van Camp from 2019, but that split is an argument, not settled law [VERIFY V-900]. Because Keiko's expansions were themselves the means by which the business profited from the park, the court will likely lean toward **Pereira**, giving the community roughly $948,000 (about $474,000 to each spouse). Keiko will likely keep the business and pay Bram his share through an offset or an equalizing payment. Under pure Van Camp, Bram's share would be $225,000.

If the DOS were January 1, 2025, the community's labor period would be only eight years. The Van Camp community share would fall to $400,000, and under Pereira the court would also have to treat growth produced by Keiko's work during 2025 as the product of her post-separation (separate) labor, so the community share would shrink.

#### (d) Bram's pension and bonus

**Pension.** Retirement benefits are deferred compensation for work, so they are CP to the extent earned during marriage and before separation, whether or not vested (*In re Marriage of Brown*). That the pension is unvested does not matter. Bram worked for Bayview from January 2016, while married and domiciled in California, so the benefits earned from then until DOS are CP. The usual measure is the time rule: months of service during marriage before DOS divided by total months of service at retirement, applied to each payment.
- With a January 2026 DOS: 120 months ÷ 240 months = **50% CP**, so Keiko is entitled to 25% of each payment.
- With a January 2025 DOS: 108 ÷ 240 = 45% CP, so Keiko's share would be 22.5%.

The court must protect each spouse's full community share (§ 2610), either by cashing out the present value of the community share with other assets or by dividing each payment as received, typically through a domestic relations order. Because the pension is unvested and Bram may change jobs, an in-kind division of future payments is the safer course.

**Bonus.** Earnings for work performed during marriage are CP even if paid after separation; earnings after DOS are SP (§§ 760, 771). The $30,000 bonus was paid for Bram's 2025 performance.
- With a January 2026 DOS, all of 2025 fell within the marriage, so the **entire $30,000 is CP** ($15,000 each).
- With a January 1, 2025 DOS, the bonus was earned after separation and would be Bram's SP. This is why Bram presses for the earlier date.

### 3. The $25,000 gift to Piet

**Character.** The money came from the joint savings account, which held only the spouses' salaries. On the likely January 2026 DOS, every salary in the account was earned during marriage, so the $25,000 was CP. Even on Bram's January 2025 date, the account is presumptively CP; Bram would have to trace the withdrawal to his own post-separation salary to claim it as SP, and nothing suggests he can.

**Management limit.** Either spouse may manage community personal property (§ 1100(a)), but a spouse may not make a gift of community personal property, or dispose of it without fair and reasonable value, without the other spouse's **written consent** (§ 1100(b)). Bram gave Piet $25,000 for nothing in return and never told Keiko, let alone obtained her written consent. Bram may argue that, as an equal manager, he could help family members, and that the money was a loan. Nothing in the facts suggests a loan: it was a gift toward Piet's down payment, and equal management does not include giving community funds away.

**Fiduciary duty.** Spouses manage community property as fiduciaries who owe each other the highest good faith and fair dealing, including full disclosure of transactions involving community assets (§§ 721, 1100(e)). Concealing a $25,000 transfer for a year breached that duty.

**Keiko's remedies.**
1. *Set aside the gift.* An unconsented gift of CP is voidable by the non-consenting spouse; while the donor spouse is alive, she may set it aside entirely and recover the funds for the community. Keiko may seek return of the $25,000 from Piet. Piet will argue that he spent it on a house, but he gave no value for the gift.
2. *Charge it against Bram at division.* The court may award Keiko, from Bram's share of the community estate, an amount for a deliberate misappropriation of community property (§ 2602). The court can therefore treat the $25,000 as an advance to Bram and give Keiko $12,500 more from other assets.
3. *Fiduciary-breach remedies.* Under § 1101, a spouse may recover for a breach of fiduciary duty that impairs her interest in the community estate. The remedy is ordinarily 50% of the asset transferred in breach, plus attorney's fees and costs. It can be 100% if the breach involved oppression, fraud or malice. Keiko could argue that the secrecy was fraudulent. Bram would respond that he was helping his brother and had no intent to defraud. On these facts, a 50% award is the likely outcome.

**Conclusion.** The gift violated § 1100(b) and Bram's fiduciary duty. Keiko can likely set it aside against Piet or, more practically, have the full amount charged to Bram's share at division, so that she receives $12,500 more from the community estate.

**Common mistakes**
1. Treating the painting as QCP because it was acquired in State X during the marriage. QCP covers only what would have been CP in California.
2. Using only Pereira or only Van Camp, or reversing their outputs (Pereira computes the SP share; Van Camp computes the CP share).
3. Treating the unvested pension as SP, or forgetting that the time-rule fraction depends on the DOS.
4. Classifying the bonus by the date it was paid rather than when it was earned.
5. Saying the gift to Piet is void. It is voidable by Keiko, and the practical remedy is usually a charge against Bram's share.
