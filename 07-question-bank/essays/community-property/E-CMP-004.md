## Facts

Iris, a vice president at a software company, and Mateo, a high school music teacher earning about $75,000 a year, became engaged in early 2021. They set their wedding for Saturday, August 14, 2021, and invited 150 guests.

In June 2021 Iris told Mateo she wanted a premarital agreement "to keep things simple." On July 6 she gave him a draft prepared by her lawyer, with a handwritten note: "My lawyer says you should have your own lawyer look at this." The draft provided that each spouse's earnings during marriage, and all property bought with those earnings, would be that spouse's separate property. Mateo did not consult a lawyer.

On August 9, five days before the wedding, Iris's lawyer emailed Mateo the final agreement. It differed from the July draft in one respect: a new clause in which each spouse waived all rights to spousal support. The lawyer attached a two-page plain-English summary of the agreement and the rights Mateo would give up. Mateo, whose first language is English, read the summary but signed nothing acknowledging it. A schedule attached to the agreement listed Iris's $320,000 salary, her condominium and a $900,000 investment account. It did not mention a $600,000 signing bonus Iris had received in May 2021 and kept in a separate savings account. Mateo did not know about the bonus.

On August 11 Mateo signed the agreement and a separate one-page form stating, "I have been advised to obtain independent counsel and I choose not to." He later said that Iris had told him, "No agreement, no wedding," and that he "couldn't call off a wedding with 150 people coming." They married on August 14.

**The brokerage account.** In September 2021 Iris opened a brokerage account in her own name. During the marriage she deposited part of her salary in it, along with company stock that vested during the marriage as part of her pay. It is now worth $1,400,000. In 2024 Mateo asked to see the account statements. Iris replied, "That's mine under the prenup. It's none of your business," and refused.

**The house.** In 2022 the spouses bought a house on Larkspur Lane with a down payment from their joint savings account, which held both salaries, and a loan both signed. They took title as "Iris Vale and Mateo Ruiz, wife and husband, as community property." The house is worth $1,500,000, with $700,000 of equity.

In February 2025 Iris met with a divorce lawyer. In March 2025 she handed Mateo a grant deed conveying the house from both spouses "to Iris Vale, a married woman, as her sole and separate property." She told him the lender for a refinance "will only deal with one borrower" and that it was "just paperwork." Mateo signed at the kitchen table without reading the deed. There was no refinance. In June 2025 Iris told Mateo she wanted a divorce, and they separated. Iris has filed for dissolution.

## Call of the Question

Answer according to California law.

1. Is the premarital agreement enforceable?
2. What are Iris's and Mateo's rights in (a) the brokerage account and (b) the Larkspur Lane house?
3. What remedies, if any, does Mateo have for Iris's conduct concerning the house and the brokerage account?

## Model Answer

### 1. Enforceability of the premarital agreement

**A. Formation and scope.** A premarital agreement must be in writing and signed by both parties; it is enforceable without consideration and becomes effective on marriage (Fam. Code §§ 1611, 1613). The parties may contract about their property rights, including earnings acquired during marriage, but provisions on spousal support are subject to § 1612(c) (§ 1612). The agreement here is a signed writing made in contemplation of marriage, and characterizing each spouse's earnings as SP is a permissible subject. It is valid in form.

**No fiduciary presumption.** Spouses owe each other fiduciary duties, but persons who are not yet married do not (*In re Marriage of Bonds*). So Mateo cannot invoke the § 721 presumption of undue influence against the premarital agreement. He must proceed under § 1615, which provides that an agreement is unenforceable if the challenging party proves that he did not sign voluntarily or that it was unconscionable when signed and he lacked fair disclosure.

**B. Voluntariness (§ 1615(c)).** A premarital agreement is deemed involuntary unless the court finds **all** of the following:
1. *Counsel, or a separate written waiver after advisement.* The party had independent counsel or, after being advised to seek counsel at least seven calendar days before signing the final agreement, waived counsel in a separate writing. Mateo had no lawyer, but on August 11 he signed a separate waiver form. Iris's July 6 note relayed her lawyer's advice to get counsel, more than a month before signing. Mateo will argue that a handwritten note from his fiancée is not a meaningful advisement; but the statute requires only that he be advised, and he was. **Likely satisfied.**
2. *Seven calendar days with the final agreement.* The party must have had at least seven calendar days between first receiving the **final** agreement and signing it, whether or not represented. Mateo received the final version on August 9 and signed on August 11: two days. Iris will argue that he had the agreement since July 6. But the final version added a new term waiving spousal support, a substantive change, so the July draft was not the final agreement. **Not satisfied.**
3. *Written explanation for an unrepresented party.* An unrepresented party must be fully informed in writing, in a language he is proficient in, of the terms and basic effect of the agreement and the rights he is giving up, and must sign a document declaring that he received the information and indicating who provided it. Mateo received a plain-English summary and read it, so the substance was met. But he signed nothing acknowledging receipt or naming who provided it. Iris will call this a technicality; the statute, however, makes the signed acknowledgment part of the requirement. **Likely not satisfied.**
4. *No duress, fraud, undue influence or incapacity.* Mateo will point to the ultimatum ("No agreement, no wedding") and the 150 guests three days away. Iris will respond that a party may lawfully condition marriage on a premarital agreement and that pressure from a wedding date is a factor to weigh, not duress in itself (*Bonds*). The ultimatum is likely hard bargaining rather than duress, but the timing reinforces the policy of the seven-day rule.
5. *Other factors.* Mateo is educated and knew for five weeks that an agreement was coming, which favors Iris, but these factors cannot replace the specific statutory requirements.

**Conclusion.** Because the court must find every factor, the failure of the seven-day requirement, and likely of the signed-acknowledgment requirement, means the agreement is **deemed involuntary and unenforceable** (§ 1615(a)(1), (c)).

**C. Unconscionability and disclosure (§ 1615(a)(2)).** Alternatively, an agreement is unenforceable if it was unconscionable when signed **and**, before signing, the challenger (1) was not given fair, reasonable and full disclosure of the other party's property and obligations, (2) did not voluntarily and expressly waive further disclosure in writing, and (3) did not have and could not reasonably have had adequate knowledge. Unconscionability is a question of law for the court.
- *Disclosure.* The schedule omitted the $600,000 signing bonus, about a third of Iris's liquid wealth; Mateo signed no written waiver of disclosure and did not know of the bonus. The disclosure prongs are met.
- *Unconscionability.* Iris will argue the terms are mutual and common: each spouse keeps his or her own earnings. Mateo will argue that, given her $320,000 salary against his $75,000, the clause guaranteed that almost everything acquired during the marriage would be hers, and the support waiver left him with nothing on divorce. Because the property terms are reciprocal and widely used, a court would probably **not** find them unconscionable when signed.
- *Conclusion.* This ground likely fails, but it does not matter given the voluntariness defect.

**D. Spousal-support waiver (§ 1612(c)).** A premarital provision regarding spousal support is unenforceable if the party against whom enforcement is sought was not represented by independent counsel when it was signed, or if it is unconscionable at the time of enforcement. Mateo had no lawyer. **The support waiver is unenforceable** even if the rest of the agreement survived.

**Overall:** the agreement is unenforceable, and the default community property rules govern.

### 2(a). The brokerage account

Property acquired during marriage while domiciled in California is presumed CP, and a spouse's earnings during marriage, and property bought with them, are CP (§ 760). Iris opened the account in September 2021, after the wedding, and funded it with her salary and with company stock that vested during the marriage as compensation for her work. Both are community earnings. Taking title in her own name does not make the account her SP; between spouses, character changes only by a valid transmutation (*In re Marriage of Valli*), and there was none. Iris's only argument is the premarital agreement, which is unenforceable.

**Conclusion.** The account is CP. Each spouse is entitled to half of its $1,400,000 value, in kind or by offset (§ 2550). (Mateo's own salary during the marriage was equally CP.)

### 2(b). The Larkspur Lane house

**Character at acquisition.** The house was bought during marriage with joint savings of both salaries and a loan both signed, and title was taken "as community property." It is CP under § 760, and at dissolution the joint-title presumption of § 2581 reinforces that result. (Even if the premarital agreement had been enforceable, the spouses' express choice of community property title would have to be reconciled with it; the point is moot.)

**The March 2025 deed: a transmutation?** Spouses may change CP into one spouse's SP, but a transmutation is valid only if made in writing by an express declaration that is made, joined in, consented to or accepted by the spouse whose interest is adversely affected (§ 852(a)). The writing must state on its face that the character or ownership of the property is being changed (*Estate of MacDonald*). The deed conveys the house from both spouses "to Iris Vale, a married woman, as her sole and separate property," and Mateo signed it as a grantor. A deed with words of grant that expressly designates the property as the grantee's SP satisfies this test (*Estate of Bibb*). On its face, **the deed meets § 852(a).**

**Presumption of undue influence (§ 721).** Spouses are fiduciaries who owe each other the highest good faith and fair dealing and may not take unfair advantage of each other. When an interspousal transaction gives one spouse an advantage, it is presumed to have been induced by undue influence, and the advantaged spouse must prove that the other acted freely and voluntarily, with full knowledge of the facts and a complete understanding of the effect of the transfer (§ 721(b); *In re Marriage of Haines*). Satisfying § 852 is only the first step.
- *Advantage.* Iris gained Mateo's half of $700,000 of equity, so the presumption arises.
- *Iris's rebuttal.* Mateo is an educated adult who signed a short, plainly worded deed and is charged with knowing what he signed.
- *Why the rebuttal fails.* Iris told Mateo the deed was for a refinance that the lender required and was "just paperwork." There was no refinance. She had consulted a divorce lawyer the month before and did not tell him. He did not read the deed and had no advice. A spouse who signs because of a false explanation does not act "with full knowledge of the facts" or understand that he is giving away his half of the family home.

**Conclusion.** Iris cannot rebut the presumption; the transmutation is invalid. **The house remains CP**, and each spouse is entitled to half of the $700,000 equity, subject to the remedies below.

### 3. Mateo's remedies

**A. The fiduciary duty.** In managing community property, each spouse must act with the highest good faith and fair dealing and without taking unfair advantage of the other (§ 721(b)). The duty includes full disclosure of all material facts about the existence, character and value of assets in which the community has or may have an interest (§ 1100(e)); access, on request, to books and records concerning community transactions; and true and full information on request about any transaction concerning community property (§ 721(b)).

**B. The deed.** Iris breached the duty by obtaining the deed through a false explanation and by concealing her plan to divorce. Under § 1101, a spouse whose interest in the community estate is impaired by a breach may obtain:
- an accounting and a court order restoring his name to title (§ 1101(b), (c));
- **50%** of any asset transferred in breach of the fiduciary duty, plus attorney's fees and costs (§ 1101(g)); or
- **100%** of the asset if the breach falls within Civil Code § 3294, that is, involves oppression, fraud or malice (§ 1101(h); *In re Marriage of Rossi*).

*Mateo's argument for 100%.* Iris made a knowing misrepresentation ("just paperwork," a nonexistent refinance) to obtain title while secretly preparing to divorce. That is fraud within § 3294.

*Iris's argument.* She has not sold or borrowed against the house, the deed will be set aside, and Mateo has lost nothing; the 100% award is a penalty for the most egregious conduct, such as hiding an entire asset.

*Likely result.* The deliberate deception to strip Mateo of his half of the home is fraud, so Mateo has a strong claim to an award of the **entire house (100%)** under § 1101(h). At a minimum, the court will set the deed aside, treat the house as CP, and award Mateo his attorney's fees and costs under § 1101(g).

**C. The brokerage account.** Iris refused Mateo's 2024 request to see the statements, saying the account was hers "under the prenup." The duty to give access and information extends to assets in which the community **has or may have** an interest; given the doubtful validity of the agreement, the account plainly qualified. Iris may claim a good-faith belief that the account was her SP, but a fiduciary who is unsure must disclose, not refuse. **This was a breach.** Mateo may obtain an accounting of the account (§ 1101(b)). Because he knew the account existed, it was not an "undisclosed" asset, so the 50% or 100% awards are unlikely on these facts. In the dissolution, however, Iris must list and value the account in her mandatory disclosure declarations (§§ 2100–2107); if she omits it, § 1101(g)–(h) and statutory sanctions become available.

### Conclusion

The premarital agreement is unenforceable because Mateo did not have seven days with the final agreement (and signed no acknowledgment of the written explanation), and the support waiver fails independently under § 1612(c). The brokerage account is CP. The house is CP because Iris cannot rebut the presumption that the deed was procured by undue influence. Mateo may set the deed aside and has a strong claim to 100% of the house under § 1101(h), plus fees, and is entitled to an accounting of the brokerage account.

**Common mistakes**
1. Applying the § 721 undue-influence presumption to the premarital agreement. Prospective spouses are not fiduciaries (*Bonds*); § 1615 controls.
2. Counting the seven days from the first draft. The period runs from first receipt of the **final** agreement, and a new support waiver is a substantive change.
3. Treating a signed waiver of counsel as curing everything. Every § 1615(c) factor must be found.
4. Concluding that the deed fails § 852. Its express "sole and separate property" language satisfies § 852; the deed fails only because Iris cannot rebut the presumption of undue influence.
5. Forgetting that the support waiver needs independent counsel under § 1612(c) even if the property terms stand.
