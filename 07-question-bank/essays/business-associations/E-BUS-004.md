## Facts

Sandpiper Robotics, Inc. is a privately held California corporation that makes warehouse robots. Its articles state, "This corporation is a close corporation," and limit the number of shareholders to 35. Its 1,000,000 shares are held by Ward (600,000), Lena (300,000) and Kofi (100,000). Each share certificate bears a legend referring to a shareholders' agreement. In 2020, all three signed a written Shareholders' Agreement providing that (1) each will vote his or her shares to elect Ward, Lena and Kofi as Sandpiper's three directors, and (2) "Lena shall serve as Chief Technology Officer at an annual salary of not less than $240,000 for so long as she holds at least 20% of the shares."

In 2025 Ward and Lena clashed over strategy. In January 2026, while still CTO, Lena started a small consulting firm that advises farms on irrigation drones. Sandpiper has no agricultural products or customers.

At the annual shareholders' meeting on February 10, 2026, three directors were to be elected. Ward nominated himself, Kofi and Hugo, Sandpiper's CFO. Lena nominated herself. No one announced an intention to cumulate votes. Ward and Kofi voted all their shares for Ward, Kofi and Hugo, who were elected. On February 12, the new board voted 3–0 to remove Lena as CTO, effective immediately. Ward said, "The board has the right to pick its officers."

On March 2, Lena sent Sandpiper a written demand to inspect the minutes of all board meetings since January 2024, the general ledger and the list of shareholders, stating that she wished "to value my shares and to investigate payments to Ward-controlled entities." Ward refused, writing, "You are a competitor fishing for our customer data."

In April, Titan Automation Corp., a Delaware corporation whose common stock is registered with the SEC and listed on the New York Stock Exchange, agreed to acquire Sandpiper by merger for $30 per share in cash. In a side agreement, Titan agreed to pay Ward a $3 million "consulting fee" over three years and to appoint him to Titan's board when the merger closed. Two months earlier, Ward had rejected an offer from another company to acquire Sandpiper for $36 per share on otherwise similar terms, with no side payments. Sandpiper's board approved the Titan merger, and on May 20 the shareholders approved it: Ward and Kofi voted yes; Lena voted no. Lena believes her shares are worth more than $30 each.

The merger closed and Ward joined Titan's board on June 1. On June 15, Ward bought 10,000 Titan shares on the NYSE at $40 per share. At a Titan board meeting on October 1, he learned that Titan's largest customer, which accounts for 30% of Titan's revenue, had decided to end its contract, and that Titan would announce this on October 30. On October 20, Ward sold 10,000 Titan shares on the NYSE at $52 per share. After the October 30 announcement, Titan's stock fell to $38.

## Call of the Question

1. Was Lena entitled to remain a director and Chief Technology Officer of Sandpiper, and what remedies does she have? Discuss.
2. Must Sandpiper permit the inspection Lena demanded? Discuss.
3. What rights does Lena have regarding the Titan merger? Discuss.
4. What liability, if any, does Ward have under federal securities law for his trading in Titan shares? Discuss.

## Model Answer

General corporate principles (MBCA and Delaware case law) apply, with California Corporations Code rules noted where they differ. Sandpiper is a California corporation, so California's rules are directly relevant to its internal affairs.

### 1. Lena's board seat and CTO position

**A. The voting agreement.** Shareholders may agree in a signed writing how they will vote their shares. Such voting (pooling) agreements are generally valid and may be specifically enforced (MBCA § 7.31; Corp. Code § 706). Shareholders may vote in their own interest, so an agreement about how they vote *as shareholders* raises no fiduciary problem.

The 2020 agreement is in writing, signed by all three shareholders, and requires each to vote for Ward, Lena and Kofi. At the February meeting Ward and Kofi voted all their shares for Hugo instead of Lena. That is a clear breach. Ward will argue that strategic disagreement freed him, but the agreement has no such exception, and the certificate legends gave notice of it. **Lena was entitled to her seat.** Because damages for the loss of a board seat are hard to measure, a court is likely to order specific enforcement, for example by setting aside the February election and ordering a new election at which Ward and Kofi vote as agreed.

**B. Cumulative voting.** In California, every shareholder may cumulate votes in a director election (shares held × number of directors to be elected, cast for one or more candidates) if the candidate was nominated before the voting and a shareholder gave notice at the meeting, before voting, of the intention to cumulate. Only a listed corporation may eliminate cumulative voting (Corp. Code §§ 301.5, 708). Under the MBCA and Delaware, there is no cumulative voting unless the articles provide for it.

With 1,000,000 shares voting for three seats, a shareholder needs 1,000,000 ÷ (3 + 1) + 1 = 250,001 shares to guarantee one seat. Lena holds 300,000. Had she given notice and cast all 900,000 of her votes for herself, Ward and Kofi's combined 2,100,000 votes could not have given three other candidates more than 900,000 votes each. Lena nominated herself but gave no notice, and no one else did, so straight voting applied and the 70% bloc elected all three. Sandpiper is unlisted, so California's rule applied. **Lena could have protected her seat by cumulating, but she lost that protection by failing to give notice.** Under the MBCA default she would have had no cumulative-voting right at all. Her remedy therefore rests on the voting agreement.

**C. The CTO clause.**
- *The board's power over officers.* The business and affairs of a corporation are managed by or under the direction of its board, which chooses officers. Ordinarily officers serve at the board's pleasure and may be removed at any time, but removal does not destroy the officer's contract rights, which survive as a damages claim (Corp. Code § 312; MBCA §§ 8.43–8.44). Traditionally, a shareholder agreement that dictates who will be an officer is invalid because it interferes with the directors' independent judgment. This is Ward's argument: "the board has the right to pick its officers."
- *Close-corporation agreements.* Under general law, the shareholders of a non-public corporation may, by an agreement signed by all of them, vary the usual governance rules, including by fixing who will serve as officers (MBCA § 7.32). In California, a statutory close corporation is one whose articles state that it is a close corporation and limit holders to 35 or fewer; an agreement among all its shareholders is not invalid on the ground that it interferes with the board's discretion (Corp. Code §§ 158, 300(b)).

Sandpiper's articles contain the close-corporation statement and the 35-holder limit; the agreement was signed by **all** shareholders; and the certificates are legended. The CTO clause is therefore valid under both § 7.32 and § 300(b). Its condition is met, because Lena still holds 30%.

*Ward's "cause" argument.* Ward may argue that Lena's new consulting firm was disloyal competition that justified removing her despite the agreement. An officer may not compete with the corporation while serving it. But her firm advises farms on irrigation drones, and Sandpiper has no agricultural products or customers. There is no competition, and the removal followed the boardroom fight, not any discovery of disloyalty.

*Remedy.* The new board's 3–0 vote breached the agreement. Hugo was not a party to it, but Ward and Kofi were, and they are bound to act consistently with it. Lena may seek an order requiring the parties to restore her as CTO or, at a minimum, damages for her lost salary of at least $240,000 a year for as long as she would have held 20% or more. Because Sandpiper is being merged out of existence, damages are the more realistic remedy.

**Conclusion.** Lena was entitled to remain a director (voting agreement) and CTO (valid close-corporation agreement). Ward and Kofi are liable for breaching the agreement.

### 2. Lena's inspection demand

In California, any shareholder may, on written demand, inspect and copy the accounting books and records and the minutes of shareholder, board and committee proceedings for a purpose reasonably related to the holder's interests as a shareholder (Corp. Code § 1601). A holder of at least 5% of the voting shares has an **absolute** right to inspect the shareholder list (§ 1600). A director has an absolute right to inspect all books and records (§ 1602). Under the MBCA (§ 16.02) and Delaware (DGCL § 220), access to the books and minutes likewise requires a good-faith demand for a proper purpose, and Delaware requires the demand to be made under oath.

- **Shareholder list.** Lena owns 30%, far above 5%, so her right to the list is absolute. Ward's "competitor" objection is irrelevant to it.
- **General ledger and minutes.** Lena gave a written demand stating two purposes. Valuing one's shares is a proper purpose, especially with a sale of the company on the horizon. Investigating possible mismanagement or self-dealing ("payments to Ward-controlled entities") is a classic proper purpose. Ward argues that Lena is a competitor seeking customer data, which would be an improper purpose. The facts do not support that charge: her firm serves farms, Sandpiper sells to warehouses, and she asked for minutes and the ledger, not customer files. Even if the court had some concern, its remedy is to limit or condition the inspection, for example with a confidentiality order, not to deny it.
- **Director's right.** Had Ward and Kofi honored the voting agreement, Lena would still be a director with an absolute right to inspect everything. Her removal in breach of the agreement strengthens her claim.

**Conclusion.** Sandpiper must permit the inspection, possibly subject to confidentiality conditions, and the court may award Lena her costs because the refusal was unjustified.

### 3. Lena's rights regarding the merger

**A. Approval.** A merger requires board approval and shareholder approval. In California, as in Delaware, the shareholders must approve by a majority of the outstanding shares entitled to vote (Corp. Code §§ 152, 1201; DGCL § 251(c)); the MBCA instead requires more votes for than against at a meeting at which a majority of the votes entitled to be cast is present (MBCA §§ 7.25, 11.04). Sandpiper's board approved it, and Ward and Kofi, holding 70% of the outstanding shares, voted yes. Lena cannot block the merger by voting no; it was validly approved.

**B. Appraisal (dissenters') rights.** A shareholder who does not vote in favor of a merger may demand that the corporation buy her shares at fair value, if she follows the statutory steps precisely (Corp. Code §§ 1300–1312; MBCA ch. 13; DGCL § 262).
- *Trigger and vote.* A cash merger is a covered reorganization, and Lena voted no.
- *Market-out.* Listed shares generally have no appraisal right, but Sandpiper's shares are not listed, so the exception does not apply.
- *Steps.* Lena must make a timely written demand and submit her shares as the statute requires. A missed step forfeits the right.
- *Value.* In California, fair value is fair market value on the day before the first announcement of the merger terms, excluding any appreciation or depreciation caused by the merger itself. Lena will point to the rejected $36 offer as evidence that $30 undervalues the company. The court may consider it, though Titan will argue that a competing bid reflects that bidder's own synergies.

**C. Ward's duty as controlling shareholder.** A controlling shareholder owes fiduciary duties to the minority. Under *Jones v. H.F. Ahmanson & Co.* (Cal. 1969), majority shareholders may not use their control to benefit themselves alone or in a manner detrimental to the minority; any use of control must benefit all shareholders proportionately. Under Delaware law, when a controller receives a non-pro-rata benefit from a transaction, the controller must prove its **entire fairness**, meaning both fair dealing and a fair price (*Weinberger*; *Sinclair*).
- *Control.* Ward owns 60% and controls the board and the shareholder vote.
- *Non-pro-rata benefit.* Ward alone received a $3 million "consulting fee" and a Titan board seat. Meanwhile, every shareholder received $6 per share less than the rejected $36 offer. That is $1.8 million less for Lena's 300,000 shares and $3.6 million less for Ward's 600,000 shares. The fee nearly offsets Ward's own shortfall, which strongly suggests that part of the price was diverted to Ward as a side payment.
- *Ward's defenses.* He will say the fee pays for genuine future consulting and that Titan's deal was more certain. But the other offer was on similar terms, and he has the burden of proving fair dealing and fair price. A process run by the person receiving a side payment, approved by a board consisting of himself, his ally Kofi and his own CFO, is not fair dealing.

*Effect on appraisal.* Under Delaware law and the MBCA, appraisal is not the exclusive remedy when the transaction involves fraud or unfair dealing by a controller, or is an interested transaction not approved by disinterested directors or shareholders (*Weinberger*; MBCA § 13.40). On that view Lena may sue Ward for damages (at least the $6-per-share shortfall, or a share of the side payments). California's statute is stricter. A shareholder who has dissenters' rights may not attack the validity of the reorganization or have it set aside, except to test whether the required shares were legally voted, unless the corporation is controlled by another party to the reorganization (Corp. Code § 1312(a)–(b)). Ward will argue that this confines Lena to appraisal, and the controlled-party exception does not apply because Ward himself is not a party to the merger. Lena will answer that she does not attack the merger but sues Ward personally for diverting part of the merger consideration to himself, the non-pro-rata use of control that *Jones v. Ahmanson* forbids. Because the scope of California's exclusivity rule is contested, Lena should perfect her appraisal rights, using the side payments as evidence that $30 understates fair value, while pursuing the claim against Ward.

**Conclusion.** Lena cannot stop the merger, but she may demand appraisal. Under general law she likely also recovers from Ward for breach of his duty as controlling shareholder; in California, Ward will argue that § 1312 confines her to appraisal, where the side payments are at least strong evidence of fair value.

### 4. Ward's federal securities liability

**A. Rule 10b-5 (classical insider trading).** Rule 10b-5 prohibits fraud, including material omissions, in connection with the purchase or sale of any security. A corporate insider who trades in the company's securities on the basis of material nonpublic information must disclose it or abstain (*Chiarella*; *Dirks*).
- *Insider.* From June 1, Ward was a Titan director.
- *Material.* The loss of a customer that provides 30% of revenue is information a reasonable investor would consider important, as the drop from $52 to $38 after the announcement confirms.
- *Nonpublic, and trading on it.* Ward learned the news at the October 1 board meeting and sold on October 20, before the October 30 announcement. Under Rule 10b5-1, trading while aware of the information is trading "on the basis of" it. Nothing suggests a pre-arranged trading plan.
- *Scienter; interstate means.* He sold knowingly, through the NYSE.

**Ward violated Rule 10b-5.** He avoided a loss of about $140,000 (($52 − $38) × 10,000). He faces an SEC civil penalty of up to three times that amount, possible criminal prosecution, and suits by investors who traded contemporaneously, whose recovery is capped at the loss avoided (Exchange Act § 20A). His June purchase, made before he learned anything, is not itself a 10b-5 violation.

**B. Section 16(b).** Any profit a director, officer or more-than-10% holder of a reporting company realizes from a purchase and sale of its equity securities within less than six months belongs to the company, regardless of intent or inside information.
- Titan has a class of equity securities registered under the Exchange Act.
- Ward was a director at both the purchase (June 15) and the sale (October 20).
- The two trades are about four months apart.
- Profit is computed by matching the lowest-priced purchase with the highest-priced sale: ($52 − $40) × 10,000 = **$120,000**.

Ward cannot defend on the ground that he bought innocently: § 16(b) is strict liability. Titan may sue to recover the $120,000, and any Titan shareholder may sue on Titan's behalf if Titan fails to do so within 60 days after a request. **Ward is liable to Titan for $120,000 under § 16(b)**, in addition to his Rule 10b-5 exposure.

**Common mistakes**
1. Treating a shareholders' voting agreement as an improper restraint on directors. Shareholders may bind how they vote their shares; the close-corporation statute is needed only for the CTO clause, which binds the board.
2. Forgetting that California cumulative voting must be triggered by notice at the meeting.
3. Treating the "competitor" objection as defeating Lena's right to the shareholder list. A 5% holder's right to the list is absolute in California.
4. Stopping at appraisal without addressing the side payments. Under general law they keep a fiduciary claim open; for a California corporation, argue that claim against § 1312(a)'s exclusivity.
5. Requiring inside information or intent for § 16(b), or forgetting that it applies only because Titan is a reporting company.
