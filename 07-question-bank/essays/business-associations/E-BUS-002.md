## Facts

Omar Haddad, a food chemist, planned to form a corporation, Verdant Labs, Inc., to develop plant-based food colorings. In January 2024, before forming the corporation, he signed a two-year lease of laboratory space from Coastline Properties at $10,000 a month. The lease named the tenant as "Verdant Labs, Inc., a California corporation to be formed," and Omar signed it "Omar Haddad, President." Coastline's leasing manager knew that Verdant had not yet been incorporated. The lease says nothing about whether Omar is personally bound.

In March 2024, Omar filed articles of incorporation for Verdant Labs, Inc. Verdant's board consists of Omar (who is also CEO and owns 40% of the shares), Paula (5%) and Quinn (5%). Outside investors own the other 50%. One of them, Sienna, bought 10% of the shares in March 2024 and still holds them. Verdant's articles eliminate the directors' personal liability for monetary damages "to the fullest extent permitted by law." The board never voted on the lease, but Verdant moved into the laboratory in April 2024 and paid the rent for 18 months. It stopped paying in October 2025, with $60,000 of rent remaining.

In June 2024, Verdant needed a specialized extraction machine. Omar proposed buying one for $900,000 from HaddadTech LLC, a company owned entirely by his wife. He told the board that his wife owned HaddadTech. He did not tell the board that an unrelated manufacturer had given him a written quote of $650,000 for a comparable machine. At a 15-minute meeting, Paula and Quinn voted to approve the purchase; Omar abstained. Paula and Quinn asked no questions about price and sought no other quotes. Quinn later explained, "Omar knows the equipment far better than we do."

In November 2025, at a board meeting, the directors learned that GreenLeaf Foods had offered to acquire Verdant for $50 a share and had asked that the offer be kept confidential. Verdant's shares are not publicly traded; recent private sales had been at about $20 a share. Two days later, after an exchange of emails, Paula bought 2,000 Verdant shares at $20 a share from Tom, a Verdant lab technician who had received the shares as a bonus. She did not mention the offer. That evening Paula told her close friend Rex, "Buy any Verdant shares you can. Trust me — it's a gift from me to you." Rex bought 1,000 shares at $20 a share from another employee. In January 2026, Verdant announced that it would be acquired by GreenLeaf for $50 a share.

Sienna intends to sue Omar, Paula and Quinn over the HaddadTech purchase without first making a demand on the board.

## Call of the Question

1. Is Omar, Verdant, or both liable to Coastline Properties on the lease?
2. What claims may Sienna assert against Omar, Paula and Quinn regarding the HaddadTech purchase, what procedural requirements must she satisfy, and what is the likely outcome?
3. What liability, if any, do Paula and Rex have under federal securities law for their purchases of Verdant shares?

## Model Answer

General corporate principles (the MBCA and Delaware case law) apply. California Corporations Code rules are noted where they differ.

### 1. Liability to Coastline Properties

**A. Omar: promoter liability.** A promoter who makes a contract on behalf of a corporation not yet formed is personally liable on it, and remains liable even after the corporation is formed and adopts the contract, unless the contract shows the parties intended otherwise (for example, that the other party agreed to look only to the future corporation) or there is a novation (MBCA § 2.04; common law).
- *Omar's argument.* Coastline knew Verdant did not yet exist and named Verdant, not Omar, as the tenant. That shows it agreed to look only to the corporation.
- *Coastline's argument.* Both parties' knowledge that the corporation had not been formed is the ordinary promoter situation and does not by itself release the promoter. The lease contains no clause releasing Omar or limiting Coastline to the corporation. At signing, the only person who could be bound was Omar.

Without language releasing him, **Omar is personally liable** for the $60,000.

**B. Verdant: adoption.** A corporation is not bound by a promoter's pre-incorporation contract until it adopts it, either expressly (a board resolution) or impliedly (knowingly accepting its benefits). Adoption takes effect when made; it does not relate back, because the corporation did not exist at signing. The board never voted on the lease, but Verdant, with its CEO's full knowledge of the terms, moved into the laboratory and paid rent for 18 months. That is **implied adoption**, and **Verdant is liable** on the lease.

**C. No novation.** A novation, an agreement among the corporation, the promoter and the third party substituting the corporation for the promoter, would release Omar. Coastline's acceptance of rent from Verdant shows it accepted Verdant as an obligor, but not that it agreed to release Omar. No novation occurred.

**Conclusion.** Both Omar and Verdant are liable to Coastline, which may recover the $60,000 once. Omar may seek indemnity from Verdant, which adopted the lease.

### 2. Sienna's claims about the HaddadTech purchase

**A. Derivative, not direct.** A claim is derivative when the injury is to the corporation and any recovery would go to it, as with self-dealing and mismanagement; it is direct when the shareholder suffers an independent injury (*Tooley*). The harm is Verdant's overpayment for the machine (about $250,000), suffered by all shareholders in proportion to their holdings. **Sienna's claims are derivative.**

**B. Standing.** A derivative plaintiff must have owned shares when the challenged transaction occurred, must remain a shareholder throughout the suit, and must fairly and adequately represent the corporation. Sienna bought in March 2024, before the June 2024 purchase, and still holds her shares. Nothing suggests a conflict with the other shareholders. **She has standing.**

**C. Demand.** Before suing derivatively, a shareholder must make a demand on the board, or plead with particularity why demand would be futile.
- *MBCA.* Under the MBCA's universal-demand rule, Sienna must make a written demand and wait 90 days, unless the board rejects it sooner or irreparable injury would result (MBCA § 7.42). She cannot skip the demand.
- *Futility (Delaware, and persuasive in California).* Demand is excused if, for at least half of the directors, a director received a material personal benefit from the transaction, faces a substantial likelihood of liability, or lacks independence from someone who did.
  - Omar is interested: his wife's company received $900,000.
  - Paula and Quinn approved the purchase without inquiry and face potential liability for the decision. They will argue that the exculpation clause makes a care claim against them unlikely to succeed, so they face no substantial likelihood of liability, and that nothing shows they lack independence from Omar.
  - Sienna will respond that the claim against them is more than a care claim: they approved an interested deal without asking about price (see below). Quinn's statement that "Omar knows the equipment far better than we do" also suggests deference to Omar that undermines their independent judgment.
  - Omar alone is clearly compromised, which is one of three directors. Futility therefore turns on whether Paula or Quinn faces a substantial likelihood of liability on a claim the exculpation clause does not cover (see Part F) or lacks independence from Omar. On these facts, that is doubtful. Sienna's safer course is to make a written demand, which the MBCA requires anyway, and, if the board refuses, to challenge the refusal as not made in good faith by an informed, disinterested board.
- *California.* Under Corp. Code § 800(b)(2), Sienna must allege with particularity her efforts to obtain board action or her reasons for not making the effort, and must inform the corporation or board in writing of the ultimate facts of each cause of action or deliver a copy of the complaint. Unlike the MBCA and Delaware, California also lets the corporation or a defendant move for a bond (security for expenses) on the ground that the suit has no reasonable possibility of benefiting the corporation. Given the $250,000 overpayment, that motion should fail.

**D. Omar: duty of loyalty.** A director must act in good faith in the corporation's best interests and may not use his position to benefit himself at the corporation's expense. A transaction between the corporation and an entity in which a director has a material financial interest is a conflicting-interest transaction. Under the MBCA and Delaware's traditional approach, it is protected from challenge only if (1) after **full disclosure of the material facts**, it is approved by a majority of the disinterested directors, or (2) by the disinterested shareholders, or (3) it is **fair** to the corporation.
- *Interest.* HaddadTech is wholly owned by Omar's wife, so Omar has a material financial interest, both through the family's finances and through his relationship with her.
- *Disclosure and approval.* Omar disclosed his wife's ownership, and the two disinterested directors approved while he abstained. But he concealed the most important fact bearing on fairness: an independent $650,000 quote for a comparable machine. Disclosing the conflict is not enough; the material facts of the transaction must be disclosed. Approval obtained without them gives no protection.
- *Fairness.* Without a valid safe harbor, the transaction stands only if it was fair. Paying $900,000 when a comparable machine cost $650,000, about 38% more, is not a fair price. Omar may argue that HaddadTech's machine had features worth the premium, but his decision to hide the competing quote undercuts that argument.
- *California.* California's § 310 likewise protects such a contract only after full disclosure, and when the protection depends on disinterested-board approval, the transaction must also be **just and reasonable** to the corporation when approved (§ 310(a)(2)). The deal fails both requirements. Without valid approval, Omar must prove the transaction just and reasonable (§ 310(a)(3)), which he cannot.

**Omar breached the duty of loyalty.** The business judgment rule does not protect an interested director. Sienna can recover for Verdant the overpayment (about $250,000) from Omar, or seek rescission of the purchase.

**E. Paula and Quinn: duty of care and the business judgment rule.** A director must act in good faith, in the corporation's best interests, and with the care, including reasonable inquiry, that an ordinarily prudent person in a like position would use (MBCA § 8.30; Corp. Code § 309). A director may rely in good faith on officers and experts, but not when the director has knowledge that makes reliance unwarranted. The business judgment rule presumes that disinterested directors acted on an informed basis and in good faith; the presumption is rebutted by showing that the directors failed to inform themselves of material information reasonably available, which Delaware measures by gross negligence (*Smith v. Van Gorkom*); California's § 309 asks whether the director used the care, including reasonable inquiry, of an ordinarily prudent person.
- *Paula and Quinn's argument.* They are disinterested, Omar disclosed his conflict, buying lab equipment is an ordinary business decision, and they reasonably relied on the CEO's technical expertise.
- *Sienna's argument.* They approved a $900,000 purchase from the CEO's wife's company after a 15-minute meeting, asked nothing about price, and sought no other quotes. The disclosed conflict was a red flag that made blind reliance on Omar unreasonable: he was the one person whose judgment on the price could not be trusted. Quinn's comment confirms that they deferred to Omar instead of inquiring.

They acted on a materially uninformed basis in an obviously conflicted deal. **The presumption is likely rebutted, and Paula and Quinn likely breached the duty of care.**

**F. The exculpation clause.** A corporation's articles may eliminate directors' personal liability for damages for breach of the duty of care, but not for breaches involving bad faith, intentional misconduct or knowing violation of law, improper personal benefit, and certain other categories (MBCA § 2.02(b)(4); DGCL § 102(b)(7)). California's list of exclusions is broader: it also excludes reckless disregard of a known risk of serious injury, an unexcused pattern of inattention amounting to abdication, and liability arising from interested-director transactions (§ 204(a)(10)).
- *Omar:* the clause does not protect him. His breach is one of loyalty, and he received an improper personal benefit through his wife's company.
- *Paula and Quinn:* the clause likely bars damages for ordinary negligence. Sienna will argue that ignoring an obvious conflict on a $900,000 deal was reckless, and in California that the claim arises from an interested-director transaction. They will respond that one hurried approval is negligence, not recklessness or abdication.

On these facts, Paula and Quinn are likely protected from damages unless the court finds their conduct reckless. Equitable relief, such as rescission, remains available against the transaction.

**Likely outcome.** Sienna should make a demand. The MBCA requires one, and futility is doubtful because only Omar is clearly compromised. If the suit proceeds, Omar is liable for about $250,000 or rescission. Paula and Quinn breached the duty of care but probably escape damages under the exculpation clause unless their conduct is found reckless.

### 3. Federal securities liability: Paula and Rex

**A. Paula: classical insider trading under Rule 10b-5.** Rule 10b-5 prohibits fraud, including material omissions, in connection with the purchase or sale of any security, through an instrumentality of interstate commerce. It applies to shares of a privately held corporation. A corporate insider who trades on the basis of material nonpublic information must disclose it or abstain from trading (*Chiarella*).
- *Insider.* Paula is a director.
- *Material, nonpublic information.* A $50-a-share acquisition offer for shares selling at $20 is information a reasonable investor would consider important. It was confidential at GreenLeaf's request.
- *Trading on it with scienter.* Paula bought two days after learning of the offer, knowing it and deliberately saying nothing.
- *Connection with a purchase; interstate means.* She bought the shares, negotiating by email.

**Paula violated Rule 10b-5.** Tom, the seller, has a private action. Because Paula had a duty to disclose and stayed silent, his reliance is presumed (*Affiliated Ute*). He can show economic loss and loss causation: he sold at $20 shares that were worth about $50 once the offer became known, a difference of about $60,000 on 2,000 shares. Paula also faces an SEC enforcement action (injunction, disgorgement and civil penalties) and possible criminal prosecution. Section 16(b) short-swing liability does not apply, even though the acquisition may close within six months of Paula's purchase, because § 16(b) reaches only insiders of companies with a class of registered equity securities, and Verdant's shares are not registered.

**B. Rex: tippee liability.** A tippee who trades on material nonpublic information is liable if (1) the tipper breached a fiduciary duty by disclosing it for a personal benefit and (2) the tippee knew or should have known of the breach (*Dirks*). A personal benefit includes making a gift of confidential information to a trading friend or relative (*Salman*).
- *Breach for personal benefit.* Paula, a director bound to keep the offer confidential, told her close friend to buy, calling it "a gift from me to you." That is the gift of information to a friend that *Salman* treats as a personal benefit.
- *Knowledge.* Rex will argue Paula never told him the offer itself. But he knew she was close to Verdant, and her urging ("Trust me ... it's a gift") signaled that she was passing on confidential inside information. He at least should have known.

**Rex is liable as a tippee** for his trades, which gained about $30,000. **Paula is also liable as the tipper** for Rex's trading profits, in addition to her own trades. Paula's disclosure also breached her duty of confidentiality to Verdant, which supports liability under the misappropriation theory as an alternative. Rule 14e-3 does not apply, because GreenLeaf made a private acquisition offer, not a tender offer.

**Common mistakes**
1. Saying adoption by the corporation releases the promoter. Only a novation or an agreement to look solely to the corporation does.
2. Treating Omar's disclosure of his wife's ownership as full disclosure. The safe harbor requires the material facts, including the competing quote.
3. Applying the business judgment rule to the interested director.
4. Forgetting that Rule 10b-5 covers private-company shares sold face to face, or applying § 16(b) to a company with no registered securities.
5. Missing *Salman*: a gift of inside information to a friend is a personal benefit to the tipper.
