## Facts

In early 2023, Dana Reyes, a real-estate developer, and Eli Strand, a retired engineer, decided to redevelop a vacant warehouse on Cove Street in San Diego into 24 loft apartments. In March 2023, Dana filed articles of organization for Cove GP LLC with the California Secretary of State. The articles state that the LLC will be managed by one manager. Dana and Eli agreed orally that Dana would own 60% and be the sole manager, that Eli would own 40%, and that the LLC's purpose was "to develop residential property in San Diego County, directly or as general partner of development partnerships." They never signed a written operating agreement, never discussed meetings, and never held any. Each contributed $500.

In April 2023, Dana filed a certificate of limited partnership for Cove Street Partners, LP (the "Partnership"), naming Cove GP LLC as its sole general partner. Fiona Lam and nine other investors contributed $3 million and were admitted as limited partners. The Partnership bought the warehouse with that money and a $6 million construction loan secured by the property. The Partnership paid Cove GP LLC a management fee of $15,000 a month.

Dana deposited the fees in Cove GP LLC's bank account but also used that account to pay her country-club dues and her daughter's tuition. In 2025 she transferred $180,000 from the account to her personal account, recording the transfers as "manager distributions." Eli received nothing and was not told.

In June 2024, the Partnership, acting through Cove GP LLC by Dana as manager, signed a $4.2 million construction contract with BuildRight, Inc. BuildRight's president asked Dana for a personal guarantee. Dana refused, and BuildRight signed anyway.

By February 2025 the project was over budget. Fiona began attending weekly site meetings. She told BuildRight's project manager, "Dana is out of her depth. I'm running this project now. Send every change order to me." She then signed four change orders totaling $300,000 as "Fiona Lam, for Cove Street Partners." The project manager believed Fiona had become a general partner. He never looked at the certificate of limited partnership. Dana knew of the change orders and let the work proceed.

In September 2025, a broker told Dana that the parcel next to the warehouse was for sale for $1.1 million and "would be perfect for a second phase of Cove Street." Dana bought it through Reyes Holdings, a company she wholly owns, without telling Eli. Meanwhile Eli, frustrated with Dana, formed his own company and began bidding against other developers, including Dana, for redevelopment sites elsewhere in San Diego. He used no information from Cove GP LLC.

In January 2026, the lender foreclosed on the warehouse. The Partnership has no remaining assets and owes BuildRight $900,000, of which $300,000 is for Fiona's change orders. Cove GP LLC has $2,000 in its account.

## Call of the Question

1. Which of the following, if any, is liable to BuildRight for the $900,000: Cove Street Partners, LP; Cove GP LLC; Dana; Eli; Fiona? Discuss.
2. Did Dana or Eli breach any duty owed to Cove GP LLC or to its other member? Discuss.

## Model Answer

General principles (the uniform limited partnership and LLC acts) apply. California Corporations Code rules are noted where they differ.

### 1. Liability to BuildRight for the $900,000

**A. Cove Street Partners, LP.** A limited partnership is formed by filing a certificate of limited partnership and must have at least one general partner and one limited partner. The general partner manages the business and is the LP's agent. Dana filed the certificate, which named Cove GP LLC as general partner and admitted ten limited partners, so the Partnership validly exists. It signed the $4.2 million contract through its general partner, so it is bound.

The $300,000 of change orders were signed by Fiona, a limited partner, who as such is not the Partnership's agent. But a principal that, knowing the material facts, accepts the benefits of an unauthorized act ratifies it, and ratification relates back. Dana, acting for the general partner, knew of the change orders and let the work proceed, and the Partnership kept the work. The Partnership has therefore ratified the change orders. **The Partnership is liable for the full $900,000**, but it has no assets.

**B. Cove GP LLC as general partner.** A general partner of a limited partnership is personally liable, jointly and severally, for all obligations of the limited partnership. Cove GP LLC is the sole general partner, so it is liable for the entire $900,000, including the ratified change orders. Because the Partnership has no assets left, any requirement that BuildRight first look to the Partnership is no obstacle. **Cove GP LLC is liable**, but it holds only $2,000. BuildRight's real question is whether it can reach the people behind the LLC.

**C. Formation of Cove GP LLC and the statutory shield.** An LLC is formed by filing articles of organization and is a legal entity separate from its members. Its members and managers are not personally liable for its obligations solely by reason of being members or managers. The operating agreement may be written, oral or implied, and the statute fills any gaps. An LLC is member-managed unless it is designated manager-managed: in California by a statement in the articles, under uniform RULLCA by the operating agreement (Corp. Code §§ 17702.01, 17703.04, 17704.07; RULLCA §§ 201, 304, 407).

Dana filed articles in March 2023, so Cove GP LLC exists. The lack of a written operating agreement does not matter: the oral agreement (60/40 ownership, Dana as sole manager, development purpose) is a valid operating agreement. Because the articles state that the LLC is managed by one manager, and the oral agreement makes Dana that manager, it is **manager-managed** under either approach, with Dana as manager. Dana and Eli are therefore shielded from the LLC's debts unless BuildRight can pierce the veil.

**D. Dana: piercing the LLC veil.** An LLC member is subject to alter-ego liability under the same standards that apply to corporate shareholders (Corp. Code § 17703.04). Courts disregard the entity when (1) there is such a unity of interest and ownership that the separate personalities of the entity and its owner no longer exist, and (2) respecting the entity would sanction a fraud or promote injustice (*Associated Vendors*; *Mesler*). Courts pierce reluctantly, and only against owners who took part in the abuse.

*Unity of interest.* No single factor decides; the court weighs them together.
- **Undercapitalization.** Cove GP LLC was formed with $1,000 to act as general partner of a $9 million project, a role that made it liable for every Partnership debt. California treats inadequate capital in light of foreseeable risks as a weighty factor (*Automotriz*; *Minton v. Cavaney*). Dana will answer that a thinly capitalized entity serving as general partner is a standard, lawful way to limit the owners' risk, and that the Partnership itself had $3 million of equity and a $6 million loan.
- **Commingling and personal use.** Dana paid her country-club dues and her daughter's tuition from the LLC's account. That is the use of entity assets for personal purposes, a classic alter-ego factor.
- **Diverting assets to the owner.** Dana moved $180,000 of management fees to herself, labeled as "distributions" that Eli never received and never approved. Stripping the entity while it is liable for the Partnership's debts is the strongest factor of all.
- **Formalities.** BuildRight will point out that the LLC never held a meeting. That is not a factor in California, because failure to hold meetings does not tend to establish alter-ego liability where neither the articles nor the operating agreement expressly requires meetings (Corp. Code § 17703.04(b)). The oral agreement says nothing about meetings. Many states and RULLCA § 304(b) take a similar approach.

*Inequitable result.* Non-payment alone is not enough; BuildRight must show injustice. Dana's best argument is that BuildRight is a **contract creditor** that knew it was dealing with an LP whose general partner was an LLC, asked for a personal guarantee, was refused, and signed anyway. Courts are more reluctant to pierce for a creditor that knowingly assumed the risk. BuildRight will respond that it assumed the risk of an honestly run entity, not one whose sole owner-manager would drain its fee income to herself while the project failed. Siphoning $180,000 out of the general partner while it was liable for the construction debt is the kind of injustice that goes beyond simple non-payment.

*Conclusion.* The case is closer than a tort case would be because BuildRight bargained for no guarantee. But the combination of minimal capital, commingling and the diversion of $180,000 to Dana makes it **likely that a court would pierce the veil and hold Dana personally liable** for the $900,000 (to the extent Cove GP LLC is liable). At a minimum, the court would not let Dana keep the diverted funds while BuildRight goes unpaid.

**E. Eli.** Eli is a 40% member who did not manage the LLC, did not take part in the commingling, and received no money. Veil piercing reaches only owners who participated in the abuse, and a passive investor's mere ownership is not enough. **Eli is not liable** to BuildRight.

**F. Fiona: the limited partner's shield.** A limited partner is not personally liable for the obligations of the limited partnership solely by reason of being a limited partner.
- *General law (ULPA (2001) § 303).* The shield holds **even if the limited partner participates in management and control**. The old control rule was abolished. Under this rule, Fiona is not liable, however actively she ran the site.
- *California (Corp. Code § 15903.03).* California keeps a narrow control rule. A limited partner who participates in control is liable as a general partner, but only to persons who transact business with the partnership with **actual knowledge** of her participation in control and a **reasonable belief, based on her conduct**, that she is a general partner.

Applying California's rule:
- **Participation in control.** Fiona declared, "I'm running this project now," directed that all change orders go to her, and signed them for the Partnership. That is control, not mere consultation or advice.
- **Actual knowledge.** BuildRight's project manager knew of her control firsthand. She told him so and signed his change orders.
- **Reasonable belief based on her conduct.** BuildRight will argue that her words and her signature "for Cove Street Partners" would lead any contractor to think she had become a general partner. Fiona will argue that the belief was unreasonable: the public certificate names Cove GP LLC as the *sole* general partner; BuildRight's own contract was signed by Cove GP LLC as general partner; she never called herself a general partner; and the project manager never checked the certificate. The statute, however, asks whether the belief was reasonable based on *her conduct*, and a limited partnership can add general partners. Taking over the project and signing for the Partnership is conduct consistent with general-partner status. The belief was probably reasonable, though the point is arguable.
- **Extent.** The control rule protects only those who transact business in reliance on the belief. BuildRight signed the original $4.2 million contract in June 2024, before Fiona became involved, so it cannot have relied on her conduct for that contract. Its reliance covers at most the four change orders.

*Conclusion.* Under ULPA (2001), Fiona is not liable. Under California law she is **likely liable for the $300,000 of change orders**, but not for the $600,000 balance of the original contract.

**Summary for call 1.** The Partnership and Cove GP LLC are liable for $900,000 but have essentially no assets. Dana is likely liable for the full amount through veil piercing. Eli is not liable. Fiona is liable for $300,000 only under California's control rule.

### 2. Duties within Cove GP LLC

**Who owes duties.** In a manager-managed LLC, the manager owes the LLC and its members a duty of loyalty and a duty of care, and must discharge them consistently with the contractual obligation of good faith and fair dealing. A member owes no fiduciary duty solely by reason of being a member (Corp. Code § 17704.09; RULLCA § 409). The duty of loyalty requires the manager to (1) account to the LLC for any property, profit or benefit derived from the LLC's activities or property, including the appropriation of an LLC opportunity; (2) refrain from dealing with the LLC as, or on behalf of, an adverse party; and (3) refrain from competing with the LLC. The operating agreement may modify, but not eliminate, the duty of loyalty, and conflicted conduct can be authorized or ratified by the other members after full disclosure. Dana, as manager, owes these duties; Eli does not.

**A. Dana: appropriation of an LLC opportunity (the adjacent parcel).**
- *Was it the LLC's opportunity?* Cove GP LLC's agreed purpose is to develop residential property in San Diego County, directly or as a general partner. The parcel adjoins the LLC's own project, and the broker described it as "perfect for a second phase of Cove Street." Dana learned of it because of her role on that project. It falls squarely within the LLC's line of business.
- *Dana's arguments.* She will argue that the LLC had only a few thousand dollars and could not have paid $1.1 million, and that the opportunity, if anyone's, belonged to the Partnership. Neither defense works well. A fiduciary cannot decide for herself that the entity is unable to take an opportunity; she must disclose it and let the LLC, through its disinterested member, decide or seek financing. And if the opportunity belonged to the Partnership, Dana's diversion is worse, not better, because Cove GP LLC as general partner owed the Partnership a duty not to appropriate its opportunities.
- *No disclosure or consent.* Dana told Eli nothing and bought through her own company. There was no authorization or ratification after full disclosure.

**Conclusion.** Dana breached the duty of loyalty. The LLC may seek a constructive trust over the parcel (Dana would be reimbursed the price she paid) or disgorgement of her profit. Eli can pursue that relief on the LLC's behalf.

**B. Dana: the $180,000 "distributions" and commingling.** A manager must account for LLC property and may not deal with the LLC as an adverse party. Taking LLC money for oneself is both. Under the oral agreement Dana owned 60%. Distributions should follow the agreed shares, yet she took 100% of $180,000, without Eli's knowledge, and labeled it a distribution. She also used the LLC's account for her personal club dues and tuition. Dana may argue that the payments were compensation for her management work. But nothing in the operating agreement gives her a salary, she sought no approval, and she concealed the transfers. Because this is intentional self-dealing, it would also fall outside the limited duty of care, which excludes only ordinary negligence and covers intentional misconduct. **Dana breached the duty of loyalty.** She must repay the LLC the diverted funds and her personal expenses. Eli may also argue that she breached the obligation of good faith and fair dealing toward him as a member.

**C. Eli: competing.**
- *Fiduciary duty.* Cove GP LLC is manager-managed and Eli is not a manager. A member of a manager-managed LLC owes no fiduciary duty, including no duty not to compete, solely by reason of being a member (Corp. Code § 17704.09; RULLCA § 409). Dana will argue that Eli's bids against her company harm the LLC's prospects. But the duty not to compete is imposed on the manager, not on a non-managing member, and nothing in the oral operating agreement adds a non-compete promise.
- *Good faith and fair dealing.* Members must still exercise their rights consistently with the contractual obligation of good faith and fair dealing. Eli used no LLC information and bid openly for unrelated sites, which does not frustrate any right under the operating agreement.

**Conclusion.** **Eli did not breach any duty.** The result would differ if the LLC were member-managed, because then every member would owe the duty of loyalty, including the duty not to compete.

**Common mistakes**
1. Holding the LLC's members liable for the Partnership's debts just because the LLC is a general partner. The LLC's own shield stands until it is pierced.
2. Counting the missed meetings against Dana. In California that is not an alter-ego factor unless the articles or operating agreement require meetings.
3. Shielding Fiona automatically under ULPA (2001) without stating California's narrow control rule, or imposing liability on her for the whole $900,000 when BuildRight could have relied on her conduct only for the change orders.
4. Imposing a duty not to compete on Eli. In a manager-managed LLC only the manager owes fiduciary duties.
5. Accepting "the LLC could not afford it" as a defense to usurping an opportunity the manager never disclosed.

**Accuracy notes.** California's control rule is stated at the level confirmed for Corp. Code § 15903.03 (actual knowledge plus reasonable belief based on the limited partner's conduct). The answer deliberately does not rely on the statutory list of activities that are not "participating in control," which the repo has not confirmed (V-703). Section numbers for the California LLC formation provisions are flagged in the repo (V-729); the substance used here (filing creates the entity; oral operating agreements; the articles designate manager management in California) matches R-BUS-101 and R-BUS-103. The facts make the LLC manager-managed under both the California (articles) and uniform (operating agreement) designation rules, so the answer does not turn on the difference. The rule that a general partner of an LP is jointly and severally liable for all LP obligations is general law (ULPA (2001) § 404).
