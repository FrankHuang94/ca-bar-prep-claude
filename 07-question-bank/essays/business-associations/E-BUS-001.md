## Facts

In January 2023, Nadia and Mateo started a drone photography business in San Diego called SkyFrame Aerial Imaging. Nadia contributed three drones worth $20,000. Mateo, a marketing consultant, contributed $5,000 and his client list. They agreed orally that Nadia would receive 60% and Mateo 40% of the profits, that they would make business decisions together, and that neither would spend more than $10,000 without the other's approval. They filed nothing with the state. They opened a bank account in the name "SkyFrame Aerial Imaging."

SkyFrame hired Pete as a drone pilot at $40 an hour; SkyFrame set his schedule and assigned his jobs. It also hired Ruth, a college student, to keep the books ten hours a week. Instead of wages, Ruth receives 10% of SkyFrame's profits. Ruth has no say in business decisions. Mateo designed SkyFrame's website, which states, "Our Partners: Nadia, Mateo and Ruth." Ruth saw the website, laughed, and said nothing.

**The drone purchase.** In June 2024, without telling Nadia, Mateo signed a contract "for SkyFrame Aerial Imaging" to buy a $60,000 cinema-grade drone from DroneWorks on 90-day credit. Before extending credit, DroneWorks' sales manager read SkyFrame's website and noted in the file, "three partners behind it." SkyFrame has used the drone on many jobs but has not paid for it. When Nadia learned of the purchase, she told Mateo he had "broken our deal."

**The accident.** In August 2024, SkyFrame assigned Pete to film a wedding at a vineyard. Between the ceremony and the reception, while he was supposed to be standing by to film guests arriving at the reception, Pete flew the SkyFrame drone half a mile to film a friend's pool party "for fun." The drone clipped a tree and fell onto Lily, a party guest, injuring her.

**The side business.** In 2024, a real estate brokerage asked Mateo whether SkyFrame could photograph its property listings every month. Mateo replied that he would do the work "personally." Using SkyFrame's drones on weekends, he has earned $30,000 from the brokerage and kept all of it. Nadia learned of this in 2025.

**The LLC.** In January 2025, Nadia and Mateo filed articles of organization for SkyFrame LLC, a member-managed limited liability company, and transferred SkyFrame's drones and equipment to it. They contributed no cash, and the LLC began with $2,000 in its account. Their written operating agreement says nothing about meetings, and they have never held one. They continued to use SkyFrame's bank account for both business expenses and personal ones, including Mateo's rent and Nadia's car payments. In March 2025, SkyFrame LLC signed a three-year lease for a hangar from Hangar Properties at $4,000 a month. Before signing, Hangar Properties ran a credit check on the LLC and asked Nadia and Mateo to guarantee the lease personally. They refused, and Hangar Properties signed anyway. The LLC stopped paying rent in October 2025.

## Call of the Question

1. What type of business entity was SkyFrame before January 2025, and was Ruth a partner?
2. Who is liable to DroneWorks?
3. Who is liable to Lily?
4. Can Hangar Properties hold Nadia and Mateo personally liable on the lease?
5. What claims does Nadia have against Mateo regarding the real estate photography work?

## Model Answer

General principles of agency, partnership and LLC law apply. California follows the Revised Uniform Partnership Act (Corp. Code § 16100 et seq.), so the California sections are noted alongside, and California's LLC rule on meetings is noted where it differs.

### 1. SkyFrame's entity type and Ruth's status

**A. General partnership.** A general partnership is the association of two or more persons to carry on as co-owners a business for profit, whether or not they intend to form a partnership (RUPA § 202; Corp. Code § 16202). No filing or written agreement is required.

Nadia and Mateo associated to run a photography business for profit. They are co-owners: both contributed capital (drones; cash and clients), they share profits 60/40, and they agreed to make business decisions jointly. They filed nothing, so SkyFrame was not a corporation, LLC, limited partnership or LLP. **Before January 2025, SkyFrame was a general partnership of Nadia and Mateo.**

**B. Was Ruth a partner?** A person who receives a share of a business's profits is presumed to be a partner, unless the profits were received as wages or other compensation to an employee (§ 16202(c)(3)). Ruth receives 10% of profits, which raises the presumption. But she receives that share **instead of wages** for bookkeeping, she contributed no capital, and she has no say in decisions. The wage exception applies. **Ruth is an employee, not a partner.**

**C. Partnership by estoppel.** A person who represents herself, or consents to being represented by another, as a partner is liable to a third party who extends credit in reliance on the representation. If the representation is made publicly, she is liable to anyone who relies on it (§ 16308).
- *DroneWorks' argument.* The website publicly called Ruth a partner. Ruth knew of it, said nothing, and let the statement stand on her employer's public site. DroneWorks actually relied on it: its file notes "three partners behind it" before it extended credit.
- *Ruth's argument.* She did not make the representation; Mateo did. She had no control over the website, and laughing it off is not consent.

The question is whether knowing silence is consent. Ruth took no action that adopted the statement, and her role was ministerial. Ruth has the better argument, but the issue is close. If a court found consent, Ruth would be liable to DroneWorks as if she were a partner.

### 2. Liability to DroneWorks

**A. Is the partnership bound?** Each partner is an agent of the partnership for the purpose of its business. An act of a partner for apparently carrying on in the ordinary course the partnership's business, or business of the kind it carries on, binds the partnership, unless the partner had no authority in the matter **and** the third party knew or had received notification of that lack of authority (§ 16301(1)).
- *Actual authority.* Mateo lacked actual authority, because the partners agreed that neither would spend more than $10,000 without the other's approval.
- *Apparent authority in the ordinary course.* Nadia will argue that a $60,000 purchase, three times the value of the original fleet, was extraordinary. DroneWorks will respond that buying drones is the core of a drone photography business; a better drone is a routine equipment purchase for such a firm; and Mateo signed in the business's name. The internal $10,000 limit was oral and undisclosed, and DroneWorks had no knowledge or notification of it.
- *Ratification.* Even if the purchase were outside the ordinary course, a principal ratifies an unauthorized act by knowingly accepting its benefits. After Nadia learned of the purchase, SkyFrame used the drone "on many jobs" rather than returning it. That is ratification.

**SkyFrame is bound on the contract.**

**B. Who is personally liable?** All partners are jointly and severally liable for all partnership obligations (§ 16306). Nadia and Mateo are both personally liable for the $60,000. A creditor must ordinarily exhaust partnership assets first: it must obtain a judgment against the partnership and have execution returned unsatisfied, unless an exception applies (for example, partnership assets are clearly insufficient) (§ 16307). Forming SkyFrame LLC in 2025 does not discharge debts the partnership and its partners already owed in 2024, and the drone was transferred to the LLC, so DroneWorks can pursue the partners. Ruth is liable only if partnership by estoppel applies.

Between the partners, Mateo exceeded his actual authority, and Nadia may seek to hold him responsible for any loss that causes the partnership.

### 3. Liability to Lily

**A. Pete.** An agent is personally liable for his own torts, even when acting within the scope of his employment; the employer's vicarious liability is additional, not a substitute. Pete negligently flew a drone into a tree over a crowd. **Pete is liable to Lily.**

**B. Employee status.** An employer is vicariously liable for an employee's torts within the scope of employment, but generally not for an independent contractor's. The test is mainly the right to control the manner and means of the work (*Borello*). SkyFrame paid Pete by the hour, set his schedule, assigned his jobs and supplied the drone. **Pete is an employee.**

**C. Scope of employment.** An employee acts within the scope of employment when performing assigned work or engaging in conduct subject to the employer's control, but not when engaged in an independent course of conduct not intended to serve any purpose of the employer. A minor deviation (a **detour**) stays within the scope; a substantial departure for the employee's own purposes (a **frolic**) does not. California also asks whether the risk was one typical of or broadly incidental to the enterprise, so that the injury is a generally foreseeable consequence of the employer's business (*Perez v. Van Groningen & Sons*).
- *SkyFrame's argument.* Pete left his assignment to film a friend's party "for fun," a purely personal purpose that served SkyFrame in no way. That is a frolic.
- *Lily's argument.* The deviation was small in time and distance: half a mile, during a gap in his work, while he was on the clock and on standby, using the employer's drone. Flying the drone was the very activity SkyFrame employed him to do. That a pilot might fly a company drone somewhere unauthorized during a job is a foreseeable risk of a drone photography business.

Pete's purpose was personal, but the departure was brief, on duty, close to the job site, and consisted of the same activity he was hired to perform. Under California's foreseeability approach, the accident was a risk inherent in the enterprise. **The deviation is likely a detour, and SkyFrame is likely vicariously liable.**

**D. The partners.** A partnership tort obligation is a partnership obligation for which all partners are jointly and severally liable (§ 16306), subject to exhaustion (§ 16307). The accident occurred in August 2024, before the LLC existed, so the liability shield of SkyFrame LLC does not apply. **Nadia and Mateo are personally liable, in addition to Pete and the partnership.** Ruth, an employee, is not, unless partnership by estoppel applies, and Lily did not rely on the website, so estoppel cannot help her against Ruth.

### 4. Hangar Properties' claim against Nadia and Mateo

**A. The LLC shield.** An LLC formed by filing articles of organization is a separate entity, and its members are not personally liable for its debts solely because they are members (Corp. Code § 17703.04; RULLCA § 304). SkyFrame LLC was validly formed in January 2025 and signed the lease in its own name in March 2025. Nadia and Mateo refused to guarantee it. They are not liable unless the veil is pierced.

**B. Veil piercing (alter ego).** Courts disregard the entity when (1) there is such a unity of interest and ownership that the separate personalities of the entity and its owners no longer exist, and (2) respecting the separate entity would sanction a fraud or promote injustice (*Associated Vendors*). LLC members are subject to the same standard, except that **in California the failure to hold meetings is not a factor** where the articles or operating agreement do not expressly require meetings (§ 17703.04). Courts pierce reluctantly.

*Unity of interest.*
- *Commingling.* The members ran personal expenses (Mateo's rent, Nadia's car payments) through the business account. This is the strongest factor.
- *Undercapitalization.* The LLC began with $2,000 in cash while committing to a $144,000 lease. Its drones and equipment had some value, but its cash was plainly inadequate for that obligation.
- *Formalities.* They never held a meeting, but their operating agreement does not require meetings, so under California law this does not count.

*Inequitable result.* Hangar Properties is a voluntary contract creditor that investigated before dealing with the LLC. It ran a credit check, asked for personal guarantees, was refused, and signed anyway. Courts are reluctant to pierce for a creditor that knowingly accepted the risk of dealing with a thinly capitalized entity without guarantees, because piercing would give it the guarantee it bargained for and did not get. Hangar will respond that the commingling means LLC funds were diverted to the members' personal bills, which is the kind of siphoning that makes the shield unjust.

**Conclusion.** Commingling and undercapitalization show some unity of interest. But Hangar knowingly accepted the risk after the members refused guarantees, and nothing shows fraud aimed at Hangar. A court will **likely not pierce**, unless Hangar can show that the members drained the LLC's funds for personal use after taking on the lease.

### 5. Nadia's claims against Mateo

**Duty of loyalty.** A partner must account to the partnership and hold as trustee any property, profit or benefit derived by the partner in the conduct of the partnership business or from the use of partnership property, including the appropriation of a partnership opportunity, and must refrain from competing with the partnership (§ 16404(b); *Meinhard v. Salmon*). The members of a member-managed LLC owe each other the same duty (§ 17704.09).
- *Partnership opportunity.* The brokerage asked whether **SkyFrame** could photograph its listings. The opportunity came to Mateo as a SkyFrame partner and is squarely within SkyFrame's line of business. He diverted it to himself ("personally") without disclosing it to Nadia.
- *Use of partnership property.* He used SkyFrame's drones to do the work.
- *Competition.* By serving SkyFrame's prospective customer for himself, he competed with the partnership.
- *Mateo's defense.* He worked weekends on his own time, and nothing in the oral agreement made the partnership exclusive. But the duty of loyalty arises from the partnership relationship, not from the oral agreement, and Mateo's own time does not matter when he took a partnership opportunity and used partnership property.

**Remedies.** Nadia may bring an action for an accounting and other relief against Mateo (§ 16405). Mateo must account for and hold the **$30,000** profit as trustee for the partnership, so that it is distributed under the 60/40 profit split (Nadia's share: $18,000). If the work continued after the business moved into the LLC in 2025, Mateo owes the LLC the same duty. Nadia may also seek his dissociation or expulsion and damages for his unauthorized drone purchase.

**Common mistakes**
1. Treating the oral $10,000 limit as binding on DroneWorks. An undisclosed limit does not defeat a partner's authority in the ordinary course.
2. Treating Ruth as a partner because she shares profits, without applying the wage exception.
3. Calling any personal errand a frolic without weighing time, distance and the nature of the deviation.
4. Using the LLC shield for debts and torts that arose while SkyFrame was a partnership.
5. Counting the absence of meetings as an alter-ego factor in California when the operating agreement does not require meetings.
