# E-XO-030 — The River Mural (Constitutional Law + Remedies)

## Facts

Nadia Okafor owns Lantern Books, an independent bookstore in the downtown district of the City of Alder Bay, California. In January 2026 she paid an artist, Theo Marsh, $9,000 to paint a 300-square-foot mural on the store's side wall, which faces busy Harbor Street. The mural shows the Alder River winding through redwoods to the sea, with a cracked concrete dam in the foreground and the words "LET THE RIVER RUN — NO ALDER DAM." A small panel in one corner reads "Lantern Books · since 2004." The store window displays books about the river and the dam. The City Council supports the proposed dam and will take a final vote on it on June 10, 2026.

In February 2026, a driver on Harbor Street rear-ended another car near the store. The police report quotes her as saying that she "was looking at the wall art." The City has done no traffic study.

On March 3, 2026, the Council adopted Ordinance 26-4:
- § 1: Wall graphics in the downtown district "distract motorists and detract from the historic character of downtown."
- § 2: No mural or painted wall graphic larger than 40 square feet may be displayed on an exterior wall visible from a public street in the downtown district.
- § 3: Exempt are (a) signs identifying the business located on the premises, up to 60 square feet, and (b) murals that "depict the history, landscape or cultural heritage of Alder Bay," as determined by the Director of Code Enforcement.
- § 4: Each day of violation carries a $200 fine. Twenty days after a notice of violation, the City may paint over the graphic and charge the owner.

At the Council meeting, Councilmember Elena Ruiz said, "Our walls should celebrate Alder Bay, not become political billboards about the dam." The City Attorney told the Council that the ordinance was "consistent with current sign-law precedent."

On April 20, the Director of Code Enforcement, Paul Pratt, sent Nadia a notice of violation. It stated that her mural "does not qualify for the heritage exemption because its dominant theme is political advocacy about the dam, not a depiction of the City's landscape." He posted a bright orange placard on her wall reading "VIOLATION — PUBLIC NUISANCE." The same week, Pratt cited Copper Kettle Brewing for a 200-square-foot mural of its beer logo (the brewery painted it over) and approved a 500-square-foot mural of the town's 1906 lumber mill two blocks away.

On May 4, to avoid a lien, Nadia paid $2,400 in accrued fines under protest. Her sales, which had averaged $40,000 a month over the past year, fell to $33,000 in May. She keeps about 40 cents of profit on each dollar of sales after paying for the books. During May the City repaved part of Harbor Street, and two neighboring shops report sales declines of about 10%. A local newspaper ran a story about the dispute on May 5.

On Wednesday, May 6, Pratt notified Nadia that City crews would paint over the mural at 7 a.m. on Monday, May 11. At 2 p.m. on Friday, May 8, Nadia filed a verified complaint under 42 U.S.C. § 1983 in the United States District Court against the City and against Pratt in his official and individual capacities. She seeks a TRO and a preliminary injunction barring the City from painting over the mural, collecting further fines or keeping the placard on her wall; a permanent injunction; a declaratory judgment; a refund of the fines; damages for lost sales; nominal damages; and attorney's fees. Her lawyer emailed the papers to the City Attorney and left a voicemail. The City Attorney's office replied that no one could appear before Monday afternoon. The City has not started any court proceeding against Nadia.

Theo says he could paint a similar mural for about $9,000, "but it would not be the same painting."

In July, the Council introduced, but has not adopted, an amendment deleting the heritage exemption.

## Call of the Question

1. Is Ordinance 26-4 valid under the First Amendment to the United States Constitution, on its face and as applied to Nadia's mural? Discuss.
2. Should the court grant Nadia a temporary restraining order and a preliminary injunction? Discuss.
3. What other relief, including damages, can Nadia obtain against the City and against Pratt? Discuss.

## Model Answer

### Call 1 — Validity under the First Amendment

**A. Justiciability and forum**

A plaintiff must show an injury in fact, fairly traceable to the defendant and likely redressed by a favorable decision, for each form of relief. Nadia has paid $2,400 in fines and faces further fines, the scheduled destruction of her mural and lost sales, all caused by the City's enforcement. An injunction and a declaration redress the threatened harm; damages, including nominal damages, redress the completed harm. The claim is ripe because the ordinance has already been enforced against her. Younger abstention does not apply: the City has filed nothing, and a notice and scheduled paint-over are executive acts, not a pending judicial or administrative proceeding. The First Amendment binds the City through the Fourteenth.

**B. Protected speech: political, not commercial**

The City may argue that the mural is commercial speech, reviewed only under Central Hudson, because it names the store and promotes the dam books in the window. Commercial speech proposes a commercial transaction. The mural proposes none: its dominant message opposes a pending public project, which is core political speech. The small store-name panel is incidental. The mural is fully protected speech on Nadia's own property, so forum doctrine does not limit her.

**C. Is the ordinance content-based on its face?**

A regulation is content-based if, on its face, it targets speech because of its topic, idea or message, or if it cannot be justified without reference to content or was adopted because of disagreement with the message. A rule is not content-based merely because an official must read the speech to apply it.

- *Heritage exemption, § 3(b).* Whether a 300-square-foot mural is lawful depends on its subject: murals about history, landscape or heritage have no size limit, and all others are capped at 40 square feet. Like the topic-based sign code in *Reed v. Town of Gilbert*, this singles out subject matter, and a facially content-based law gets strict scrutiny whatever the City's benign motive (*Reed*).
- *The City's response.* Relying on *City of Austin*, the City will argue that it merely distinguishes by location. That likely works for § 3(a): like *Austin*'s on-/off-premises rule, it turns on the sign's tie to the site, so it is content-neutral. Nadia will reply that exempting only business identification is a function-based proxy for commercial subject matter, but an on-site business name is the paradigm location rule. The argument fails for § 3(b), which turns on what the mural is about, not where it is.
- *Commercial preference.* Section 3(a) allows 60-square-foot business signs but caps noncommercial murals at 40. A plurality in *Metromedia* found it invalid to allow on-site commercial messages while restricting noncommercial ones; the City will answer that § 3(a) is a location rule.

**Conclusion:** At least § 3(b) makes the ordinance content-based, so strict scrutiny applies.

**D. As applied: viewpoint discrimination**

Viewpoint discrimination is the most egregious form of content discrimination and is presumptively invalid. Pratt denied the exemption not because the mural lacks landscape (it depicts the river and redwoods) but because its "dominant theme is political advocacy about the dam." Ruiz's "political billboards" remark, the ordinance's adoption weeks after the mural appeared, and the Council's support for the dam point to the anti-dam message as the target. The City will respond that one councilmember does not speak for the Council, that Pratt also cited a beer-logo mural and approved the lumber-mill mural regardless of viewpoint, and that the City Attorney vetted the law. Even so, Pratt classified the mural by its political subject, which is content discrimination at minimum, and the evidence of viewpoint targeting is substantial. Section 3(b)'s standardless delegation to the Director invites exactly such calls (*Lakewood*).

**E. Strict scrutiny**

A content-based restriction is valid only if the City proves it is narrowly tailored, by the least restrictive means, to a compelling interest. Courts rarely treat traffic safety and aesthetics as compelling. Even assuming they are, as *Reed* did, the ordinance is fatally underinclusive: the approved 500-square-foot lumber-mill mural distracts drivers and alters downtown at least as much as Nadia's smaller one. The record is one collision and no study, and uniform size, placement or setback rules would be less restrictive. **The ordinance fails strict scrutiny.**

**F. Alternative: a uniform size cap**

If the exemption were deleted, a uniform 40-square-foot cap would be content-neutral and valid if narrowly tailored to a significant interest, leaving ample alternative channels. Aesthetics (*Taxpayers for Vincent*) and traffic safety are significant interests. Tailoring need not be the least restrictive means, but the cap may not burden substantially more speech than necessary, and the City must show it seriously considered less burdensome tools (*McCullen*); one collision and no study is weak support. Nadia would keep ample alternatives (a smaller mural, her window, leaflets), and unlike the near-total ban on residential signs in *Ladue*, a size cap does not close off a medium. **A uniform, evidence-backed cap would likely survive.**

**Overall:** The ordinance is content-based as written and likely viewpoint-based as applied. **It is invalid on its face and as applied to Nadia.**

### Call 2 — TRO and preliminary injunction

The case is in federal court, so FRCP 65 and the *Winter* factors govern.

**A. Temporary restraining order**

A TRO preserves the status quo until a preliminary-injunction hearing. It may issue without notice only if specific facts in an affidavit or verified complaint clearly show that immediate and irreparable injury will result before the adverse party can be heard, and the movant's attorney certifies in writing the efforts made to give notice and why notice should not be required. The *Winter* factors also apply, and the order lasts no more than 14 days unless extended for good cause.

- *Notice.* The crews arrive at 7 a.m. Monday; the City cannot appear until Monday afternoon. The verified complaint shows the imminent harm, and counsel can certify the email and voicemail. The court may rule on the papers or by telephone.
- *Likelihood of success.* Strong (Call 1).
- *Irreparable harm.* Painting over the mural would destroy an original artwork and silence Nadia weeks before the June 10 vote; the loss of First Amendment freedoms, even briefly, is irreparable injury (*Elrod*, plurality). The City will argue that Theo can repaint for $9,000. But a new mural "would not be the same painting," and the chance to speak before the vote cannot be recreated.
- *Balance and public interest.* The City loses a few days of enforcement against a mural up for months, with one collision as its safety evidence; Nadia would lose the mural for good. The public interest favors constitutional rights, and the City has no legitimate interest in enforcing an unconstitutional ordinance.

**Conclusion:** The court should issue a TRO barring abatement and further fines for up to 14 days, set a preliminary-injunction hearing and require security.

**B. Preliminary injunction**

A preliminary injunction issues only on notice to the adverse party. The plaintiff must show that she is likely to succeed on the merits, is likely to suffer irreparable harm without relief, that the balance of equities tips in her favor, and that an injunction is in the public interest; a mere possibility of irreparable harm is not enough.

- *Success.* Likely; the City's safety arguments cannot meet strict scrutiny.
- *Irreparable harm.* Destruction of the mural and the chilling of speech before the vote are likely, not merely possible. The fines are money and can be refunded, so alone they are not irreparable; but continuing daily fines coerce Nadia to remove her speech, so stopping their accrual protects it.
- *Equities and public interest.* As for the TRO; the City can address traffic safety by other means pending trial.
- *California contrast.* In superior court, the judge would weigh two interrelated factors, the likelihood of prevailing and the relative interim harm, on a sliding scale (*IT Corp. v. County of Imperial*), with a mandatory undertaking (CCP § 529) and an order-to-show-cause hearing within 15 days of a TRO (CCP § 527). Strong merits and lopsided interim harm give the same result.

**Conclusion:** The preliminary injunction should issue.

**C. Scope of interim relief, status quo and bond**

Interim relief preserves the status quo ante litem, the last peaceable, uncontested state of affairs; mandatory interim orders are disfavored. Before April 20, the mural stood with no fines and no placard. Barring abatement and fines is prohibitory and preserves that state. Removing the placard is mandatory in form but restores the last peaceable status, so it should be ordered. The $2,400 refund is money and awaits final judgment. Under FRCP 65(c) Nadia must give security in an amount the court considers proper; because the City's losses from delay are minimal, a modest bond suffices.

### Call 3 — Other relief

**A. Permanent injunction**

In federal court the plaintiff must show irreparable injury, inadequate legal remedies, a balance of hardships favoring equity, and that the public interest would not be disserved (*eBay*); the general elements add a protectable interest, feasibility and no defenses. Having won on the merits, Nadia faces a continuing restriction on speech; repeated fines would mean a multiplicity of suits, and lost speech is not measurable. Her First Amendment right is a protectable interest. A prohibitory order not to enforce §§ 2 and 4 against the mural, plus removal of the placard, is easy to enforce. The balance favors her, and there is no laches (she sued within weeks) or unclean hands. Relief should be no broader than complete relief requires, and it binds the City's officers, agents and employees with actual notice, including Pratt and the crews (FRCP 65(d)). **A permanent injunction should issue.**

**B. Declaratory judgment**

In a case of actual controversy, a federal court may declare the parties' rights (28 U.S.C. § 2201). The City has cited Nadia, fined her and scheduled abatement, so the dispute is concrete. A declaration that the ordinance violates the First Amendment would settle the legal question and guide the Council's pending amendment, and it does not require the irreparable-harm showing an injunction needs (compare *Steffel*). **A declaration is likely**, though discretionary.

**C. Effect of the proposed amendment**

A case is moot when events eliminate the live controversy, but voluntary cessation does not moot it unless the defendant makes absolutely clear that the conduct could not reasonably be expected to recur. The amendment is only introduced, and a council can re-enact a provision. More importantly, the claims for fines, lost profits and nominal damages redress completed injuries and keep the case alive (*Uzuegbunam*). If a content-neutral cap is adopted, prospective relief against the old version may lapse, and the new cap would face intermediate scrutiny (Call 1F).

**D. Proper defendants under § 1983**

Section 1983 gives a cause of action for damages and injunctive relief against any person who, under color of state law, deprives another of federal rights; local governments are liable only for their own policies or customs (*Monell*).

- *The City.* The ordinance is official City policy, and Pratt enforced it, so the City is liable. Cities have no Eleventh Amendment immunity and cannot assert their officers' qualified immunity (*Owen*). A claim against Pratt in his official capacity is in substance a claim against the City.
- *Pratt individually.* Officials have qualified immunity unless they violated a clearly established right of which a reasonable person would have known. Nadia will argue that *Reed* (2015) clearly established that subject-matter sign exemptions are content-based, that viewpoint discrimination has long been forbidden, and that Pratt himself rejected the mural for "political advocacy." Pratt will respond that he enforced a duly enacted ordinance the City Attorney called lawful, that no case had addressed a heritage-mural exemption, and that *City of Austin* limited *Reed*'s reach. **Pratt likely has qualified immunity from damages**, though his "political advocacy" judgment makes it close. Immunity does not bar injunctive or declaratory relief, and the City remains liable.

**E. Damages**

Compensatory damages put the plaintiff where she would have been absent the wrong. She must prove causation for each item and the fact of loss with reasonable certainty; the amount may be a reasonable estimate.

- *Fines.* The $2,400 paid under protest is a certain loss caused directly by enforcement and is recoverable (or as restitution of the City's gain).
- *Lost profits.* Sales fell $7,000, but the measure is lost profit: about $2,800 at 40 cents per dollar. The repaving probably explains a 10% decline ($4,000 of sales), as at the neighboring shops, so only about $3,000 of sales ($1,200 of profit) is plausibly traceable to the placard and enforcement; the City will add that the May 5 news story may have deterred or attracted customers, clouding causation. With a year of sales history, a reasonable estimate is possible. **About $1,200 is recoverable if she proves causation.**
- *Nominal damages.* Even without proven loss, she recovers nominal damages (such as $1) for the violation.
- *Punitive damages.* Cities are immune from punitive damages under § 1983 (*City of Newport*); against Pratt they require malicious or reckless conduct, and he enforced an ordinance counsel had vetted and is likely immune. **None.**
- *Fees.* As a prevailing § 1983 plaintiff she may recover reasonable attorney's fees (42 U.S.C. § 1988), likely the largest money item.

Damages for past harm and an injunction against future enforcement cover different harms, so combining them is no double recovery.

**Best relief:** interim and then permanent injunctions saving the mural, a declaration, and, from the City, the $2,400, provable lost profits (or nominal damages) and § 1988 fees.

**Common mistakes**
1. Calling the mural commercial speech because the store's name appears on it. The dominant message is political, and the name is incidental.
2. Reading *City of Austin* to make the whole ordinance content-neutral. It covers the on-premises business-sign distinction, not an exemption that turns on a mural's topic.
3. Treating the fines as the irreparable harm, or asking for a refund in the preliminary injunction. Money is refundable; the irreparable harms are the destruction of the mural and the loss of speech before the vote.
4. Applying *IT Corp.* as the test in a federal case, or *Winter* alone in superior court.
5. Giving the City qualified immunity, or treating Pratt's immunity as a bar to injunctive relief.
6. Claiming the $7,000 drop in gross sales as damages without netting costs or separating the repaving effect.

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: In PRC administrative litigation a court reviews the legality of a specific administrative act and may award state compensation (国家赔偿), but it cannot strike down a local regulation as unconstitutional; a § 1983 suit lets a US federal court do both (approx.).

**Accuracy notes.**
- Rules follow R-CON-092 to -094, -101, -106, -016, -043, -117 and R-REM-055 to -069, -091, -092, -094. *Elrod* (irreparable harm) and *Metromedia* are plurality opinions and are labelled as such. *City of Austin* (2022) is an October Term 2021 decision cited in the rulebook without a verification tag.
- The scope-of-relief point rests only on the complete-relief principle and FRCP 65(d) (R-REM-080), not on *Trump v. CASA* (V-035).
- *Owen v. City of Independence*, 445 U.S. 622 (1980) (no qualified immunity for municipalities) is in R-CON-117's authority line, but the rule text does not state its holding. The point that an official-capacity claim is a claim against the entity is black-letter law the rulebook does not state. Graders should accept both.
- § 1983 damages are measured by ordinary compensatory principles (R-REM-007, -008, -010). Do not award a sum for the abstract "value" of the right; without proven loss, nominal damages are the remedy (R-REM-094).
- The model answer states that a municipality is immune from punitive damages under § 1983 (*City of Newport v. Fact Concerts, Inc.*, 453 U.S. 247 (1981)). This is black-letter law, but the repo rulebook does not yet state it; it is flagged for R-CON-117 in the Phase 7 audit.
- The *Lakewood* point applies R-CON-106's no-unbridled-discretion rule by analogy: § 3(b) works like a permit decision committed to one official.
- Severability (whether a court would strike only § 3(b) and leave a uniform cap in force) is not required. Credit an answer that raises it consistently with Call 1F.
- The facts are original. The only past Con Law + Remedies essay (Jul 2019 Q2) involved a free-exercise challenge to a candle ban; this question tests free speech, damages and § 1983 defendants instead.
