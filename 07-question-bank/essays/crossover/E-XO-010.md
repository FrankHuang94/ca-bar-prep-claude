# E-XO-010 — Moreno v. FitCore (Civil Procedure + Evidence)

## Facts

Pia Moreno, 22, grew up in Phoenix, Arizona. Since August 2022 she has been a student at San Diego State University. In 2024 she signed a two-year apartment lease in San Diego, obtained a California driver's license, registered to vote in California and voted there in November 2024. She works part-time at a San Diego bank and, in December 2025, accepted the bank's offer of a full-time position starting after she graduates in May 2026. Her car is still registered in Arizona, she keeps her childhood bedroom at her parents' home in Phoenix and spends most summers there, and her parents hope she will move back.

FitCore, Inc. operates 300 gyms in 12 states. It is incorporated in Delaware. Its chief executive officer and executive team work at its headquarters in Phoenix, where all major decisions are made. California has 120 of its gyms and about 60% of its employees.

On January 10, 2026, while Pia was using a lat-pulldown machine at FitCore's San Diego gym, the cable snapped; the weight stack crashed down and the bar struck her face, fracturing her jaw. On February 2, Pia sued FitCore in San Diego County Superior Court for negligence, seeking $400,000. On February 3, Pia's lawyer emailed a copy of the complaint to FitCore's general counsel. FitCore was formally served on March 2. On March 27, FitCore filed a notice of removal to the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of California. Pia moved to remand, arguing that she and FitCore are both California citizens and that removal was untimely. The court denied the motion.

**The incident report.** Within an hour of the accident, gym manager Dale Ortiz filled out FitCore's standard "Incident Report" form, which company policy requires for every member injury. Completed forms go to FitCore's risk-management department and its in-house legal department; FitCore also uses them in quarterly safety training for staff. The form's printed header says: "Confidential — prepared at the request of counsel in anticipation of litigation." Dale wrote: "Cable on machine #7 was frayed. Trainer Evan reported it Jan. 3; work order pending." Pia requested the report in discovery. FitCore objected on grounds of attorney-client privilege and work product. At his deposition, Dale testified that he "doesn't remember" what the cable looked like. Pia moved to compel.

**The video.** On January 12, Pia's lawyer wrote to FitCore asking it to preserve all surveillance video of the accident. The gym's camera system automatically records over footage after 30 days. FitCore's regional manager watched the footage on January 11 and emailed the legal department, "Nothing useful there — the machine just failed." No one saved it, and it was overwritten on February 9. Pia moved for sanctions, asking the court to instruct the jury that it must presume the video was unfavorable to FitCore.

**Trial.** At the jury trial:
- (a) Pia testified that seconds after the accident, FitCore trainer Evan ran over, looked at the dangling cable, and exclaimed, "Oh no — I told them that cable was fraying a week ago!" Evan has since quit FitCore and moved abroad, and he did not testify. FitCore objected; the court overruled the objection.
- (b) Pia called Mo, the gym's security guard, who testified that on January 11 he watched the surveillance video of machine #7 and that it showed the cable "visibly frayed and unwinding" for several minutes before it snapped while Pia was using the machine normally. FitCore objected; the court overruled the objection.

## Call of the Question

1. Did the court properly deny Pia's motion to remand? Discuss.
2. How should the court rule on (a) Pia's motion to compel production of the incident report and (b) Pia's motion for sanctions? Discuss.
3. Did the court properly overrule FitCore's objections to the testimony described in (a) and (b)? Answer according to the Federal Rules of Evidence. Discuss.

## Model Answer

### Call 1 — The motion to remand

**Rule.** Federal courts have limited subject-matter jurisdiction, which cannot be waived. A defendant may remove a civil action over which the federal courts would have original jurisdiction (28 U.S.C. § 1441(a)). Diversity jurisdiction requires complete diversity of citizenship between all plaintiffs and all defendants and an amount in controversy exceeding $75,000, exclusive of interest and costs (§ 1332(a)), measured when the action is filed and, for removal, also at removal.

**Amount in controversy.** Pia seeks $400,000 for a fractured jaw. Her good-faith claim controls unless it is legally certain she cannot recover more than $75,000, which it is not. This requirement is met.

**Pia's citizenship.** An individual is a citizen of the state of her domicile: where she is physically present with the intent to remain indefinitely. A domicile continues until a new one is acquired, and students often keep their pre-college domicile because they intend to leave after graduation.
- *Arizona:* Pia grew up in Phoenix, keeps her childhood room there, spends summers there, keeps her car registered there, and her parents want her back.
- *California:* She has lived in San Diego since 2022 under a two-year lease, holds a California driver's license, registered and voted in California, works in San Diego, and, most importantly, accepted a full-time job in San Diego to begin after graduation. That job offer shows she intends to stay after school ends, which removes the usual reason to treat a student's presence as temporary. Her parents' hopes do not establish her intent, and a car registration and summer visits are weaker indicators than voting and a long-term job.
- *Conclusion:* At filing (February 2026) and removal (March 2026), Pia was domiciled in, and a citizen of, California.

**FitCore's citizenship.** A corporation is a citizen of every state where it is incorporated and of the state where it has its principal place of business, which is its "nerve center," the place where its high-level officers direct, control and coordinate its activities (*Hertz Corp. v. Friend*). FitCore is incorporated in Delaware. Its CEO and executive team direct the company from Phoenix. Pia will argue that FitCore is really a California company because California has 120 of its gyms and 60% of its employees, but *Hertz* rejected a test based on where the business activity is concentrated. FitCore is a citizen of Delaware and Arizona only.

**Complete diversity.** Pia (California) is diverse from FitCore (Delaware and Arizona). Diversity jurisdiction exists.

**Removal procedure.**
- *Forum-defendant rule.* A diversity case cannot be removed if a properly joined and served defendant is a citizen of the forum state (§ 1441(b)(2)). FitCore is not a California citizen, so the rule does not apply.
- *Timing.* A defendant must remove within 30 days after formal service of the summons and complaint (§ 1446(b)). Pia will argue that the clock started on February 3 when her lawyer emailed the complaint, making the March 27 removal late. But informal receipt of the complaint does not start the clock; only formal service does (*Murphy Bros. v. Michetti Pipe Stringing*). FitCore was served on March 2 and removed 25 days later, which was timely. The one-year limit for diversity removals does not apply: it governs only cases that become removable later (§ 1446(b)(3), (c)(1)), and this case was removable from the start.
- *Venue.* The case was removed to the Southern District of California, the district embracing San Diego County, where the state action was pending (§ 1441(a)).
- *Unanimity* is not an issue because FitCore is the only defendant.

**Conclusion:** The court properly denied the motion to remand.

### Call 2 — Discovery motions

#### (a) Motion to compel the incident report

**Scope of discovery.** Parties may obtain discovery of any nonprivileged matter relevant to a claim or defense and proportional to the needs of the case (FRCP 26(b)(1)). Dale's report that the cable was frayed and had been reported a week earlier goes directly to FitCore's notice and negligence, so it is relevant and proportional. The question is whether it is protected.

**Which privilege law applies (Erie and FRE 501).** In federal court, privileges are generally governed by federal common law, but in a civil case, state law governs privilege regarding a claim or defense for which state law supplies the rule of decision (FRE 501). Pia's negligence claim arises under California law, so California's attorney-client privilege rules apply, not the federal *Upjohn* test.

**Attorney-client privilege under California law.** The privilege protects confidential communications between client and lawyer made for the purpose of legal advice (Evid. Code §§ 952, 954). When a corporate employee makes a statement at the employer's direction, the statement is privileged if the dominant purpose of the employer in requiring it was to transmit information to counsel for legal advice (*D.I. Chadbourne, Inc. v. Superior Court*).
- *FitCore's argument:* The form is sent to the legal department and says it is "prepared at the request of counsel in anticipation of litigation."
- *Pia's argument:* Company policy requires the form for every member injury, regardless of whether a claim is likely; it goes to risk management as well as legal; and FitCore uses it in safety training. Those are ordinary business purposes. A printed label cannot convert a routine business record into a privileged communication.
- *Resolution:* The dominant purpose appears to be routine risk management and safety, not obtaining legal advice. FitCore likely cannot carry its burden of showing the privilege. In any event, the underlying facts (that the cable was frayed and had been reported) are not privileged and must be disclosed in some form.

**Work product (FRCP 26(b)(3)).** Work-product protection is not a privilege; under the majority view, in federal court it is governed by FRCP 26(b)(3) even in a diversity case. Documents prepared in anticipation of litigation by or for a party or its representative are discoverable only if the requesting party shows substantial need and cannot obtain their substantial equivalent without undue hardship; mental impressions, conclusions and legal theories of an attorney or other representative remain protected.
- *Anticipation of litigation?* Courts ask whether the document was prepared because of the prospect of litigation, rather than in the ordinary course of business in a form it would have taken anyway. A serious facial injury from equipment failure makes litigation foreseeable, which supports FitCore. But the report is required for *every* member injury, serious or not, and is used for safety training, so FitCore would have prepared it in the same form regardless of litigation. It is likely an ordinary-course business record, not work product.
- *Substantial need.* Even if it were work product, Pia can show substantial need for Dale's contemporaneous factual account: Dale now "doesn't remember" what the cable looked like, the video has been destroyed, and there is no other way to obtain an equivalent same-day description. Dale's report contains facts, not an attorney's mental impressions; any legal analysis added by counsel could be redacted.
- **Conclusion:** The court should grant the motion to compel, redacting any attorney opinions if present.

#### (b) Motion for sanctions — the overwritten video

**Rule (FRCP 37(e)).** If electronically stored information that should have been preserved in anticipation or conduct of litigation is lost because a party failed to take reasonable steps to preserve it, and it cannot be restored or replaced through additional discovery, the court (1) on finding prejudice to another party may order measures no greater than necessary to cure the prejudice; and (2) only on finding that the party acted with the intent to deprive another party of the information's use in the litigation may it presume the information was unfavorable, instruct the jury that it may or must so presume, or dismiss or enter default.

**Threshold.**
- *ESI and duty to preserve.* Surveillance video is ESI. FitCore's duty arose at the latest on January 12, when Pia's lawyer asked it to preserve the video; a jaw-breaking injury on its equipment also made litigation reasonably foreseeable.
- *Failure to take reasonable steps.* FitCore knew its system overwrote footage after 30 days and had four weeks to save it; its regional manager watched the footage and still did nothing. That is a failure to take reasonable steps.
- *Cannot be restored or replaced.* The video was overwritten; Mo's recollection is only a partial substitute.

**Prejudice — Rule 37(e)(1).** The video was the best evidence of the cable's condition before the accident and of how Pia used the machine. Its loss prejudices Pia, who now must rely on memory. The court may order curative measures no greater than necessary: for example, allowing Pia to present evidence and argument to the jury about the loss of the video, and precluding FitCore from arguing that Pia misused the machine or that the cable looked normal.

**Intent to deprive — Rule 37(e)(2).** The mandatory adverse-inference instruction Pia requests requires a finding that FitCore *intended* to deprive her of the video. Pia will argue that the manager watched the footage, which (according to Mo) showed a visibly fraying cable, and let it be destroyed despite a written preservation demand, from which intent can be inferred. FitCore will respond that the manager honestly thought the footage was "nothing useful," that the overwrite was automatic, and that his email ("the machine just failed") is not consistent with a plan to hide damaging evidence. Negligence or even gross negligence is not enough. On these facts, the court likely will not find intent to deprive.

**Conclusion:** The court should deny the mandatory adverse-inference instruction but grant curative measures under Rule 37(e)(1).

### Call 3 — Evidentiary rulings (FRE)

The Federal Rules of Evidence govern trials in federal court, including diversity cases (except where FRE 501, 601 and 302 defer to state law on privilege, competency and presumptions).

#### (a) Evan's statement: "I told them that cable was fraying a week ago!"

**Relevance.** It shows the cable was frayed before the accident and that FitCore had notice, both central to negligence (FRE 401).

**Hearsay.** It is an out-of-court statement offered to prove its truth (that the cable was fraying and had been reported), so it is hearsay unless excluded or excepted (FRE 801(c), 802).

**Opposing-party statement by an employee (FRE 801(d)(2)(D)).** A statement offered against a party is not hearsay if made by the party's employee on a matter within the scope of the employment while the relationship existed. Evan was a FitCore trainer; monitoring equipment and reporting problems is within a trainer's duties; he spoke while still employed. That he later quit and moved abroad is irrelevant: what matters is his status when he spoke, and unavailability is not required. Personal knowledge is not required for opposing-party statements, though Evan had it. The employment relationship must be shown by evidence beyond the statement itself, which Pia can do (FitCore's own records; Dale's report naming "Trainer Evan").

**Alternative: excited utterance (FRE 803(2)).** A cable snapping and a weight bar striking a member's face is a startling event. Evan spoke "seconds after," running over and exclaiming "Oh no," while under the stress of excitement, and the statement relates to the event. FitCore may argue that the part about telling management "a week ago" describes a past event rather than the startling event itself, but the excited-utterance exception covers statements that *relate to* the startling event, and the cable's prior condition does.

**FRE 403.** The statement is highly probative and not unfairly prejudicial.

**Conclusion:** The court properly overruled the objection.

#### (b) Mo's testimony about the overwritten video

**Best evidence rule (FRE 1002).** To prove the content of a writing, recording or photograph, the original is required unless the rules provide otherwise. The rule applies when the witness's knowledge comes only from the recording. Mo did not see the accident; he knows what happened only from watching the video, so his testimony proves its content and the rule applies.

**Excuse — FRE 1004(a).** An original is not required, and other evidence of its content is admissible, if all originals are lost or destroyed, unless the *proponent* lost or destroyed them in bad faith. Pia is the proponent; FitCore's system destroyed the video. FitCore cannot invoke the best evidence rule to exclude the only remaining evidence of a recording it failed to preserve.

**Personal knowledge and lay opinion.** Mo testifies to what he personally saw on the screen (FRE 602). His description of the cable as "visibly frayed and unwinding" and of Pia's use as "normal" is lay opinion rationally based on his perception and helpful to the jury, not based on specialized knowledge (FRE 701).

**Authentication.** Mo can identify the footage as the gym's surveillance recording of machine #7 on January 10, which is sufficient to support a finding that it is what Pia claims (FRE 901(b)(1)).

**Hearsay.** A video recording of events is not a "statement" by a person, and Mo testifies in court, so there is no hearsay problem.

**FRE 403.** FitCore will argue that Mo's memory of a video viewed once is unreliable. That goes to weight and can be tested on cross-examination; it does not substantially outweigh the testimony's probative value.

**Conclusion:** The court properly overruled the objection.

**Common mistakes**
1. Using *Upjohn* in a diversity case. FRE 501 makes state privilege law govern state-law claims.
2. Treating "prepared at the request of counsel" on a form as decisive. Routine reports prepared for business purposes are not privileged or work product merely because of a label.
3. Granting a mandatory adverse-inference instruction for negligent loss of ESI. Rule 37(e)(2) requires intent to deprive.
4. Excluding Evan's statement because Evan no longer works for FitCore. Scope and employment are measured when the statement was made.
5. Forgetting that 1004(a) bars secondary evidence only when the *proponent* destroyed the original in bad faith.

**Accuracy notes.** The answer does not rely on the 2025 privilege-log amendments to FRCP 26 or on any OT2025 Supreme Court decision on diversity (repo item V-034). The rule that federal law governs work product in diversity cases is the majority approach; it is stated as such.
