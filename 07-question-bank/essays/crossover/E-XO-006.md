# E-XO-006 — People v. Coleman (Evidence + Professional Responsibility)

## Facts

On the night of September 5, 2026, a cyclist, Paula Grant, was struck and seriously injured on Elm Street in Sacramento by a car that drove away. A week later Nate Coleman was charged in Sacramento County Superior Court with felony hit-and-run causing injury. Nate retained Alicia Romero, a California criminal-defense lawyer.

On September 7, before he was charged, Nate went to Alicia's office. His roommate, Ty, drove him. On the way, Nate told Ty, "I really messed up last night." At the office, Nate asked that Ty sit in on the meeting "because he was home that night and can help me with the timeline." Alicia agreed. During the meeting Nate said: "I hit something on Elm Street — I think it was a person on a bike. I panicked and drove home." Ty added that when Nate got home that night, "the right headlight was smashed." Alicia took notes.

Nate then handed Alicia the car's broken right side mirror, which he had picked up from his own driveway the morning after the accident, saying, "This fell off when I got home." Alicia locked it in her office safe. Ten days later she had a forensic expert examine it without altering it; the expert found blue paint consistent with Paula's bicycle. The mirror has remained in Alicia's safe since then, about two months.

At the end of the meeting Nate said, "My cousin runs a body shop. I'll get the car fixed tomorrow before anyone sees it. That's okay, right?" Alicia answered, "I can't tell you to do that — but there's no law against fixing your own car." The next day Nate's cousin replaced the headlight and repainted the front bumper.

At Nate's bail hearing on September 20, the judge asked whether there was any reason to believe Nate would tamper with evidence. Alicia said, "Your Honor, my client has nothing to hide. His car has been sitting untouched in his garage since that night." Alicia knew about the repairs. The judge set low bail.

The prosecution has now (a) subpoenaed Alicia to testify about what Nate said at the September 7 meeting, including the body-shop exchange; (b) listed Ty as a witness to testify about what Nate said in the car and at the meeting and about the smashed headlight; and (c) after Ty told police that Nate gave Alicia "a piece of the car," moved for an order requiring Alicia to produce it and to testify about where she got it.

## Call of the Question

1. Under the California Evidence Code: (a) Can Alicia be compelled to testify about Nate's statements at the September 7 meeting? (b) To what may Ty testify? (c) Must Alicia produce the mirror, and can she be compelled to testify about where she got it? Discuss.
2. What ethical violations, if any, has Alicia committed? Answer according to California and ABA authorities.

## Model Answer

### Call 1 — Evidence (California Evidence Code)

Because this is a criminal case, Proposition 8 (Cal. Const. art. I, § 28(f)(2)) admits all relevant evidence except as limited by, among other things, the statutory privileges and the hearsay rule, which it expressly preserves. In California, privileges exist only by statute (Evid. Code § 911).

#### (a) Can Alicia be compelled to testify about Nate's statements?

**Rule — lawyer-client privilege.** The client holds a privilege to refuse to disclose, and to prevent another from disclosing, a confidential communication between client and lawyer made in the course of the lawyer-client relationship (§ 954). A person who consults a lawyer to retain her is a client (§ 951). A communication is confidential if transmitted in confidence by means that disclose it to no third persons other than those present to further the interest of the client in the consultation or those to whom disclosure is reasonably necessary to accomplish the purpose of the consultation (§ 952). A communication is presumed confidential, and the opponent of the privilege must prove otherwise (§ 917). The lawyer must claim the privilege whenever the communication is sought and the holder has not waived it (§ 955).

**Application — Nate's account of the accident.** Nate consulted Alicia for legal advice about potential criminal charges, so he was a client and his statements ("I hit something … I panicked and drove home") were communications made to obtain legal services.

**Ty's presence.** The prosecution will argue that Nate destroyed confidentiality by bringing his roommate, a percipient witness who is not his agent or relative and whose presence was not needed for Alicia to give advice. Nate will respond that he brought Ty specifically to help supply the facts of the night ("help me with the timeline"), which furthered his interest in the consultation, and that § 917 presumes confidentiality. The statute protects communications made in the presence of persons who further the client's interest in the consultation, and a person brought to help give the lawyer accurate facts arguably fits. The question is close; the better view is that the privilege survives, but a court could find that a friend-witness's presence was not reasonably necessary. If confidentiality was lost, Alicia could be compelled to testify and Nate's statements would be admissible against him as party admissions (§ 1220).

**Crime-fraud exception (§ 956).** There is no privilege if the lawyer's services were sought or obtained to enable or aid anyone to commit or plan a crime or fraud. Nate's admission about the accident concerns a *past* crime and is the core of what the privilege protects, so § 956 does not reach it. The body-shop exchange is different. Nate announced a plan to have the car repaired "before anyone sees it" and asked Alicia whether that was "okay"; he then did it. Destroying or concealing evidence of a crime that one knows will be sought in an investigation is itself a crime (Pen. Code § 135). Nate will argue that he was merely asking for legal advice about whether repairing his car was lawful, which is a legitimate purpose of consultation. The prosecution will respond that he was seeking the lawyer's approval to carry out a plan to conceal evidence and obtained it ("there's no law against fixing your own car"). Because the plan's stated purpose was concealment and Nate carried it out the next day, a court would likely find the body-shop exchange within § 956.

**Conclusion (a):** Alicia cannot be compelled to testify to Nate's account of the accident (if, as is likely, Ty's presence did not destroy confidentiality); she must claim the privilege (§ 955). She likely can be compelled to testify to the body-shop exchange under the crime-fraud exception.

#### (b) Ty's testimony

- **The smashed headlight.** Ty may testify to what he personally saw when Nate came home (§ 702). It is relevant to whether Nate's car struck Paula, and it is not hearsay. It is not privileged: a fact a witness observed does not become privileged because it was later repeated in a lawyer's office.
- **"I really messed up last night."** Nate said this to Ty in the car, with no lawyer present, so no privilege applies. Offered for its truth, it is hearsay, but a party's own statement offered against him by the opposing party is admissible under § 1220. Nate may argue under § 352 that the statement is vague (it could refer to anything), but its timing, the morning after the accident and on the way to a lawyer, gives it real probative value. Admissible.
- **Nate's statements at the meeting.** If the privilege applies, Nate can prevent "another," including Ty, from disclosing the confidential communication (§ 954), so Ty cannot repeat it. If Ty's presence destroyed confidentiality, Ty may testify to Nate's statements as party admissions (§ 1220). As to the body-shop exchange, the crime-fraud exception (if found) would also allow Ty to testify to it.

**Conclusion (b):** Ty may testify to the headlight and to "I really messed up"; whether he may repeat Nate's statements at the meeting turns on the confidentiality and crime-fraud analysis above.

#### (c) The mirror

**Physical evidence is not privileged.** The privilege protects communications, not objects. A client cannot shield physical evidence of a crime by handing it to his lawyer. A defense lawyer who takes possession of such evidence may keep it only long enough to examine it and then must turn it over to the authorities (in California practice, often to the court); she may not conceal it (Pen. Code § 135; *People v. Meredith*). Alicia must produce the mirror.

**The source and Nate's statements about it.** Nate's act of handing the mirror to his lawyer and his statement "This fell off when I got home" were communications made in confidence for legal advice, and they remain privileged. *Meredith* holds that when the *defense* removes or alters evidence, the privilege does not bar the prosecution from proving the evidence's original location and condition, because the defense's act deprived the prosecution of the chance to find it there. Here Alicia did not remove the mirror from anywhere; Nate had already picked it up from his driveway before he consulted her. So *Meredith*'s location exception does not reach Alicia's knowledge, and she should not be compelled to testify that Nate gave it to her. The prosecution can connect the mirror to the car by other means (the car's missing mirror, the paint evidence, Ty's testimony), and the court can receive the mirror from counsel without telling the jury its source. Because Alicia's expert examined the mirror without altering it, there is no issue of changed condition.

**Conclusion (c):** Alicia must produce the mirror, but likely cannot be compelled to testify that she received it from Nate or to repeat what he said about it.

### Call 2 — Alicia's ethical violations

California lawyers are governed by the California Rules of Professional Conduct (CRPC) and the State Bar Act (Bus. & Prof. Code); the ABA Model Rules (MR) are persuasive.

#### A. Confidentiality and privilege

**ABA:** MR 1.6(a) forbids revealing information relating to the representation without consent or an exception. **California:** B&P § 6068(e)(1) requires a lawyer to maintain inviolate the client's confidences "at every peril"; rule 1.6 permits disclosure only to prevent a criminal act likely to cause death or substantial bodily harm. The duty of confidentiality is broader than the privilege: it covers all information relating to the representation, whatever its source, and bars voluntary disclosure.

**Application.** Faced with the subpoena, Alicia must assert the privilege (§ 955) and may not volunteer anything. If the court rules that the crime-fraud exception applies and orders her to testify, testifying to what the order requires does not violate either rule; disclosure compelled by a court after the privilege is properly asserted is permitted (MR 1.6(b)(6); in California the court's order controls). So far, Alicia has not breached confidentiality, but she must not testify beyond the scope of any order.

#### B. Retaining the mirror

**ABA:** MR 3.4(a) forbids unlawfully obstructing another party's access to evidence or concealing a document or other material having potential evidentiary value, or counseling or assisting another to do so; the comment notes that a lawyer may take temporary possession of physical evidence for examination but may be required by law to turn it over. **California:** Rule 3.4(a) is the same in substance, and Penal Code § 135 makes it a crime to conceal evidence knowing it is about to be produced in an investigation.

**Application.** Alicia was not required to accept the mirror, but once she did, she could keep it only for a reasonable time to examine it. Her expert finished in ten days; she has now kept it locked in her safe for about two months, while Nate was charged and the prosecution investigated, without turning it over. Holding it that long, after examination was complete, is concealment of evidence.

**Conclusion:** Alicia violated MR 3.4(a) and CRPC rule 3.4(a). She should promptly turn the mirror over, without revealing Nate's communications.

#### C. The body-shop advice

**ABA:** MR 1.2(d) forbids counseling or assisting a client in conduct the lawyer knows is criminal or fraudulent, though the lawyer may discuss the legal consequences of a proposed course of conduct; MR 3.4(a) forbids counseling or assisting another to alter or destroy evidence; MR 1.4(a)(5) requires the lawyer to explain the limits on her conduct when the client expects assistance the rules do not permit. **California:** Rule 1.2.1 is broader, forbidding counseling or assisting conduct the lawyer knows is criminal, fraudulent or a violation of any law; rule 3.4(a) and rule 1.4 are the same in substance.

**Application.** Nate said he would have the car fixed "before anyone sees it," a plain statement that the purpose was to destroy evidence of the collision before police could examine it. Alicia knew Nate had likely committed a hit-and-run. Her answer, "I can't tell you to do that — but there's no law against fixing your own car," was a wink: it told Nate that repairing the car was lawful when, in these circumstances, it was concealment of evidence. Alicia may argue that she declined to advise him to do it and only described the law. But the statement was misleading, she did not explain that destroying evidence of a crime is itself a crime, and Nate acted on it the next day. A lawyer may explain the consequences of a proposed course of conduct; she may not give it her blessing.

**Conclusion:** Alicia likely violated MR 1.2(d) and 3.4(a), and CRPC rules 1.2.1 and 3.4(a), and failed to explain the limits on her assistance (MR 1.4(a)(5); CRPC rule 1.4).

#### D. The statement at the bail hearing

**ABA:** MR 3.3(a)(1) forbids knowingly making a false statement of fact to a tribunal and requires the lawyer to correct a false statement of material fact she previously made; under MR 3.3(c), these duties apply even if compliance requires disclosure of information otherwise protected by MR 1.6. MR 8.4(c) forbids dishonesty. **California:** Rule 3.3(a)(1) contains the same prohibition and duty to correct, and B&P § 6068(d) requires a lawyer never to seek to mislead a judge by an artifice or false statement of fact; rule 8.4(c) forbids dishonesty and intentional misrepresentation.

**Application.** Alicia told the judge that Nate's car "has been sitting untouched in his garage since that night," knowing it had been repaired. The statement was material: the judge asked about tampering and set low bail. This was a knowing false statement of fact, not advocacy or opinion. Alicia might have said nothing about the car or simply argued for low bail; she chose to assert a fact she knew was false.

**Correction.** Under the ABA, Alicia must correct the false statement even if doing so reveals confidential information. In California, rule 3.3(a)(1) likewise requires her to correct a false statement she made, but California does not allow candor to override § 6068(e) generally; the safest California course is to correct her own assertion without revealing more of Nate's confidences than the correction requires (for example, by withdrawing her representation about the car), and to seek to withdraw if she cannot do so.

**Conclusion:** Alicia violated MR 3.3(a)(1) and 8.4(c), and CRPC rules 3.3(a)(1) and 8.4(c) and B&P § 6068(d).

#### E. Personal-interest conflict; lawyer as witness; competence

- **Personal interest.** Alicia's own conduct (the body-shop advice, the retained mirror, the false statement) exposes her to discipline and possibly criminal liability. That personal interest creates a significant risk of limiting her representation, for example by tempting her to steer Nate away from defenses that would expose her own advice (MR 1.7(a)(2); CRPC rule 1.7(b)). She must disclose the conflict and obtain Nate's informed consent (written, in California), or withdraw.
- **Lawyer as witness.** If the court orders her to testify about the body-shop exchange, she may be a necessary witness at trial. MR 3.7 and CRPC rule 3.7 bar a lawyer from acting as advocate at a trial in which she is likely to be a necessary witness, unless an exception applies (California allows the client's informed written consent, except for prosecutors).
- **Competence and reasonable precautions.** By allowing Ty into a privileged consultation, Alicia created the very risk of waiver the prosecution now exploits. A competent lawyer would have met Nate alone (MR 1.1, 1.6(c); CRPC rule 1.1 and § 6068(e)). Under California's higher threshold, a single lapse may not be disciplinable, but under the ABA it may be.

**Common mistakes**
1. Calling physical evidence privileged. Only communications are privileged; the object must be turned over.
2. Applying the crime-fraud exception to the confession of a past crime. It applies to services sought to commit or plan a crime or fraud, here the repair plan.
3. Treating *Meredith* as letting the prosecution prove that the client gave the object to the lawyer. Its exception concerns original location and condition when the defense moved or altered the evidence.
4. Saying Alicia breached confidentiality by testifying under a court order after asserting the privilege.
5. Saying California requires disclosure to correct a false statement to the same extent as MR 3.3(c). California does not let candor override § 6068(e) generally.

**Accuracy notes.** The answer relies on *People v. Meredith* only for its core holding (repo R-PRF-020) and not on *Fairbank* (repo item V-462). The scope of California's rule 3.3 duty to correct a lawyer's own false statement when correction would reveal client confidences is not settled in the repo's sources, so the answer recommends the cautious course and hedges the conclusion. It does not use the criminal-defendant perjury "narrative" approach (V-461).
