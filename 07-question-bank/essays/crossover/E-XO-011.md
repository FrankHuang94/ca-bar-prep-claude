## Facts

Silas and Delia married in California in 2010 and have lived here ever since.

**The Harriet Vance Trust.** In 2012, Silas's mother, Harriet, created an irrevocable trust funded with a commercial building she owned and named her sister, Agnes, as trustee. The trust provides: "The trustee shall pay all net income to my son, Silas, at least annually for his life, and may pay principal to Silas for his health and support in the trustee's discretion. On Silas's death, the trustee shall distribute the principal to Silas's children." It also provides: "No interest of any beneficiary shall be subject to voluntary or involuntary transfer before payment to the beneficiary." Agnes has paid Silas about $40,000 of net income each year. Silas deposited each payment into the couple's joint checking account, into which both spouses' salaries were also deposited and from which the family's living expenses were paid.

**The boat.** On May 2, 2020, Agnes paid Silas a $45,000 income distribution, which he deposited into the joint account. Before the deposit, the account held $20,000, all from salaries. On May 3, Silas wrote a $60,000 check on the joint account to buy a fishing boat, titled in his name. He wrote "trust money" on the memo line. The boat is now worth $40,000.

**The Vance Family Living Trust.** In 2016, Silas and Delia signed the Vance Family Living Trust, a revocable trust naming themselves as co-trustees. They transferred to it their home on Maple Street, which they had bought in 2011 with their salaries and which is now worth $800,000, and an investment account Delia had inherited from her father in 2014. The trust instrument states: "Property transferred to this trust shall retain its character as community or separate property. Schedule A lists community property; Schedule B lists Delia's separate property." The home is listed on Schedule A and Delia's inherited account on Schedule B. Article 5 provides: "Either settlor may revoke this trust as to property he or she contributed, by a signed writing delivered to the other settlor."

**Separation.** Silas and Delia separated in March 2025, and Delia filed for dissolution. In April 2025, Delia signed a letter stating, "I revoke the Vance Family Living Trust as to my separate property listed on Schedule B," handed it to Silas, and transferred the investment account, now worth $210,000, into her own name. Silas objects that the account became "ours" when it went into the joint trust and that, as co-trustee, he never agreed to its removal.

**Support.** In September 2025, the family court ordered Silas to pay Delia $3,000 a month in temporary spousal support. Silas has paid nothing. In October 2025, at Silas's request ("I don't want Delia getting any of it"), Agnes stopped paying income to Silas and began holding it in the trust. Silas now owes $24,000 in support. Delia wants to collect it from the Harriet Vance Trust.

## Call of the Question

Answer according to California law.

1. What are Silas's and Delia's rights in (a) the boat, (b) the Maple Street home, and (c) the investment account Delia removed from the Vance Family Living Trust?
2. Can Delia reach Silas's interest in the Harriet Vance Trust to collect spousal support, and did Agnes breach any duty by accumulating the trust income?

## Model Answer

California law governs. Property acquired during marriage while domiciled in California is presumed to be community property (CP) (Fam. Code § 760). Property acquired by gift or inheritance, and its rents, issues and profits, is separate property (SP) (§ 770). Character can change only by a written transmutation (§ 852). At dissolution the community estate is divided equally (§ 2550). For trust questions, the Probate Code rule is given with the general rule where they differ.

### 1. Characterization

#### (a) The boat

**The trust distributions are Silas's SP.** Silas's beneficial interest in the Harriet Vance Trust came to him by gift from his mother, so it is his SP (§ 770(a)(2)). Distributions of income from that interest are its rents, issues and profits, which are also SP (§ 770(a)(3)). The $45,000 Silas received on May 2, 2020 was his SP.

**Commingling.** The boat was bought during marriage from the joint account, which held both salaries and trust income, so it is presumptively CP (§ 760), and title in Silas's name does not change that (*In re Marriage of Valli*). Mixing SP into a joint account does not change its character, but Silas bears the burden of tracing. If he cannot trace, the commingled funds are treated as CP (*See v. See*; *In re Marriage of Mix*).

**Direct tracing.** Under *Mix*, a spouse traces directly by showing that SP funds were available in the account when the purchase was made and that he intended to use them.
- *Intent:* the memo "trust money" shows Silas meant to use his separate funds.
- *Availability:* the account then held $20,000 of community salary and $45,000 of trust income. SP funds of $45,000 were available, but the check was for $60,000, so at least $15,000 had to come from community funds.

Delia will argue that the account was a family account into which years of trust income disappeared, so nothing can be traced. But this purchase came the day after a specific, documented $45,000 deposit, which is the kind of contemporaneous record direct tracing requires. The exhaustion method does not help Silas further, because community funds were not exhausted when the check was written. Earlier trust distributions spent on living expenses are presumed gifts to the community and create no reimbursement claim (See).

**Result.** The boat is apportioned by the funds used: $45,000/$60,000 = **75% Silas's SP** and **25% CP**. Of its current $40,000 value, $30,000 is Silas's SP and $10,000 is CP, so Delia is entitled to $5,000. The court will likely award the boat to Silas with a $5,000 offset to Delia.

#### (b) The Maple Street home

The home was bought in 2011, during marriage, with salaries, so it is **CP**. Transferring it to the revocable Vance Family Living Trust did not change its character. A transmutation requires a writing with an express declaration that changes character or ownership (§ 852(a); *Estate of MacDonald*). The trust instrument says the opposite: property "shall retain its character," and the home is listed on Schedule A as CP. Placing community property in a revocable trust, with both spouses as trustees, preserves the spouses' community ownership. The home is CP worth $800,000; each spouse is entitled to $400,000, and the court will award it to one spouse with an offset or order a sale.

#### (c) Delia's inherited investment account

**Character.** The account was inherited by Delia in 2014, so it and its growth are her SP (§ 770).

**Did it become CP when placed in the joint trust?** Silas has two arguments, and both fail.
1. *Transmutation.* A transfer into a trust held by both spouses is not an express declaration that ownership has changed. Here the instrument expressly preserves character and lists the account on Schedule B as "Delia's separate property." There is no transmutation.
2. *Joint-title presumption.* At dissolution, property acquired during marriage in joint form is presumed CP, but the presumption is rebutted by a clear statement in the title document or by a written agreement that the property is SP (§ 2581). Even if title held by both spouses as co-trustees counts as "joint form," the signed trust instrument is a written agreement stating that the account is Delia's SP. The presumption is rebutted.

**Was Delia's removal of the account valid?**
- *Revocability.* In California a trust is revocable unless the instrument expressly makes it irrevocable (Prob. Code § 15400). The Vance trust is expressly revocable.
- *Method.* A trust may be revoked by any method stated in the instrument, or by a signed writing (other than a will) delivered to the trustee during the settlor's lifetime (§ 15401(a)). Article 5 lets either settlor revoke as to property he or she contributed by a signed writing delivered to the other settlor. Delia contributed the account, signed a revocation limited to her Schedule B property, and handed it to Silas. She followed the trust's own method exactly.
- *Silas's co-trustee argument.* Revocation is a power of the **settlor**, not an act of trust administration, so the rule that co-trustees act together does not apply. Once the trust was revoked as to the account, the account was no longer trust property, and Delia was entitled to have it returned to her. Silas's consent was not needed.

**Result.** The $210,000 account is **Delia's SP**, validly withdrawn. Silas has no interest in it.

### 2. The Harriet Vance Trust

**A. Character of Silas's interest.** Silas's beneficial interest in the trust is his SP (a gift from his mother), and no community labor or funds contributed to it. Delia has no community claim to the trust interest at dissolution. Her claim is as a support creditor.

**B. The spendthrift clause.** If a trust instrument provides that a beneficiary's interest is not subject to voluntary or involuntary transfer, the restraint is valid, and creditors cannot reach the interest before payment to the beneficiary, subject to statutory exceptions (Prob. Code §§ 15300, 15301). The Harriet trust contains such a clause, and Harriet, not Silas, created the trust, so the clause is valid. Silas has two kinds of interest: a **mandatory** right to all net income at least annually, and a **discretionary** interest in principal for his health and support. A creditor generally cannot compel a discretionary distribution (§ 15303).

**C. The support exception.** Notwithstanding a spendthrift clause, a beneficiary's interest may be reached to satisfy a judgment or order for support of the beneficiary's **spouse** or former spouse or child. The court may order the trustee to pay the support from income or principal that is payable to the beneficiary, or that the trustee in its discretion has decided to pay, as is equitable (§ 15305). General trust law likewise recognizes support claimants as exception creditors.
- *Delia qualifies.* The dissolution is pending, so she is still Silas's spouse, and she holds a court order for spousal support on which $24,000 is unpaid.
- *Income.* Silas's income is mandatory. Under the trust's terms it is payable to him at least annually, whether or not Agnes chooses to pay it. The court can order Agnes to pay Delia the $24,000 arrears and the ongoing $3,000 a month from income.
- *Principal.* Principal is payable only when Agnes exercises her discretion to distribute it. Unless she decides to pay principal, Delia cannot reach it.
- *Silas's argument.* The spendthrift clause shows Harriet's intent to protect her son, and his children's remainder should not bear his obligations. But § 15305 applies notwithstanding the clause, and paying support from income does not touch the children's remainder in principal.

**D. Agnes's accumulation of income**

*Breach of the trust's terms.* A trustee must administer the trust according to the trust instrument (§ 16000). Harriet's trust says the trustee "shall pay all net income" to Silas at least annually; Agnes has no power to accumulate it. By holding the income since October 2025, Agnes is administering the trust contrary to its terms.

*Agnes's defense: Silas asked her to.* A beneficiary who consents to the trustee's conduct generally cannot later hold the trustee liable for it, so Silas has no claim against Agnes. The remainder beneficiaries benefit from the accumulation rather than being harmed by it. But the beneficiary's consent does not change the trust's terms, and a trustee may not depart from mandatory terms to help a beneficiary evade a court support order. Agnes's purpose, which Silas stated openly ("I don't want Delia getting any of it"), was to defeat the very exception the Legislature created for support claimants.

*Delia's remedy.* Delia is not a trust beneficiary, so she cannot sue Agnes for breach of trust. But she does not need to. Under § 15305 she may obtain an order directing the trustee to pay the support from income payable to Silas, and the accumulated income was payable to him under the mandatory terms. The court can order Agnes to pay the arrears from the income she is holding. A trustee who disregarded such an order would face contempt and, at the instance of a beneficiary or the court, surcharge or removal.

**Conclusion.** Delia can reach Silas's mandatory income interest, including the income Agnes has been holding, to collect the $24,000 arrears and future support, but not discretionary principal. Agnes breached her duty to administer the trust according to its terms. Silas, who requested the accumulation, cannot complain, and Delia's remedy is a § 15305 order rather than a breach-of-trust suit.

**Common mistakes**
1. Treating trust distributions from a parent's trust as CP because they were received during marriage. They are SP (gift and its profits).
2. Finding direct tracing for the full $60,000 when only $45,000 of SP was available.
3. Treating the transfer of property into a joint revocable trust as a transmutation, despite a clause preserving character.
4. Requiring both co-trustees to consent to a settlor's revocation that follows the trust's own method.
5. Letting the spendthrift clause defeat a spouse's support claim, or letting Delia reach discretionary principal.
