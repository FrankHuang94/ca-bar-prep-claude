# E-XO-023 — The banner across from the mosque

## Facts

In 2025 the State X legislature enacted the Community Harmony Act. Section 18 provides:

> "It is a misdemeanor for any person, within 300 feet of a house of worship, school or community center, to display any sign, banner, flag or emblem that a reasonable person would find degrading to any person or group on account of race, religion, national origin, sex or sexual orientation."

No court had ruled on Section 18 before the events below.

At 1:15 p.m. on Friday, September 11, 2026, as midday prayers ended at the Al-Noor Mosque, Dale Mercer stood silently on the public sidewalk across the street, about 150 feet from the mosque's entrance. He held a six-foot banner reading "YOUR RELIGION IS A FRAUD — GO BACK WHERE YOU CAME FROM." About 40 worshippers gathered across the street. Some shouted at Dale. One man crossed the street, stood a foot from Dale's face and yelled at him; Dale said nothing. No one was hurt.

Officer Hayes arrived, told Dale that he was violating Section 18, and ordered him to take the banner down. Dale refused, saying, "This is a free country." Hayes arrested him for violating Section 18, handcuffed him and locked him in the back of the patrol car. Dale's car was parked legally at a paid meter about 30 feet away. Looking through the car's rear window from the sidewalk, Hayes saw a partly unrolled banner on the back seat with the visible words "YOUR RELIGION IS." Hayes opened the car with Dale's keys, took the second banner, and then unzipped a closed backpack on the front passenger floor. Inside it he found a bag of methamphetamine.

Dale asked that his car be left where it was so that his sister could pick it up. The police department's written impound policy provides: "Vehicles of arrested persons shall be impounded only if illegally parked, if a traffic hazard, or if no other person can remove them within a reasonable time; otherwise the vehicle shall be left legally parked and locked." Hayes nonetheless had the car towed. His report states: "Impounded to determine whether the suspect had further hate materials or contraband." An inventory at the tow yard found nothing else of note.

Dale has been charged with violating Section 18 and with possession of methamphetamine. He moves to dismiss the Section 18 charge on constitutional grounds and to suppress the methamphetamine.

## Call of the Question

1. Can Dale constitutionally be convicted of violating Section 18? Discuss.
2. Should the court grant Dale's motion to suppress the methamphetamine? Discuss.

## Model Answer

### 1. The Section 18 charge

Section 18 is a state criminal statute enforced by a state officer, so it is state action subject to the First Amendment through the Fourteenth.

**Protected speech in a public forum.** Dale's banner is pure written speech, displayed on a public sidewalk, a traditional public forum where content-based restrictions get strict scrutiny and content-neutral rules must satisfy the time, place and manner test (*Hague*; *Perry Education Ass'n*). The State may argue that the banner falls into an unprotected category:
- *Fighting words* are personally abusive words, directed face-to-face at a person, likely to provoke an immediate violent reaction (*Chaplinsky*). Dale stood silently with a sign addressed to the public at large, across the street. The one man who confronted him came to Dale; Dale said nothing to him. Offensive words aimed at no particular person are protected (*Cohen v. California*).
- *Incitement* requires advocacy directed to and likely to produce imminent lawless action (*Brandenburg*). The banner urges no lawless action at all.
- *True threats* are serious expressions of intent to commit unlawful violence against a person or group. "Go back where you came from" is hostile and insulting, but it expresses no intent to commit violence.

The crowd's anger does not change the analysis: the government may not suppress speech because listeners react with hostility (a "heckler's veto"; compare *Forsyth County*). **The banner is protected speech.**

**Content and viewpoint discrimination.** A law is content-based if it applies to speech because of its message, topic or viewpoint; viewpoint discrimination is the most egregious form and is presumptively invalid in every forum (*Reed*; *Rosenberger*). Section 18 applies only to signs a reasonable person would find "degrading" on account of race, religion, national origin, sex or sexual orientation. Liability turns on what the message says: whether it degrades people on account of listed traits. That is a distinction based on communicative content (*Reed*), not merely a need to read the sign, which alone does not make a law content-based (*City of Austin*). A banner praising a religion, or degrading people for their politics or profession, is untouched; a banner degrading people for their religion is a crime. Laws that suppress speech because it disparages or demeans are viewpoint-based (*Matal v. Tam*). **Section 18 is content-based and viewpoint-based.**

**Strict scrutiny.** The State must prove that Section 18 is narrowly tailored, by the least restrictive means, to a compelling interest.
- *Interest.* The State's interests in protecting members of historically targeted groups, and in allowing people to worship and attend school without harassment, are compelling (the *R.A.V.* Court accepted a similar interest).
- *Captive audience.* The State will argue that worshippers leaving a mosque, and children at school, are a captive audience who cannot avoid the message. But the captive-audience idea is narrow and has been applied mainly to the home (*Frisby*); worshippers on a public street can avert their eyes (*Cohen*). And Section 18 covers any community center, not only places where people are unable to avoid the speech.
- *Tailoring.* The law is not the least restrictive means. Content-neutral rules would serve the interest directly: bans on obstructing entrances, noise limits during services, laws against true threats, intimidation or harassment of identified people, or a neutral buffer zone. In *R.A.V.*, the Court struck down a law reaching only those fighting words that insulted on the basis of race, religion or gender, because content-neutral alternatives were available and the government may not single out disfavored viewpoints even within an unprotected category. Section 18 is broader still, because it is not even limited to fighting words.

**Section 18 fails strict scrutiny and is unconstitutional as applied to Dale.**

**Overbreadth.** A law regulating speech is facially invalid if it prohibits a substantial amount of protected speech relative to its plainly legitimate sweep, and a defendant may raise the claim on behalf of others whose speech is chilled (*Broadrick*; *Stevens*). Section 18's legitimate sweep, true threats and fighting words near houses of worship, is small. Its protected sweep is enormous: a satirical cartoon about a religion, a banner criticizing a religious group's treatment of women, or a protest sign about a nation's government displayed near a school could all be "degrading" in someone's reasonable view. No narrowing construction is available without rewriting the statute. **Section 18 is facially overbroad.**

**Vagueness.** A law is void for vagueness if it fails to give a person of ordinary intelligence fair notice of what is forbidden or invites arbitrary enforcement, and the standard is strictest for laws reaching speech (*Grayned*; *Coates*). Whether a reasonable person would find a message "degrading" depends on the sensibilities of the audience and the officer, much like the "annoying" conduct struck down in *Coates*. The State will argue that the "reasonable person" standard supplies an objective measure, as in many tort and criminal laws. But applied to expression, the standard leaves speakers to guess which criticisms will be judged degrading rather than merely critical, and it leaves officers with broad discretion, as Officer Hayes's on-the-spot judgment shows. **Section 18 is likely void for vagueness.**

**Conclusion:** Dale cannot constitutionally be convicted under Section 18; the charge should be dismissed.

### 2. The motion to suppress the methamphetamine

**Standing.** Fourth Amendment rights are personal. Dale owns the car and the backpack, so he has a reasonable expectation of privacy in both and may challenge the search.

**The arrest.** An arrest requires probable cause, and an officer may make a warrantless custodial arrest in public for a minor offense committed in his presence (*Watson*; *Atwater*). Hayes saw Dale display a banner within 300 feet of a mosque that a reasonable person could find degrading on account of religion, which is probable cause that Dale violated Section 18 as written.

**Effect of the statute's unconstitutionality (*DeFillippo*).** Dale will argue that because Section 18 is unconstitutional, his arrest was unlawful and everything found as a result is fruit of the poisonous tree. In *Michigan v. DeFillippo*, the Supreme Court held that an arrest made in good-faith reliance on an ordinance that had not yet been declared unconstitutional is valid, and evidence found in a search incident to that arrest is admissible, unless the law is so grossly and flagrantly unconstitutional that any person of reasonable prudence would be bound to see its flaws. Police are not expected to predict constitutional rulings on newly enacted laws.

Dale will argue that Section 18 is that kind of law: it targets speech by viewpoint in terms close to the law struck down in *R.A.V.* decades ago. The State will respond that the legislature enacted it only a year earlier, no court had questioned it, and its "reasonable person" standard and location limits make it look like an ordinary public-order law to a non-lawyer. **The better view is that the arrest was valid under *DeFillippo*.** (This is distinct from *Illinois v. Krull*, which applies the good-faith exception to statutes that *authorize searches*; here the question is the validity of an arrest for a substantive offense.)

**Search incident to arrest: the car and the backpack.** After a lawful custodial arrest, police may search a vehicle's passenger compartment, and containers in it, only if (1) the arrestee is unsecured and within reaching distance of the compartment, or (2) it is reasonable to believe that evidence of the offense of arrest might be found in the vehicle (*Arizona v. Gant*).
- *Threshold: recent occupant.* *Gant* governs a vehicle search incident to the arrest of an occupant or recent occupant (*Thornton*). Dale was arrested on the sidewalk, 30 feet from a car parked at a meter, and nothing shows when he last occupied it. The State will argue the car was close by and tied to the display; Dale will argue he was arrested as a pedestrian, so the vehicle rule does not apply at all.
- *Prong 1.* Dale was handcuffed and locked in the patrol car. **Not met.**
- *Prong 2.* The State will argue that Hayes saw, through the window, a second banner beginning with the same words as the one Dale displayed, which made it reasonable to believe that more evidence of the display offense, such as other banners or the materials used to make them, was in the car, and a backpack could hold such items. Dale will respond that the offense of arrest, displaying a banner on the sidewalk, was complete and fully evidenced by the banner Hayes had already seized; a second, undisplayed banner proves no element of the charged display; and *Gant* prong 2 is meant for offenses, such as drug crimes, where evidence is likely to be in the car, not for public-display offenses.

This is close. The second banner ties the car to the offense of arrest, and courts generally treat "reasonable to believe" as less demanding than probable cause, so **a court could find prong 2 satisfied and the backpack within its scope.** But the State bears the burden of justifying a warrantless search, the facts do not show that Dale was a recent occupant, and the display offense was complete and fully evidenced by the banner already seized. **The better view is that the search was not a valid search incident to arrest.**

**Automobile exception and plain view.** Police with probable cause to believe a vehicle contains contraband or evidence of a crime may search it, including containers that could hold the object of the search (*Carroll*; *Acevedo*). Hayes was lawfully on the sidewalk when he saw the partly unrolled banner, so his observation was not a search. But plain view permits a seizure only if the item's incriminating character is immediately apparent. Possessing an undisplayed banner in a car is not a crime, and its evidentiary value for the completed display offense is slight. **Probable cause for the automobile exception is doubtful.**

**Inventory search and inevitable discovery.** Inventory searches of lawfully impounded vehicles are valid if conducted under standardized criteria and not as a ruse for general rummaging for evidence (*Bertine*; *Wells*). Here the department's policy required leaving a legally parked car in place where, as here, another person (Dale's sister) could remove it; the car was legally parked at a paid meter, and Hayes's report admits that he impounded it "to determine whether the suspect had further hate materials or contraband," an investigative purpose. The impound violated the policy, so the inventory cannot justify the search. For the same reason, the State cannot show by a preponderance that the methamphetamine would *inevitably* have been discovered by lawful means (*Nix v. Williams*): a lawful application of the policy would have left the car unsearched.

**Exclusionary rule and conclusion.** Evidence obtained in violation of the Fourth Amendment is excluded from the prosecution's case-in-chief unless an exception applies; the good-faith exception does not cover an officer's own mistaken judgment about the scope of a search incident to arrest. Because the arrest was valid under *DeFillippo*, the outcome turns on *Gant*. **If the court finds Dale a recent occupant and accepts the State's prong-2 argument, the methamphetamine is admissible; otherwise it must be suppressed**, since neither the automobile exception, the inventory exception nor inevitable discovery applies. On these facts, the lack of recent occupancy and the completed display offense give Dale the better argument, so the motion is likely granted.

**Common mistakes**
1. Calling offensive speech "fighting words." The doctrine requires face-to-face personal insults likely to provoke immediate violence.
2. Treating a law that bans only "degrading" messages as content-neutral. Disparagement-based bans are viewpoint discrimination.
3. Assuming every fruit of an arrest under an unconstitutional statute must be suppressed. *DeFillippo* upholds arrests made in reasonable reliance on a presumptively valid law.
4. Applying the old *Belton* rule. After *Gant*, a secured arrestee's car may be searched incident to arrest only if evidence of the offense of arrest might be inside.
5. Accepting an "inventory" that departs from written policy and is admittedly investigative.

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: PRC law punishes inciting ethnic hatred (煽动民族仇恨) and similar speech offenses; the US First Amendment protects offensive and hateful expression unless it falls into a narrow category such as true threats or incitement to imminent lawless action, and forbids viewpoint-based bans (approx.).
