# E-XO-009 — The Cypress Glen Inn

## Facts

In 1979, Harbor Lands, Inc. subdivided a tract in the City of Marisol into 40 lots called Cypress Glen. Before selling any lot, it recorded a "Declaration of Restrictions" covering all 40 lots. The Declaration provides: "Each lot shall be used solely for single-family residential purposes. No trade, business or commercial activity shall be conducted on any lot. These restrictions run with the land, bind all owners and their successors, and may be enforced by any lot owner." Thirty-nine of the original deeds expressly referred to the Declaration. The deed for Lot 7 did not.

Hana Mori bought Lot 7 in 2024 for $850,000. Her title report listed the Declaration as an exception to coverage, but she did not read it. Cypress Glen remains entirely single-family homes. Over the years, three owners have worked from home offices: a software engineer who never has visitors, an online seller who ships packages, and Jun Park, the owner of Lot 6, a tax accountant who meets clients at his home about twice a week during tax season. From 2022 to 2023, one owner rented his house to tourists about 40 nights a year until neighbors complained, and then he stopped.

Lot 7 is one of eight lots that front Coast Highway on the subdivision's outer edge. In 2015 a shopping center was built across the highway, outside Cypress Glen, and highway traffic has increased. The interior streets of Cypress Glen remain quiet.

In 2025, the City rezoned the eight highway-fronting lots to allow bed-and-breakfast inns with a conditional use permit. Hana applied for a permit to convert her five-bedroom house into the "Cypress Glen Inn," with four guest rooms, breakfast for guests, online booking, a small sign, and Hana living on site as innkeeper.

The City's zoning code requires one off-street parking space for each guest room plus two for the owner, six in total. Hana's lot can hold four spaces, in part because a city drainage easement crosses one side yard; the other seven highway-fronting lots are the same size but have no easement. Hana requested a variance from the parking requirement. The City denied it, finding that "the applicant could operate with two guest rooms and comply, so any hardship is self-created."

The Planning Commission approved the conditional use permit on two conditions:
1. Hana must dedicate to the City a 10-foot-wide strip along her highway frontage (1,200 square feet) for a public bicycle path, part of a coastal bike route the City has planned since 2010; and
2. Hana must pay a $15,000 "park mitigation fee," because "inn guests may use City parks."

The City's traffic engineer estimated that the inn would add about eight vehicle trips per day to Coast Highway, which carries about 30,000 trips per day. The Commission made no other findings about the inn's effect on traffic or parks.

Jun has sued Hana to enjoin her from operating the inn. Hana has sued the City, challenging the variance denial and the permit conditions.

## Call of the Question

1. Can Jun enforce the Declaration to prevent Hana from operating the bed-and-breakfast? Discuss.
2. Did the City properly deny Hana's parking variance? Discuss.
3. Are the bike-path dedication and the park fee conditions valid under the United States Constitution? Discuss.

## Model Answer

### 1. Can Jun enforce the Declaration?

**Real covenant or equitable servitude.** A promise about land use can bind successors at law as a real covenant (remedy: damages) or in equity as an equitable servitude (remedy: injunction). Jun wants to stop the inn, so the equitable servitude is the right vehicle. (A real covenant would also likely run: the restriction was created in the developer's conveyances, giving horizontal privity, and Hana succeeded to the whole estate of Lot 7, giving vertical privity.)

An equitable servitude binds a successor if (1) it is in a writing (or implied from a common scheme), (2) the original parties intended it to bind successors, (3) it touches and concerns the land, and (4) the successor took with notice.

- **Writing and intent.** The Declaration is a recorded writing stating that the restrictions "run with the land" and "bind all owners and their successors." **Satisfied.**
- **Touch and concern.** A restriction limiting a lot to residential use restricts how the burdened land is used and preserves the residential character and value of the neighboring lots. **Satisfied.**

**Notice despite the silent deed.** Hana will argue that she is not bound because her own deed does not mention the Declaration. That argument fails for several reasons.

- *Record notice.* The Declaration was recorded in 1979, before any lot was sold, and expressly covers all 40 lots, including Lot 7. A purchaser is charged with notice of a recorded instrument in her chain of title. Her title report even listed the Declaration; failing to read it does not help her.
- *Inquiry notice.* Cypress Glen consists entirely of single-family homes, a uniform pattern that would prompt a reasonable buyer to ask whether restrictions apply.
- *Common scheme.* Under the majority rule, when a developer sells lots under a general plan with restrictions in most deeds (39 of 40 here), courts imply a reciprocal servitude binding every lot in the plan, including one whose deed is silent, if its owner had notice (*Sanborn v. McLean*).
- *California.* California will not imply a servitude from a common plan alone, but a recorded declaration that describes the property and states that it binds all purchasers is enforceable as an equitable servitude even if a later deed is silent (*Citizens for Covenant Compliance v. Anderson*). The Cypress Glen Declaration fits that rule exactly.

**Hana took with notice; the restriction binds Lot 7.**

**Who may enforce.** The Declaration says it "may be enforced by any lot owner," and under a common scheme every lot owner holds the benefit. Jun, the owner of neighboring Lot 6, **may enforce it.**

**Does the inn violate the restriction?** A servitude is interpreted to carry out the parties' intent as shown by its words and the circumstances; older cases resolve genuine ambiguity in favor of free use of land. Hana will argue that she will live in the house, so its use remains residential, and that guests use the bedrooms for sleeping, a residential activity. But the Declaration says "solely for single-family residential purposes" and separately forbids any "trade, business or commercial activity." An inn renting four rooms to paying guests, with online booking, breakfast service and a sign, is a commercial lodging business. It brings strangers and traffic into the neighborhood in a way that changes the residential character the Declaration protects. **The inn violates the restriction.**

**Defense: changed conditions.** A restriction becomes unenforceable if conditions have changed so radically that it can no longer achieve its purpose. Changes outside the restricted area usually do not suffice if the restriction still benefits the lots inside it. Hana will point to the 2015 shopping center, the heavier highway traffic and the City's rezoning. But the shopping center is across the highway, outside Cypress Glen; the interior streets remain quiet; and the restriction still protects the interior lots from commercial traffic. Border lots facing a changed street may remain bound when interior lots still benefit. The City's rezoning does not help her either: zoning permission does not override private restrictions. **The defense fails.**

**Defenses: abandonment, acquiescence and unclean hands.** A restriction is abandoned only if violations are so widespread that the plan's purpose is defeated. A plaintiff who has acquiesced in similar violations, or who violates the same restriction himself, may be barred.

- *Abandonment.* Three home offices, mostly invisible from the street, and one short-term rental that stopped after complaints are minor and do not defeat the residential plan of a 40-lot subdivision that remains entirely single-family. **No abandonment.**
- *Unclean hands and acquiescence.* Hana's best argument is that Jun himself violates the "no trade, business" clause by meeting accounting clients at his home about twice a week during tax season. Jun will respond that his office is incidental to his residential use, with occasional visitors, like a typical home office, and differs in kind from a full-time inn with paying overnight guests and signage. Courts generally treat minor incidental home uses as not violating "residential purposes," and acquiescence bars a plaintiff only as to violations similar to those he tolerated or committed. Jun's own practice is a stronger point than the others, and a court could find he must also stop meeting clients at home, but it is not comparable to an inn.

**Jun will likely obtain an injunction** against operating the inn.

### 2. The parking variance

**Rule.** A variance is administrative relief from a zoning requirement, granted only where strict application would cause unnecessary hardship because of conditions peculiar to the property (such as size, shape or topography), the hardship is not self-created, and the variance will not harm the neighborhood or conflict with the general plan. An area variance (a setback or parking standard) is easier to obtain than a use variance (permitting a use not allowed in the zone). In California, variances may not authorize a use not otherwise allowed and require special circumstances that deprive the property of privileges enjoyed by others in the same zone (Gov. Code § 65906), and the agency must make findings that bridge the evidence and its decision (*Topanga*).

**Application.** Hana seeks relief from a parking standard for a use the zone permits by conditional use permit, so this is an **area** variance.

- *Unique condition.* Hana will point to the city drainage easement across her side yard, a feature none of the other seven highway-fronting lots shares and one that reduces her parking area.
- *Self-created hardship.* The City found that Hana could operate with two guest rooms and comply. The hardship arises less from the easement than from her choice to operate four rooms. A variance is not meant to let an owner use land more intensively than the code allows, and Hana can still use the lot reasonably, as a home or as a smaller inn.
- *Neighborhood impact.* Two missing spaces would push guest parking onto streets in a residential neighborhood, the very effect the parking standard targets.

The easement gives Hana a genuine argument that her lot is unusual. But because a reasonable use remains available and the shortfall results from her chosen intensity, **the denial was likely proper**, provided the City's findings, which it made, link the evidence to the decision.

### 3. The permit conditions under the Takings Clause

**Ripeness and framework.** The Fifth Amendment's Takings Clause, applied to the States through the Fourteenth, forbids taking private property for public use without just compensation. The Commission has made a final decision imposing specific conditions, so Hana's challenge is ripe, and she may sue in federal court under § 1983 without first seeking compensation in state court (*Knick*).

If the City simply required Hana to give the public a permanent right to travel across her land, that would be a per se physical taking (*Loretto*; *Nollan*). The City may not avoid that rule by demanding the easement as the price of a permit unless the condition satisfies the unconstitutional-conditions test for exactions: (1) an **essential nexus** between the condition and a legitimate interest that would justify denying the permit (*Nollan*), and (2) **rough proportionality**, shown by an individualized determination, between the condition and the project's impact (*Dolan*).

**Bike-path dedication: nexus.** The City's legitimate interest would be reducing the traffic congestion the inn creates; a permit could be denied, or conditioned, to address congestion. A bicycle path can offer an alternative to car trips, and the Supreme Court in *Dolan* accepted that a bike path could have a nexus to traffic from an expanding business. Hana will respond that the true purpose is to obtain land for a coastal route the City has planned "since 2010," regardless of her inn, and that guests at a four-room inn will arrive by car, not bicycle. The facts suggest the condition may be an attempt to obtain land the City wanted anyway, the concern behind *Nollan*. **A nexus is arguable but weak.**

**Bike-path dedication: rough proportionality.** Even with a nexus, the City must make an individualized determination that the dedication is related in nature and extent to the inn's impact. Its own engineer estimated that the inn would add about **eight** trips per day to a highway carrying 30,000, less than three hundredths of one percent. The City demanded a permanent public easement over 1,200 square feet, made no finding that the path would offset any of those trips, and conducted no other study. As in *Dolan*, where the city failed to quantify how a bike path would offset the store's added traffic, **the dedication is not roughly proportional and is an unconstitutional condition.**

**The $15,000 park fee.** Nollan and Dolan apply to monetary exactions as well as to dedications of land: a demand for money as a condition of a land-use permit must meet the nexus and rough-proportionality tests (*Koontz*). The Commission imposed this fee in Hana's individual permit proceeding.

- *Nexus.* The City will argue that more visitors mean more use of public parks. Hana will respond that the only support is the speculative statement that guests "may use City parks"; overnight visitors to a four-room inn have no demonstrated connection to park demand.
- *Proportionality.* The City made no study of how many guests would use parks, what that use costs, or how $15,000 relates to it. The amount appears arbitrary.

**The park fee likely fails *Koontz*'s application of *Nollan* and *Dolan*.**

**Remedy.** Because the conditions are unconstitutional, Hana may obtain declaratory and injunctive relief striking them, so that the permit stands without them. If she had already conveyed the strip or paid the fee under protest, she could seek just compensation or a refund. Injunctions against takings are generally unavailable where compensation is available, but an unconstitutional permit condition not yet satisfied may be set aside.

**Practical note.** Even if Hana wins on call 3, the conditional use permit does not override the private restriction in call 1. If Jun obtains his injunction, Hana still cannot operate the inn.

**Common mistakes**
1. Relying on Hana's silent deed. A recorded declaration covering all lots gives record notice.
2. Treating the City's rezoning as overriding the private restriction. Zoning and covenants operate independently.
3. Using *Penn Central* balancing for a demand for land or money as a permit condition. Use *Nollan*/*Dolan* (and *Koontz* for money).
4. Ignoring the City's own figures. The eight-trip estimate is the key fact for rough proportionality.
5. Calling a parking variance a "use variance." It is an area variance; California's ban on use variances does not apply.
