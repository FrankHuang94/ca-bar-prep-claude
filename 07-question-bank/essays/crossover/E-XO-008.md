## Facts

Dev and Kira married in California in 2012. In 2016, Dev formed Kestrel Consulting LLC, a software consulting business, with $30,000 from the couple's joint savings account. Dev is listed as Kestrel's sole member and has worked in the business full time ever since. Kestrel was worth about $500,000 when the spouses separated and is worth about the same today.

Dev began contributing to a 401(k) plan at his former employer in 2008, and the account moved with him to Kestrel. At the 2012 wedding the balance was $40,000. The plan statements show that this $40,000 has remained invested in a single index fund, now worth $100,000. All contributions made during the marriage, and the $6,000 Dev has contributed since separation, were invested in other funds, which are now worth $280,000.

Kira, a freelance graphic designer, has since 2019 deposited part of her freelance earnings into a savings account at Harbor Savings in her own name. Dev does not know about the account, which holds $60,000.

In January 2025, Dev told Kira that he wanted a divorce and moved out.

In February 2025, Dev and Kira met together with attorney Celeste Ng, who has represented Kestrel Consulting LLC since 2017 on client contracts and still does. They told Celeste they wanted an "amicable, cheap divorce" using one lawyer. Celeste said, "I can represent you both and make sure it's fair to each of you." She did not explain any risks of representing both of them, did not mention her work for Kestrel, and obtained no written consent. They orally agreed to a flat fee of $12,000 for the entire dissolution, which they paid from their joint checking account. There is no written fee agreement. As they were leaving, Celeste told Kira privately, "If I get you more than half of the property, I'll take a bonus of 10% of the extra."

A week later, Kira called Celeste alone. She described the Harbor Savings account and said, "Please don't tell Dev. It's my rainy-day money." Celeste replied, "That stays between us." Celeste then prepared both spouses' preliminary declarations of disclosure, which each spouse must sign under penalty of perjury and serve on the other, listing all assets and debts. Kira's declaration, as Celeste prepared it, omits the Harbor Savings account. Both spouses signed, and the declarations were exchanged.

Dev has since asked Celeste to "keep Kestrel's valuation low, since you know the business."

## Call of the Question

1. What are Dev's and Kira's rights in (a) Kestrel Consulting, (b) Dev's 401(k) account, and (c) the Harbor Savings account? Answer according to California law.
2. What ethical violations, if any, has Celeste committed? Answer according to California and ABA authorities.

## Model Answer

### 1. Community property rights

California is a community property state. Property acquired during marriage while domiciled in California is presumed to be community property (CP) (Fam. Code § 760); property acquired before marriage, and earnings after the date of separation (DOS), are separate property (SP) (§§ 770, 771). The DOS is when a spouse expresses to the other the intent to end the marriage and acts consistently with it (§ 70). Dev announced he wanted a divorce and moved out in January 2025, so that is the DOS. The community estate is divided equally (§ 2550).

#### (a) Kestrel Consulting

Kestrel was formed in 2016, during the marriage, with $30,000 of community savings, and its value was built by Dev's full-time labor during the marriage, which belongs to the community. It is presumptively CP. Dev will argue that he is Kestrel's sole member and the business is "his." But record title or membership in one spouse's name does not make a community-funded asset his SP absent a valid written transmutation (*In re Marriage of Valli*; § 852(a)), and Kira signed nothing. **Kestrel is CP worth about $500,000.** The court will likely award it to Dev, who runs it, and give Kira $250,000 in offsetting assets or an equalizing payment.

#### (b) Dev's 401(k)

Retirement benefits are deferred compensation and are CP to the extent earned by employment during marriage and before separation (*In re Marriage of Brown*). Contributions and their growth before marriage are SP, as are contributions after DOS.
- *Premarital balance.* The $40,000 Dev had at marriage stayed in one index fund, which the plan statements trace to its current $100,000 value. That growth is the rents, issues and profits of SP, so **$100,000 is Dev's SP**.
- *Marital contributions.* Contributions from 2012 to January 2025 came from Dev's earnings during marriage and are **CP**. They sit in the other funds, which, together with the post-DOS contributions, are now worth $280,000.
- *Post-DOS contributions.* The $6,000 contributed since separation, and its growth, is Dev's SP.

Kira is entitled to half of the community portion (about $137,000 after removing the post-DOS contributions from the $280,000). If the plan is governed by ERISA, the division will be made through a qualified domestic relations order (QDRO), the domestic-relations exception to ERISA's anti-alienation rule.

#### (c) The Harbor Savings account

*Character.* Kira's freelance earnings during the marriage are CP regardless of the account title (§ 760). Deposits since 2019 came from those earnings. Even deposits after the January 2025 DOS would be her SP, but the facts indicate the balance was built during the marriage. **The account is CP.**

*Concealment.* Each spouse owes the other the duty of the highest good faith and fair dealing, including full disclosure of all material facts about community assets, until the property is divided (§§ 721, 1100(e)), and each must disclose all assets and debts in the dissolution (§ 2100 et seq.). Kira hid the account during the marriage and omitted it from her sworn declaration of disclosure. If a spouse conceals or fails to disclose a community asset, the court may award the other spouse **50%** of its value, or **100%** where the concealment involved fraud, oppression or malice (§ 1101(g)–(h)), and may impose sanctions and attorney's fees. Omitted assets remain subject to later division.
- *Kira's argument.* It was "rainy-day money," not a scheme to defraud.
- *Dev's argument.* Kira asked the lawyer to keep the account from him and then signed a disclosure under penalty of perjury that omitted it. That is deliberate concealment, which is fraud.

**Dev is likely entitled to the whole $60,000** under § 1101(h), and in any event to at least half.

### 2. Celeste's ethical violations

California lawyers are governed by the California Rules of Professional Conduct (CRPC) and the State Bar Act (Bus. & Prof. Code § 6000 et seq.). The ABA Model Rules (MR) are not binding in California but are persuasive. Each issue is analyzed under both.

**A. Joint representation of divorcing spouses (CRPC 1.7; MR 1.7)**
*Rule (both).* A lawyer may not represent a client if the representation is directly adverse to another current client, or if there is a significant risk that the lawyer's duties to another client will materially limit the representation. Such a conflict can be cured by consent only if (1) the lawyer reasonably believes she can represent each client competently and diligently, (2) the representation is not prohibited by law, (3) it does **not** involve a claim by one client against another in the same litigation, and (4) each client consents. California requires **informed written consent**; the ABA requires informed consent, **confirmed in writing**.

*Application.* In a dissolution, the spouses are opposing parties, and every dollar of property or support one receives comes from the other. Here the interests are already sharply divergent: Kestrel's valuation, the 401(k) split, and a hidden $60,000 account. Celeste will argue that the divorce was "amicable" and that she would only draft an agreed settlement. But a dissolution is a proceeding before a tribunal in which each spouse's claims against the other are resolved, so representing both involves claims by one client against another in the same proceeding. No lawyer could reasonably believe she could advocate fully for both. Even if a limited joint representation were consentable, Celeste obtained no consent in writing and disclosed none of the risks, such as information sharing, the loss of privilege between the two clients, and possible withdrawal from both. Her promise to be "fair to each" is a mediator's role, not an advocate's.

*Conclusion.* Celeste violated rule 1.7 and MR 1.7.

**B. Conflict from her representation of Kestrel; CRPC 1.7(c)**
*Rule.* A material-limitation conflict exists if the lawyer's duties to another client or her own interests create a significant risk of limiting the representation (CRPC 1.7(b); MR 1.7(a)(2)). California also requires **written disclosure** to a client whenever the lawyer has a professional or business relationship with or responsibility to a party or witness in the same matter, even absent a significant risk (CRPC 1.7(c)). The ABA has no separate written-disclosure rule. When a lawyer represents an organization, the client is the organization acting through its constituents (rule 1.13; MR 1.13).

*Application.* Celeste currently represents Kestrel, a fee-paying client controlled by Dev, and Kestrel's value is the largest asset Kira claims a share of. Her ongoing relationship with Dev's company creates a significant risk that she would pull her punches for Kira on valuation. Dev's request to "keep Kestrel's valuation low" shows the risk is real. Celeste did not even disclose the Kestrel relationship, let alone obtain consent.

*Conclusion.* Celeste violated both rules, and California's rule 1.7(c) written-disclosure duty in addition.

**C. Kira's secret: confidentiality vs. communication**
*Confidentiality.* California lawyers must "maintain inviolate the confidence, and at every peril ... preserve the secrets" of a client (Bus. & Prof. Code § 6068(e)(1); CRPC 1.6). The only exception permits disclosure to prevent a criminal act likely to cause death or substantial bodily harm. The ABA rule (MR 1.6(b)) adds exceptions, including disclosure to prevent, mitigate or rectify substantial financial injury from a client's crime or fraud in which the client used the lawyer's services.

*Communication.* A lawyer must keep each client reasonably informed of significant developments and explain matters enough for informed decisions (CRPC 1.4; § 6068(m); MR 1.4). As Dev's lawyer, Celeste must tell him about a $60,000 community asset that bears directly on his property rights.

*Application.* By promising Kira "that stays between us," Celeste put herself in an impossible position. She cannot tell Dev without breaching her duty of confidentiality to Kira, and she cannot keep the secret without failing her duty of communication to Dev and leaving him to sign off on a division he does not understand. That bind is precisely why the joint representation required clear consent terms on information sharing.
- Under California law, no exception permits disclosure: the harm is financial, not death or bodily harm.
- Under the ABA rule, Kira used Celeste's services (the disclosure declaration) to commit a fraud likely to cause Dev substantial financial injury, so Celeste *may* disclose under MR 1.6(b)(2)–(3) to the extent reasonably necessary.

The secret is also unlikely to remain protected: communications made by joint clients in a common matter are not privileged as between those clients in a later dispute (Evid. Code § 962), so Dev could later reach it.

*Conclusion.* Celeste breached her duty of communication to Dev (both rule sets) by agreeing to hold the secret. In California she now may not disclose it without Kira's consent; under the ABA she may disclose it. Under either, she must withdraw (Part F).

**D. Preparing Kira's false disclosure declaration**
*Rule.* A lawyer may not counsel or assist a client in conduct the lawyer knows is criminal or fraudulent (MR 1.2(d)). California's rule is broader: it also bars assisting conduct that the lawyer knows violates **any law, rule or ruling of a tribunal** (CRPC 1.2.1). A lawyer may not knowingly make a false statement of material fact to a third person (CRPC 4.1; MR 4.1), must employ only means consistent with truth (§ 6068(d)), and commits an act of moral turpitude through dishonesty (§ 6106).

*Application.* Celeste knew the Harbor Savings account was a community asset Kira was hiding, yet she prepared a declaration, signed under penalty of perjury and served on Dev, that omits it. That assists Kira's fraud on Dev and her violation of the Family Code's disclosure statutes. It also makes Celeste a participant in deceiving her other client. Celeste may argue that she only typed what Kira wanted to list. But she knew the omission was false.

*Conclusion.* Violation of CRPC 1.2.1, 4.1 and § 6068(d), and likely § 6106; violation of MR 1.2(d) and 8.4(c).

**E. Fees**
*1. No written fee agreement.* In California, when it is reasonably foreseeable that total expense to the client, including fees, will exceed $1,000, the fee agreement must be in writing, signed by the lawyer and client, and state the basis of compensation and the nature of the services (§ 6148). An agreement that does not comply is voidable at the client's option, leaving the lawyer a reasonable fee. The $12,000 flat fee was oral, and no exception (emergency, corporate client, prior course of dealing, written waiver) applies. *Violation of § 6148.* The ABA requires only that the basis of the fee be communicated, preferably in writing (MR 1.5(b)), so Celeste's oral statement of a flat fee likely satisfies the ABA.

*2. The 10% "bonus."* A lawyer may not make an agreement for, charge or collect a fee in a family-law matter whose payment or amount is contingent on securing a dissolution or on the amount of spousal or child support, "or property settlement in lieu thereof" (CRPC 1.5(c)(1); MR 1.5(d)(1)). Celeste's bonus of 10% of whatever she obtains for Kira "more than half" is contingent on the size of Kira's property award in the dissolution. Celeste might argue that the text reaches property settlements in lieu of support, not an ordinary division of community property. But the rules reflect a policy against fees that give the lawyer a stake in the outcome of a divorce, and a bonus measured by how much one spouse takes from the other falls within that policy. Either way, the bonus gives Celeste a personal financial stake in Kira's beating Dev, her other client, which is itself a material-limitation conflict (CRPC 1.7(b); MR 1.7(a)(2)), and it altered the agreed flat fee through a private, unwritten side deal (§ 6148). *Likely violation of CRPC 1.5(c) and MR 1.5(d), and a clear violation of the conflict rules*, whether or not the bonus is ever paid, because the rules bar making such an agreement.

**F. Withdrawal**
A lawyer must withdraw if continuing the representation will result in a violation of the rules or the State Bar Act (CRPC 1.16(a); MR 1.16(a)). Celeste cannot continue for both spouses without violating the conflict, confidentiality and communication rules, and she cannot help Dev undervalue Kestrel or help Kira hide the account. She must withdraw from representing both spouses. If she has appeared in court, she must obtain the court's permission. On withdrawal she must take reasonable steps to avoid prejudice to each client, release the client files, and refund any unearned part of the $12,000 (CRPC 1.16(d)–(e); MR 1.16(c)–(d)). Because the joint representation was improper from the start, she may also forfeit some or all of the fee.

**Conclusion.** Celeste violated the conflict rules (joint representation; her relationship with Kestrel; her financial stake through the bonus), the duty of communication, the rules against assisting fraud and making false statements, the prohibition on contingent fees in dissolutions and, in California, § 6148 and rule 1.7(c). She must withdraw now.

**Common mistakes**
1. Treating joint representation of divorcing spouses as curable by oral consent. In a dissolution it is likely non-consentable, and California at a minimum requires informed written consent.
2. Stating the ABA's financial-fraud exception as California law. California's only exception is for death or substantial bodily harm.
3. Overlooking that a "bonus" tied to a larger property share is a prohibited contingent fee, and also a conflict.
4. Characterizing the 401(k) as all CP or all SP instead of tracing the premarital balance and excluding post-separation contributions.
5. Forgetting the § 1101(h) 100% remedy for fraudulent concealment, or confusing CP liability rules with the remedy for nondisclosure.
