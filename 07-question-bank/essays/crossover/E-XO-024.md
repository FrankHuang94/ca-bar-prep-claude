## Facts

Crestview Homes, Inc. ("Crestview"), an Arizona corporation with its principal place of business in Phoenix, built the 40-home Vista Ridge subdivision in San Diego in 2021 and 2022. GeoBase LLC ("GeoBase"), a California LLC whose two members are California citizens, tested the soil before construction. Four Vista Ridge homeowners, all California citizens, found cracks spreading through their foundations in 2025. Repair estimates range from $120,000 to $180,000 per home. Crestview's crews poured the slabs in different months, and one homeowner, Sam Brandt, installed a swimming pool in 2023; Crestview contends that the pool, not the soil, caused his cracks.

Attorney Mark Daley agreed to represent all four homeowners under a single engagement letter that says nothing about conflicts. In September 2025 he filed suit for them in the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of California against Crestview and GeoBase, asserting negligence and breach of warranty. He added a count alleging that Crestview "knew the soil was expansive clay and deliberately concealed it from buyers." Mark had no information supporting that allegation. He told a colleague, "A fraud count gets their attention. We'll find something in discovery." The complaint does not identify the fraud allegations as ones likely to have support after discovery.

Helen Park, Crestview's lawyer, served a Rule 11 motion on Mark on October 6, 2025, targeting the fraud count. Mark did not withdraw it. Crestview filed the motion on October 31. GeoBase moved to dismiss the entire action for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction. Crestview also argued that the four plaintiffs were improperly joined because their homes were built at different times by different crews.

In November 2025, Mark telephoned Victor Salas, Crestview's current construction superintendent, who supervised the Vista Ridge slab pours. Mark knew Helen represented Crestview and did not contact her. Victor told Mark, "We were told to pour standard slabs to save money."

The plaintiffs requested all documents concerning soil testing and foundation design for Vista Ridge. Helen signed responses that objected to every request as "overbroad, unduly burdensome, and privileged," without explanation. The night before the plaintiffs' scheduled deposition of Victor, she produced 380,000 pages of unindexed, unsearchable scanned images covering every Crestview project nationwide since 2010. She withheld a 2020 memo from Crestview's outside geotechnical consultant to Crestview's engineering department, which states that "Vista Ridge clay has high expansion potential; post-tensioned slabs recommended." Her response listed the memo in one line as "privileged" and provided no privilege log. The plaintiffs' lawyer conferred with Helen twice without result.

In March 2026, Crestview offered $500,000 to settle all four homeowners' claims "as a package." Mark told the four, "Take it; I'll divide it fairly based on the repair estimates," without telling any of them what the others would receive. Three want to accept. Sam Brandt wants to go to trial.

## Call of the Question

1. How should the court rule on (a) GeoBase's motion to dismiss, (b) Crestview's challenge to the joinder of the four plaintiffs, and (c) Crestview's Rule 11 motion? Discuss.
2. What relief may the plaintiffs obtain regarding Crestview's responses to their document requests? Discuss.
3. What ethical violations, if any, have Mark and Helen committed? Answer according to California and ABA authorities.

## Model Answer

### 1. The motions on jurisdiction, joinder and Rule 11

**A. GeoBase's motion to dismiss.** The claims arise under state law, so jurisdiction depends on diversity: an amount in controversy exceeding $75,000 and **complete** diversity, meaning no plaintiff shares citizenship with any defendant, measured when the action is filed (28 U.S.C. § 1332(a); *Strawbridge*). A corporation is a citizen of its State of incorporation and principal place of business. An LLC takes the citizenship of **every one of its members** (*Carden*).
- Each homeowner claims $120,000 to $180,000, so each exceeds $75,000.
- Crestview is a citizen of Arizona only, so it is diverse from the California plaintiffs.
- GeoBase's two members are Californians, so GeoBase is a California citizen, and complete diversity fails.

Supplemental jurisdiction cannot save the claims against GeoBase, because in a diversity-only case § 1367(b) bars plaintiffs' claims against persons joined under Rule 20, and in any event a nondiverse defendant destroys original jurisdiction over the whole action. But the court need not dismiss the entire case. It may cure the defect by dropping a **dispensable** nondiverse party under Rule 21 (*Newman-Green*). GeoBase is an alleged joint tortfeasor; joint tortfeasors are not required parties, because complete relief can be accorded against Crestview alone. **The court should dismiss GeoBase (without prejudice to suit in state court) and keep the case against Crestview**, rather than dismiss the whole action.

**B. Joinder of the four plaintiffs.** Persons may join as plaintiffs if they assert rights to relief arising out of the same transaction, occurrence or **series** of transactions or occurrences, and any question of law or fact common to all of them will arise (Rule 20(a)(1)). Misjoinder is not a ground for dismissal; the court may sever claims or order separate trials (Rules 20(b), 21, 42(b)).
- *Crestview's argument.* Different pour dates, different crews, and Brandt's pool make each house a separate occurrence.
- *Plaintiffs' response.* All four homes are in one subdivision, built by one builder on soil tested by one consultant, using the same slab design. Whether Vista Ridge's soil was expansive and whether Crestview's standard slab design was negligent are questions common to all four.

The claims arise from a single series of occurrences (the design and construction of Vista Ridge) and share core common questions. **Joinder is proper.** Brandt's pool raises an individual causation issue, which the court can manage with instructions or, if necessary, a separate trial on his damages.

**C. Crestview's Rule 11 motion.** By presenting a pleading, an attorney certifies that, to the best of his knowledge, information and belief formed after an inquiry reasonable under the circumstances, it is not presented for an improper purpose and its factual contentions have evidentiary support or, **if specifically so identified**, will likely have evidentiary support after a reasonable opportunity for discovery (Rule 11(b)(1), (3)).
- *Factual support.* Mark had "no information" that Crestview knew of and concealed expansive clay. The complaint did not flag the fraud allegations as likely to have support after discovery, so the safe-harbor language of Rule 11(b)(3) does not help him. Fraud must also be pleaded with particularity (Rule 9(b)), which underscores that he needed a factual basis before alleging it.
- *Improper purpose.* "A fraud count gets their attention" shows the count was added for leverage, not because Mark believed it was supported.
- *Procedure.* A Rule 11 motion must be made separately, served, and filed only if the paper is not withdrawn or corrected within 21 days after service. Crestview served on October 6 and filed on October 31, 25 days later, after Mark refused to withdraw. The procedure was proper.
- *Later evidence.* The withheld geotechnical memo now suggests that Crestview knew about the clay. But Rule 11 asks whether Mark's inquiry was reasonable when he presented the pleading; a lucky later discovery does not make a baseless allegation reasonable when made. The court may consider the memo in choosing a sanction.
- *Sanction.* A sanction must be limited to what suffices to deter repetition: nonmonetary directives, a penalty paid into court or, on motion, reasonable attorney's fees caused by the violation. The law firm is jointly responsible absent exceptional circumstances (Rule 11(c)). The homeowners did not participate in the decision, so the sanction should fall on Mark and his firm, not on them.

**Conclusion:** grant the motion. A modest sanction, such as striking the fraud count with leave to replead if discovery supports it, plus some or all of Crestview's fees for the motion, is appropriate.

### 2. Relief regarding Crestview's document responses

**A. Boilerplate objections and the document dump.** A responding party must state with specificity the grounds for any objection and whether any responsive materials are being withheld on the basis of that objection (Rule 34(b)(2)(B)–(C)). It must produce documents as they are kept in the usual course of business or organize and label them to correspond to the categories in the request (Rule 34(b)(2)(E)(i)). Discovery must be relevant and proportional (Rule 26(b)(1)).
- Helen's blanket objections ("overbroad, unduly burdensome, and privileged") state no specific grounds and do not say what is being withheld. They are improper.
- Dumping 380,000 unindexed, unsearchable pages covering every project nationwide since 2010, the night before a key deposition, does not comply with Rule 34(b)(2)(E). It buries the responsive Vista Ridge documents among irrelevant material. An evasive or incomplete response is treated as a failure to respond (Rule 37(a)(4)).
- After certifying good-faith conferral (done twice), the plaintiffs may move to compel. The court should order Crestview to identify the Bates ranges responsive to each request (or produce Vista Ridge documents in searchable form organized by request), to withdraw or particularize its objections, and to make Victor available for a second deposition at Crestview's expense. If the motion is granted, the court **must** award reasonable expenses, including fees, unless Crestview's position was substantially justified (Rule 37(a)(5)). It was not.

**B. The withheld geotechnical memo.** A party withholding information as privileged must expressly make the claim and describe the withheld documents in a manner that enables the other parties to assess the claim, typically with a privilege log (Rule 26(b)(5)(A)). Failure to provide an adequate log may result in waiver in the court's discretion.
- *Inadequate claim.* A one-line "privileged" with no log does not satisfy Rule 26(b)(5)(A).
- *Not privileged.* The attorney-client privilege protects confidential communications made to obtain legal advice. The memo is a communication from an engineering consultant to Crestview's engineering department about soil and slab design. No lawyer was involved, and its purpose was technical, not legal.
- *Not work product.* Work product covers documents prepared in anticipation of litigation (Rule 26(b)(3)). A 2020 design recommendation, written years before any dispute, was prepared in the ordinary course of business.
- *Order.* The court should order the memo produced. Given the bare assertion and the obvious lack of any privilege, it may also find the claim waived.
- *Rule 26(g).* By signing the responses, Helen certified that they were consistent with the Rules, not interposed for an improper purpose such as delay or needless expense, and not unreasonable or unduly burdensome. Baseless blanket objections, a burying production and an unsupported privilege claim over a key document violate that certification. Unless the violation was substantially justified, the court **must** impose an appropriate sanction on the signer, the party, or both (Rule 26(g)(3)), such as the fees caused by the violation. If Crestview later disobeys an order compelling production, Rule 37(b)(2) sanctions, such as treating Crestview's knowledge of the clay as established, become available.

### 3. Ethical violations

The California Rules of Professional Conduct and the State Bar Act govern; the ABA Model Rules are persuasive.

**A. Mark: the fraud count.**
- *ABA (MR 3.1):* A lawyer may not assert an issue unless there is a basis in law and fact for doing so that is not frivolous.
- *California (rule 3.1; B&P § 6068(c)):* A lawyer may not bring an action, or assert a position, without probable cause and for the purpose of harassing or maliciously injuring any person, or present a claim not warranted by existing law or a good-faith argument for changing it. Section 6068(c) requires a lawyer to maintain only actions that appear legal or just.
- *Application:* Mark asserted fraud with no factual basis, to "get their attention." That is frivolous under the ABA rule. California's rule 3.1(a)(1) is narrower: it requires both a lack of probable cause and a purpose to harass or maliciously injure. The count lacked probable cause. Crestview will argue that leveling a baseless fraud charge to pressure an opponent is harassment; Mark will say his purpose was to advance his clients' legitimate claims. His admitted leverage purpose makes a violation likely, and § 6068(c) independently forbids maintaining a claim that does not appear legal or just. Violation under the ABA rule; likely violation under California's.

**B. Mark: the call to Victor.**
- *Rule:* In representing a client, a lawyer may not communicate about the subject of the representation with a person the lawyer knows is represented by another lawyer in the matter, without that lawyer's consent (rule 4.2(a); MR 4.2). For a represented organization, California's rule 4.2(b) covers current officers, directors, partners and managing agents, and any current employee or other constituent when the communication concerns that person's own act or omission in the matter that may be binding on or imputed to the organization. Comment [7] to MR 4.2 similarly covers a constituent who supervises, directs or regularly consults with the organization's lawyer about the matter, has authority to obligate the organization, or whose act or omission in the matter may be imputed to it.
- *Application:* Mark knew Helen represented Crestview. Victor is a current employee who supervised the very slab pours at issue, so his conduct may be imputed to Crestview for liability purposes. He is covered under both rules. Mark did not seek Helen's consent. Victor's willingness to talk does not matter, because only Crestview's lawyer can consent.
- *Consequences:* Beyond discipline, the court may bar use of the statement or, in serious cases, disqualify counsel.

Violation under both rules.

**C. Mark: joint representation, the package offer and settlement authority.**
- *Joint representation.* A lawyer may represent multiple clients with potentially conflicting interests only if he reasonably believes he can represent each competently and diligently and each gives informed written consent (rule 1.7; California) or informed consent confirmed in writing (MR 1.7), after disclosure of the risks, such as divergent positions, shared information, and the possibility of withdrawal. Brandt's pool made a conflict reasonably foreseeable from the start: Crestview would blame the pool, and any lump-sum recovery would have to be divided. Mark's engagement letter said nothing about conflicts, so he lacked valid consent under both systems.
- *Aggregate settlement.* A lawyer who represents two or more clients may not participate in an aggregate settlement of their claims unless each client gives informed written consent (CA rule 1.8.7) or informed consent in a writing signed by the client (MR 1.8(g)), after disclosure of the existence and nature of all the claims and of each client's participation in the settlement. Crestview's $500,000 "package" is an aggregate settlement. Mark proposed to divide it himself without telling anyone the others' shares, which violates both rules.
- *Authority to settle.* Whether to settle belongs to each client (rule 1.2(a); MR 1.2(a)). Brandt is entitled to reject the offer. Mark may not pressure him to accept so the others can settle, and he cannot advocate for three clients' acceptance against the fourth's position. If Crestview insists on an all-or-nothing deal, the clients' interests directly conflict over the deal itself, and Mark will likely have to withdraw from representing at least some of them.

Violations of rules 1.7, 1.8.7 and 1.2(a) and the matching Model Rules.

**D. Helen: discovery conduct.**
- *ABA:* A lawyer may not unlawfully obstruct another party's access to evidence or conceal a document having potential evidentiary value (MR 3.4(a)), or fail to make reasonably diligent efforts to comply with a legally proper discovery request (MR 3.4(d)). A lawyer must make reasonable efforts to expedite litigation (MR 3.2) and may not use means that have no substantial purpose other than to burden a third person (MR 4.4(a)).
- *California:* Rule 3.4(a)–(b) forbids unlawfully obstructing access to evidence, concealing documents with evidentiary value, and suppressing evidence that the client has a legal obligation to produce. California's rule has no express discovery-diligence clause. Discovery abuse is addressed through the procedural rules of the forum (here, the Federal Rules, not the Civil Discovery Act), through rule 3.4(f), which forbids knowingly disobeying an obligation under the rules of a tribunal, and through rule 3.2, which forbids means that have no substantial purpose other than delay or needless expense.
- *Application:* Withholding the most important responsive document behind a baseless privilege label is suppression of evidence Crestview was obligated to produce. Dumping 380,000 unindexed pages the night before a deposition served only to burden and delay. Helen may argue that she produced "everything" and asserted privilege in good faith, but a good-faith privilege claim requires some plausible basis and a log; neither existed.

Violations of rules 3.4 and 3.2 (California) and MR 3.4(a), (d) and 3.2/4.4(a) (ABA).

**Common mistakes**
1. Using an LLC's state of organization or principal place of business as its citizenship. An LLC takes the citizenship of each member.
2. Dismissing the entire action instead of dropping the dispensable nondiverse defendant.
3. Applying Rule 11 to discovery responses. Discovery papers are governed by Rules 26(g) and 37 (Rule 11(d)).
4. Assuming a current employee may be interviewed if he agrees. Only the organization's lawyer can consent for a covered constituent.
5. Letting the lawyer allocate a package settlement among joint clients without full disclosure and each client's signed consent.

**Accuracy notes.** The ABA scope of rule 4.2 is stated from current comment [7] to MR 4.2 (supervisors, those who regularly consult with the organization's lawyer, those with authority to obligate the organization, and those whose act or omission may be imputed). R-PRF-073 now states the current ABA scope; the pre-2002 "admission" category, deleted from the comment in 2002, is not used. *Newman-Green* and Rule 21 are cited as in R-CIV-004; the answer does not rely on *Hain Celestial* (V-034), which concerns erroneous dismissal after removal.
