# E-XO-020 — "Whether she likes it or not"

## Facts

Ray Dunn, 34, and Lena Cho, 30, dated for two years but lived apart. On February 20, 2026, Lena ended the relationship and stopped answering Ray's calls.

On March 3, Ray spent the evening at a bar and drank six beers. At 8:52 p.m. he texted his brother Sam: "She won't answer me. Tonight she's coming with me whether she likes it or not." At 9:30 p.m., Lena left her job at a pharmacy. Her coworker Paul saw Ray take Lena by the arm beside Ray's car; Paul could not hear them, but said Lena "looked upset" and got into the passenger seat after Ray opened the door. Ray drove eight miles to his apartment.

In Ray's kitchen, the two argued. At about 10:20 p.m., a downstairs neighbor heard a woman scream, "Let me go!" and knocked on Ray's door. Lena ran out past the neighbor. She had a four-centimeter cut on her left forearm that needed eight stitches. Police found a shattered glass vase on Ray's kitchen floor and a kitchen knife in the sink. Ray told police: "She swung that big vase at my head, so I grabbed a knife to scare her off. She grabbed my arm and got cut. I never forced her to come."

At 11:15 p.m., in the hospital emergency room, Officer Diaz recorded Lena's account on his body camera: "He grabbed me outside work, shoved me into his car and locked the doors so I couldn't get out. At his place I tried to leave and he stood in front of the door. I picked up a vase to make him move, and he grabbed a knife and slashed at me."

Ray was charged in California superior court with kidnapping, false imprisonment, battery and assault with a deadly weapon. At trial, Lena testified for the prosecution that she got into Ray's car voluntarily, that she "could have left anytime," and that she was cut when "we both grabbed the knife." Asked on direct examination about her statement to Officer Diaz, she said she "was upset and exaggerated." The court admitted the following over Ray's objections:

**(a)** The prosecution played Officer Diaz's body-camera recording of Lena's statement. Diaz testified that the recording was a fair and accurate copy of what his camera captured.

**(b)** The prosecution offered Ray's text to Sam. Police found it on Ray's phone, seized and searched under a valid warrant. The phone account is in Ray's name, and Sam testified that he received the message from Ray's number that night.

**(c)** Tess Moran, Ray's girlfriend until 2023, testified that in June 2023, when she told Ray she was leaving him, he choked her until she nearly passed out. She reported it to police, but no charges were filed.

**(d)** In the defense case, Lena's ex-husband, Ken, testified that in 2021, during an argument, Lena threw a heavy ceramic ashtray at his head. In rebuttal, the prosecution called Ray's former coworker, who testified that in 2024 Ray punched him during a parking-lot argument.

## Call of the Question

1. Of what crimes, if any, can Ray be convicted, and what defenses can he raise? Discuss.
2. Applying the California Evidence Code and the United States Constitution, did the court properly admit items (a) through (d)? Discuss.

## Model Answer

### 1. Ray's crimes and defenses

The call does not ask for California law, so general principles apply.

**Kidnapping.** Kidnapping is the unlawful confinement of a person against her will, coupled with moving her or concealing her in a secret place. Consent obtained by force is ineffective. Movement merely incidental to another crime does not suffice unless it substantially increases the risk of harm or reduces the chance of detection.
- *Confinement without consent.* The prosecution will rely on Ray's text ("whether she likes it or not"), Paul's observation that Ray took Lena "by the arm" and she "looked upset," and Lena's recorded statement that he "shoved" her in and "locked the doors so I couldn't get out." Ray will rely on Lena's trial testimony that she went voluntarily, and on Paul's account that she got in after Ray opened the door, with no visible struggle.
- *Movement.* Ray drove her eight miles to his apartment. That movement was not incidental to any other crime; it moved Lena away from her workplace and coworkers to a place where she was alone with him, increasing the danger and decreasing the chance of detection.
- *Mental state.* Kidnapping is a general-intent crime; Ray intended to take her, as his text shows.

If the jury credits the text and the recorded statement over Lena's trial recantation, **Ray is guilty of kidnapping.** If it believes Lena went willingly, he is not.

**False imprisonment.** False imprisonment is unlawfully confining a person without consent; no movement is required. Standing in front of the only door while Lena tried to leave, as she told Diaz, and the neighbor's report of a scream of "Let me go!" support confinement at the apartment. False imprisonment is a lesser included offense of kidnapping; Ray cannot be punished for both arising from the same continuous confinement, but it is the fallback if the jury finds consent to the car ride.

**Battery and assault with a deadly weapon.** Battery is the unlawful application of force resulting in injury or offensive touching; it is satisfied by intentional, reckless or (in many States) criminally negligent conduct. Assault is either an attempted battery or the intentional creation of a reasonable apprehension of imminent bodily harm; it is aggravated when committed with a deadly weapon.
- On Lena's recorded account, Ray "slashed" at her with a knife and cut her: an attempted and completed battery with a deadly weapon.
- On Ray's own account, he "grabbed a knife to scare her off," an intentional attempt to create apprehension with a deadly weapon, which is assault with a deadly weapon; and wielding a knife at close quarters in a struggle is at least reckless as to contact, which supports battery when she was cut.

**Ray's conduct satisfies both crimes on either version, unless self-defense applies.**

**Self-defense.** A person may use non-deadly force when he reasonably believes it necessary to protect himself from imminent unlawful force, and deadly force only against a reasonably feared threat of death or serious bodily injury. An initial aggressor may not claim self-defense unless he withdraws and communicates the withdrawal, or the victim of his non-deadly aggression suddenly escalates to deadly force.
- *Ray's version.* Lena came willingly and could leave; she swung a heavy glass vase at his head, which is deadly force; he grabbed a knife to make her stop. On that version he was not the aggressor, and a knife was proportionate to a blow to the head.
- *The prosecution's version.* Ray seized Lena, drove her away against her will and blocked the door. He was the initial aggressor, and his confinement of her was a continuing unlawful use of force. A person being kidnapped or falsely imprisoned may use reasonable force to escape, and Lena's force was lawful resistance, not "unlawful force" against which Ray could defend. Ray never withdrew: he was still blocking the door. The escalation exception does not help him, because Lena raised the vase to get out of an unlawful confinement, which her right of self-defense allowed.

**The defense turns on which account the jury believes; on the prosecution's evidence it likely fails.**

**Voluntary intoxication.** Voluntary intoxication is a defense only to specific-intent crimes, and only if it prevented the defendant from forming the intent. Kidnapping, false imprisonment, battery and assault with a deadly weapon are general-intent crimes, and Ray's text shows a plan formed while he was drinking. **His six beers are no defense.**

### 2. The evidentiary rulings

**Truth-in-Evidence.** Because this is a California criminal case, Proposition 8 (Cal. Const. art. I, § 28(f)(2)) provides that relevant evidence shall not be excluded except under statutory rules of hearsay and privilege, Evidence Code §§ 352, 782 and 1103, statutes passed after 1982 by a two-thirds vote (§ 1101 survives on that basis, *Ewoldt*), and federal constitutional rules such as the Confrontation Clause. Each item is relevant (§ 210: tending to prove a disputed fact), so each objection must rest on one of those surviving rules.

#### (a) Lena's body-camera statement

*Hearsay.* Hearsay is an out-of-court statement offered to prove the truth of the matter stated (§ 1200). The prosecution offers Lena's recorded account to prove that Ray forced her into the car, blocked the door and slashed at her. It is hearsay, and the hearsay rule survives Proposition 8.

*Prior inconsistent statement (§ 1235).* A witness's prior statement is admissible for its truth if it is inconsistent with her trial testimony and offered in compliance with § 770, which requires that the witness have been given an opportunity to explain or deny it while testifying, or that she not have been excused from giving further testimony. Lena testified that she went voluntarily, "could have left anytime," and was cut when "we both grabbed the knife"; her recorded statement says the opposite on each point. She was asked about the statement on direct and explained that she "exaggerated," so § 770 was satisfied. Her claim that she exaggerated does not make the statement consistent; it is the classic recanting witness that § 1235 addresses. (A prosecutor may impeach his own witness, § 785.)

*Confrontation Clause.* A statement made to police in the hospital an hour after the event, narrating past events, is likely testimonial. But the Confrontation Clause is satisfied when the declarant testifies at trial and is subject to cross-examination about the statement (*California v. Green*; *Crawford*). Lena testified, so no constitutional bar exists.

*Authentication.* A recording is authenticated by evidence sufficient to sustain a finding that it is what it purports to be (§§ 1400–1401). Officer Diaz, who made it, testified that it is a fair and accurate copy. The digital recording is itself an original, so the secondary evidence rule raises no problem.

*§ 352.* The recording is highly probative on the central disputed facts and is not unduly prejudicial; its impact comes from its content, not from any improper emotional appeal.

**Properly admitted for its truth.**

#### (b) Ray's text message

*Authentication.* A writing is authenticated by evidence sufficient to sustain a finding that the proponent's claim is true, including its content and circumstances (§§ 1400, 1410–1421). The message was found on Ray's phone, the account is in his name, Sam received it from Ray's number that night, and its content ("She won't answer me") fits the events. A jury could find that Ray wrote it.

*Hearsay.* Offered against Ray to prove his intent, the text is a statement of a party offered against him, admissible under § 1220. It is also a statement of Ray's then-existing intent or plan (§ 1250), admissible to prove that he later acted on it, and nothing suggests untrustworthiness (§ 1252).

*§ 352.* It is strong evidence of intent and lack of consent; its prejudice is the legitimate kind that comes from probative force. The phone was searched under a valid warrant, so no Fourth Amendment issue arises.

**Properly admitted.**

#### (c) Tess's testimony about the 2023 choking

*Propensity ban.* Evidence of a person's character, including specific instances, is inadmissible to prove conduct on a specified occasion (§ 1101(a)), and § 1101 survives Proposition 8.

*Domestic-violence propensity (§ 1109).* In a criminal action in which the defendant is accused of an offense involving domestic violence, evidence of his other acts of domestic violence is not made inadmissible by § 1101, if it is not inadmissible under § 352. Acts more than ten years old are presumptively inadmissible. The charges here (kidnapping, battery and assault with a deadly weapon against a former dating partner) involve domestic violence, which § 1109(d)(3) defines by reference to Pen. Code § 13700 to include abuse of a person with whom the defendant has had a dating relationship [VERIFY V-993]. Choking Tess, a former girlfriend, is another act of domestic violence, and it occurred in 2023, well within ten years. A conviction is not required.

*§ 1101(b) alternative.* The incident is also relevant to motive and intent: in both episodes Ray responded with violence when a partner tried to leave him. That supports the prosecution's theory that Ray intended to take and hold Lena against her will.

*§ 352.* The court weighs similarity, remoteness, the risk of confusion and of punishing uncharged conduct, and whether the prior act is more inflammatory than the charged one. The episodes are similar (violence at a breakup), recent (three years), and no more inflammatory than a knife attack. Ray will argue that the lack of charges invites a mini-trial and that choking is highly inflammatory; but Tess's testimony is brief and first-hand. The probative value is not substantially outweighed.

*Limiting instruction.* On request, the court must limit the evidence to its proper purpose (§ 355); the standard pattern instruction also tells jurors that the prior act is not sufficient by itself to prove guilt.

**Properly admitted.**

#### (d) Ken's testimony and the prosecution's rebuttal

*Victim's character (§ 1103(a)).* In a criminal action, § 1101 does not bar evidence of the victim's character, in the form of opinion, reputation **or specific instances**, offered by the defendant to prove the victim acted in conformity with it. Ray claims Lena swung the vase first. Ken's testimony that Lena threw a heavy ashtray at his head during an argument is a specific instance of violence offered to show she was the aggressor. California, unlike the Federal Rules, permits proof by specific instances. Ray did not need to know of the incident, because it is offered to prove Lena's conduct, not his fear. Section 1103 is expressly preserved by Proposition 8, and § 352 still applies: the 2021 incident is recent and similar (an object thrown during an argument). **Ken's testimony was properly admitted.**

*Prosecution rebuttal (§ 1103(b)).* Once the defendant offers evidence that the victim has a violent character, the prosecution may offer evidence of the defendant's character for violence, again by opinion, reputation or specific instances, to prove he acted in conformity. Ray opened the door, so the coworker's testimony that Ray punched him in a 2024 parking-lot argument is admissible under § 1103(b).

*§ 352.* Ray will argue that a workplace punch is dissimilar to the charged events and adds prejudice. But it rebuts the specific claim that Lena, not Ray, was the violent one, and it is no more inflammatory than the charged knife assault. A limiting instruction should confine it to the question of who was the aggressor.

**Both the defense evidence and the rebuttal were properly admitted.**

### Summary

Ray is likely guilty of kidnapping (or at least false imprisonment), battery and assault with a deadly weapon unless the jury believes his account of the vase; intoxication is no defense. The court properly admitted items (a) through (d).

**Common mistakes**
1. Treating a recanting witness's prior statement as impeachment only. Under § 1235 it is substantive evidence once § 770 is satisfied.
2. Invoking *Crawford* when the declarant testifies. The Confrontation Clause is satisfied by cross-examination at trial.
3. Forgetting that § 1109 lets the prosecution use other domestic-violence acts for propensity, subject to § 352.
4. Limiting the victim's character to reputation or opinion. California's § 1103(a) allows specific instances, and § 1103(b) then opens the door to the defendant's violence.
5. Letting voluntary intoxication defeat general-intent crimes such as kidnapping and assault.

> 🇨🇳 PRC bridge: in PRC practice a victim's changed testimony is weighed against her earlier statements by the court; in California, § 1235 lets the jury use the earlier statement as substantive evidence, while the defendant's right to cross-examine her is preserved because she testifies (approx.).
