## Facts

Elena Ruiz, a California lawyer, and her friend Marcus Webb, a certified public accountant who is not a lawyer, opened an office in San Diego in January 2025 under the name "Ruiz Webb Startup Counsel." They filed nothing with the Secretary of State. They agreed orally to split all fees, legal and accounting, 50/50, deposited all receipts in one bank account, and shared rent and staff. Their website says: "Ruiz Webb Startup Counsel — your legal and tax partners. Elena Ruiz and Marcus Webb, Partners." Elena does the legal work. Marcus prepares tax filings and meets new clients. When Elena is busy, Marcus reviews clients' draft stock agreements and tells them which terms to accept. Elena knows this and has told him, "Fine, just keep it simple."

In March 2025, Tom Reed and Nina Park hired Ruiz Webb after reading its website. Elena's engagement letter, signed by both founders, identified the clients as "Kestrel Drones, Inc. (to be formed) and its founders, for formation and founder tax matters." Elena incorporated Kestrel Drones, Inc. ("Kestrel"), a California corporation, and prepared stock agreements under which Tom and Nina each received 50% of the shares, subject to vesting. Tom and Nina became Kestrel's only directors; Tom is president and Nina is CEO. Marcus told the founders that he would file their federal tax elections for the restricted stock, which had to be filed within 30 days after the shares were issued. He forgot. Their tax adviser estimates that the missed elections will cost Tom and Nina an extra $180,000 in income tax.

In June 2025, Kestrel raised $500,000 by selling convertible notes to outside investors at terms implying a value of $1.50 per share. Elena bought a $50,000 note on the same terms. She prepared all the documents, and Tom signed for Kestrel. Elena did not suggest that Kestrel consult another lawyer and obtained no written consent from Kestrel other than the signed note.

In January 2026, Tom and Nina began to clash over strategy. Tom met with Elena privately and asked her to (1) prepare a written consent removing Nina as CEO, which Tom then signed alone "as director"; and (2) advise him how Kestrel could issue 2 million new shares to him at $0.01 per share, so that he would control the company. Elena prepared the written consent and a memo to Tom outlining the share issuance. She did not tell Nina. When Nina learned of the written consent and protested, Tom told Elena, "You represent me now, not her." Elena agreed to keep advising Tom.

## Call of the Question

1. What type of business is Ruiz Webb, and who is liable to Tom and Nina for the missed tax elections? Discuss.
2. Was Tom's written consent effective to remove Nina as CEO, and could Kestrel validly issue the new shares to Tom as proposed? Discuss.
3. What ethical violations, if any, has Elena committed? Answer according to California and ABA authorities.

## Model Answer

### 1. Ruiz Webb and liability for the missed elections

**A. What kind of business is Ruiz Webb?** A general partnership is formed by the association of two or more persons to carry on as co-owners a business for profit, whether or not they intend to form a partnership; no filing is required (Corp. Code § 16202; RUPA § 202). A person who receives a share of the business's profits is presumed to be a partner.

Elena and Marcus run one office under one name, share rent and staff, pool all receipts in one account and split all fees 50/50. That is co-ownership of a business for profit, and the profit share triggers the presumption, which no exception (wages, loan interest, rent) rebuts. **Ruiz Webb is a general partnership.**

It is nothing else. An LLC, an LLP or a corporation exists only if a filing is made, and nothing was filed. In any event, California does not allow an LLC to render professional legal services (Corp. Code § 17701.04(e)). A California law firm's limited-liability options are a registered LLP or a law corporation, and both are reserved for lawyers. A registered LLP practicing law could not include a nonlawyer partner under the ethics rules (Part 3).

**B. The partnership's liability.** A partnership is liable for loss caused by a partner's wrongful act or omission in the ordinary course of the partnership's business (§ 16305). Marcus undertook to file the founders' tax elections as part of Ruiz Webb's advertised "tax" services, under an engagement for "founder tax matters," and negligently failed to do so within the 30-day period. His omission occurred in the ordinary course of the firm's business. **Ruiz Webb is liable** for the resulting $180,000 loss. Marcus is also personally liable for his own negligence.

**C. Elena's personal liability.** All partners are liable jointly and severally for all partnership obligations, whether in contract or tort (§ 16306). Elena did nothing wrong in the tax filing, but as Marcus's partner she is personally liable for the firm's obligation to Tom and Nina. The firm never registered as an LLP, so no shield applies; and even a registered LLP partner remains liable for her own misconduct. Under the exhaustion rule, Tom and Nina must first obtain a judgment against the partnership and exhaust partnership assets before levying on Elena's personal assets, unless an exception applies (§ 16307). They may proceed against Marcus's personal assets directly because his liability is independent of the partnership obligation.

**D. Alternative: partnership by estoppel.** Elena may argue that a partnership between a lawyer and a nonlawyer to practice law is unlawful and therefore no true partnership. Even if a court agreed, a person who represents herself, or consents to being represented, as another's partner is liable to a third party who relies on the representation in dealing with them; a public representation reaches anyone who relies on it (§ 16308). The website called Elena and Marcus "Partners," and Tom and Nina hired Ruiz Webb after reading it. **Elena would be liable as a purported partner** in any event.

**Conclusion:** the partnership, Marcus and Elena are all liable to Tom and Nina, subject to the exhaustion rule as to Elena.

### 2. Tom's written consent and the proposed share issuance

**A. Removal of Nina as CEO.** A corporation's business is managed by or under the direction of its board, which acts only collectively: at a properly noticed meeting with a quorum, by majority vote of the directors present, or by **unanimous** written consent without a meeting (Corp. Code § 307; MBCA § 8.21). Officers are chosen and removed by the board. Kestrel has two directors. A consent signed by Tom alone is not unanimous, and at a meeting Tom could not muster a majority over Nina's vote. **The consent is ineffective; Nina remains CEO.** The two-director deadlock is a governance problem to be resolved by the shareholders or, if necessary, a court, not by one director acting alone.

**B. Issuing 2 million shares to Tom at $0.01.** A director has a duty of loyalty, and a transaction between the corporation and one of its directors is an interested-director transaction. It is protected only if, after full disclosure of the material facts, it is approved by disinterested directors or disinterested shareholders, or if it is fair to the corporation (MBCA §§ 8.60–8.63). In California, § 310 requires disclosure and approval by disinterested shareholders (with the interested director's shares not counted) or by a sufficient disinterested-board vote **plus** a finding that the transaction was just and reasonable, or proof that it was just and reasonable when approved.

Tom would be on both sides of the deal. The only disinterested director and shareholder is Nina, who opposes it. Fairness is not plausible: shares the investors valued at about $1.50 would go to Tom at $0.01, purely to shift control. **Kestrel could not validly issue the shares as proposed**, and a director who caused it would breach the duty of loyalty, exposing the issuance to rescission.

### 3. Elena's ethical violations

A California lawyer is governed by the California Rules of Professional Conduct and the State Bar Act; the ABA Model Rules are persuasive. Each issue below states the ABA rule, then California's.

**A. Partnership with a nonlawyer; sharing legal fees.**
- *ABA:* A lawyer may not share legal fees with a nonlawyer, may not form a partnership with a nonlawyer if any of its activities consist of the practice of law, and may not practice in a for-profit organization in which a nonlawyer owns an interest (MR 5.4(a), (b), (d)).
- *California:* Rule 5.4 is substantively the same. The exceptions (a deceased lawyer's estate, retirement plans for nonlawyer employees, court-awarded fees shared with certain nonprofits, and in California a certified lawyer-referral service) do not apply.
- *Application:* Ruiz Webb's activities include Elena's practice of law, and Marcus, a nonlawyer, is a co-owner. Their 50/50 split of "all fees, legal and accounting," shares legal fees with a nonlawyer. Elena may argue that this is a multidisciplinary firm offering tax services, but the rules forbid exactly this combination. The concern is that a nonlawyer owner can influence the lawyer's independent judgment.

**Conclusion:** Elena violated rule 5.4 and MR 5.4 under both systems.

**B. Assisting the unauthorized practice of law; supervising Marcus.**
- *Rule:* No one may practice law in California unless an active licensee of the State Bar (B&P §§ 6125–6126). A lawyer may not knowingly assist another in the unauthorized practice of law (rule 5.5(a); MR 5.5(a)). A lawyer with managerial authority over nonlawyers must make reasonable efforts to ensure their conduct is compatible with the lawyer's obligations, and is responsible for conduct she ratifies (rule 5.3; MR 5.3).
- *Application:* Telling clients which terms of their stock agreements to accept is legal advice about their legal rights. Marcus did it, and Elena knew and approved ("Fine, just keep it simple"). Elena is a partner of the firm with managerial authority over Marcus's work in the office.

**Conclusion:** Elena knowingly assisted UPL and failed to supervise Marcus, under both systems.

**C. Misleading website and firm name.**
- *Rule:* A lawyer may not make a false or misleading communication about her services (rule 7.1; MR 7.1). In California, rule 7.5 separately bars a firm name that is misleading or implies a relationship that does not lawfully exist. The ABA folds firm names into MR 7.1.
- *Application:* "Ruiz Webb Startup Counsel — your legal and tax partners," naming both as "Partners," implies that Marcus is counsel and that the firm is a lawful law partnership offering legal advice through both principals. Neither is true. The communication is materially misleading under both rules.

**D. Elena's $50,000 convertible note: business transaction with a client.**
- *Rule (ABA and California):* A lawyer may not enter into a business transaction with a client unless (1) the terms are fair and reasonable and fully disclosed in writing in a manner the client can understand; (2) the client is advised in writing to seek independent counsel and given a reasonable opportunity to do so; and (3) the client consents. California requires informed written consent (rule 1.8.1); the ABA requires informed consent in a writing signed by the client to the essential terms and to the lawyer's role, including whether she represents the client in the transaction (MR 1.8(a)).
- *Application:* Lending to Kestrel through a convertible note is a business transaction with a current client, and Elena prepared all the documents. Her terms matched the outside investors', so they were likely fair, and the note itself discloses its terms. But she gave no written advice to consult independent counsel, and Kestrel gave no consent addressing her dual role as lender and counsel. A signed note is not that consent.
- *Personal-interest conflict.* As a noteholder, Elena has her own stake in Kestrel's future financings and capitalization, for example in any issuance that dilutes the notes. A significant risk that those interests will limit her advice requires informed written consent under rule 1.7(b) (informed consent confirmed in writing under MR 1.7).

**Conclusion:** Elena violated rule 1.8.1 and MR 1.8(a), and likely rule 1.7 / MR 1.7.

**E. Organization as client; conflicts with Kestrel and Nina.**
- *Rules.* A lawyer retained by an organization represents the organization acting through its authorized constituents (rule 1.13(a); MR 1.13(a)). If she knows that a constituent intends to act in violation of a legal obligation to the organization in a way likely to cause it substantial injury, she must proceed in the organization's best interest, ordinarily by reporting up to the highest authority that can act for it (rule 1.13(b); MR 1.13(b)). California, unlike the ABA, forbids reporting out confidential information (rule 1.13(c); B&P § 6068(e)), but reporting up within the organization is required under both. When the organization's interests are adverse to those of the constituent she is dealing with, the lawyer must explain whom she represents (MR 1.13(f)). A lawyer may not represent a client in a matter directly adverse to another current client without each client's informed written consent (rule 1.7; MR 1.7), or against a former client in a substantially related matter without the former client's consent (rule 1.9; MR 1.9).
- *Kestrel.* Elena's client is Kestrel. Tom's plan would breach his duty of loyalty to Kestrel and injure it by issuing shares worth about $1.50 for $0.01, and his "written consent" was an invalid attempt by one director to act for the board. Elena knew both. Instead of reporting the plan to Kestrel's highest authority, its board (which includes Nina), she prepared the invalid consent and a memo to implement the scheme, and concealed it. That violates rule 1.13(b) and MR 1.13(b). Helping Tom carry out an invalid act and a breach of fiduciary duty also risks counseling or assisting a violation of law (rule 1.2.1; MR 1.2(d) if the conduct is fraudulent).
- *Nina.* Nina was Elena's client: the engagement letter covered "founder" matters, and Elena drafted Nina's stock agreement. If the joint representation continues, Nina is a current client, and representing Tom in a control fight against her is direct adversity requiring both founders' informed written consent. That consent was never sought, and the conflict is likely nonconsentable once the two are in open dispute. If Nina is treated as a former client, the control fight over the very shares Elena documented is substantially related, and Nina has not consented. Elena also may not use information Nina shared as a joint client to her disadvantage.
- *Tom.* Representing Tom personally against Kestrel's interests is a further conflict. Consent on Kestrel's behalf would have to come from someone other than Tom, and none was obtained. "You represent me now, not her" does not cure any of this.

**Conclusion:** Elena violated rules 1.13, 1.7 and 1.9 (and the matching Model Rules). She must stop advising Tom against Kestrel and Nina, and will likely have to withdraw from representing Tom and possibly from representing Kestrel as well.

**Common mistakes**
1. Saying Elena is not liable for Marcus's negligence because she did not commit it. A general partner is jointly and severally liable for partnership obligations.
2. Treating the firm as an LLC or LLP without a filing, or forgetting that California bars LLCs from practicing law.
3. Missing rule 5.4 because Marcus does "only" tax work. Any partnership with a nonlawyer that includes the practice of law is forbidden, as is sharing legal fees.
4. Accepting a signed promissory note as the client's informed written consent to a business transaction with its lawyer.
5. Treating Tom's instruction ("represent me, not her") as settling who the client is. The organization is the client, and Nina's status as a joint or former client independently bars adversity.
