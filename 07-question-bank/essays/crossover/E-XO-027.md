## Facts

Marlow Creamery, Inc. ("Marlow"), a California corporation based in Sonoma, makes artisanal ice cream. For ten years its best-known flavor has been made with "Silver Lady" peaches, an heirloom variety grown commercially only by Hollis Orchards LLC ("Hollis"), an Oregon LLC whose two members are Oregon citizens. The Silver Lady flavor accounts for about 40% of Marlow's $3 million in annual sales. In blind taste tests, Marlow's customers consistently distinguish it from versions made with other peaches, which are widely available at about $2.80 a pound.

In January 2023, Hollis agreed in a signed writing to sell Marlow its entire Silver Lady crop for each of the five harvests from 2023 through 2027, at $3 a pound, payable within 30 days of delivery. The contract states that California law governs. Hollis delivered about 40,000 pounds in each of 2023 and 2024. In 2024 Marlow paid two invoices ten days late; Hollis accepted the payments without objection.

In June 2025, a national grocery chain offered Hollis $5.50 a pound for its Silver Lady crop. On June 15, Hollis wrote to Marlow: "Because of your late payments, our contract is over. We are selling the 2025 and later crops to another buyer." The 2025 harvest was to begin July 20. Silver Lady peaches ripen and spoil within about three weeks of harvest.

On June 22, Marlow sued Hollis for breach of contract in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California, invoking diversity jurisdiction. (Assume that personal jurisdiction and venue are proper.) On notice to Hollis, Marlow moved for a preliminary injunction (a) barring Hollis from selling Silver Lady peaches to anyone else and (b) requiring Hollis to deliver the 2025 crop to Marlow as harvested, at the contract price. Marlow argued that the court should apply California's preliminary-injunction standard because California law governs the contract, and that, as in California practice, the security should be minimal. Hollis argued that the federal standard applies and asked for a $250,000 bond, pointing to the $100,000 premium it would lose on the 2025 crop alone.

After a bench trial in March 2026, the court found that Hollis had breached the contract. Two harvests, 2026 and 2027, remain. Marlow asks the court to order Hollis to sell it those crops at the contract price. Hollis asks the court to award damages instead, arguing that Marlow can use other peaches, that supervising two more harvests would burden the court, and that Marlow's late payments bar equitable relief.

## Call of the Question

1. What standard should the federal court apply to Marlow's motion for a preliminary injunction, should it grant the motion, and what security should it require? Discuss.
2. After trial, should the court order Hollis to sell Marlow the 2026 and 2027 crops, or award Marlow damages instead? Discuss.

## Model Answer

### 1. The preliminary injunction

**A. Which standard? (Erie).** A federal court hearing a state-law claim in diversity applies state substantive law and federal procedural law (*Erie*; 28 U.S.C. § 1652). If a valid Federal Rule is broad enough to answer the question, it controls (*Hanna*). If no Rule answers it, the court asks whether disregarding the state rule would be outcome-determinative in a way that encourages forum shopping or the inequitable administration of the laws, and whether countervailing federal interests favor the federal practice (*Guaranty Trust*; *Byrd*).
- *The competing standards.* Under the federal standard, the movant must show that it is likely to succeed on the merits, likely to suffer irreparable harm without relief, that the balance of equities tips in its favor, and that an injunction is in the public interest (*Winter*). California courts weigh two interrelated factors on a sliding scale: the likelihood that the plaintiff will prevail and the relative interim harm to the parties (*IT Corp. v. County of Imperial* (Cal. 1983)).
- *Is there a Federal Rule on point?* Rule 65 governs notice, procedure, security and the form of injunctions, but it does not state the standard for granting one. So the analysis turns on the unguided-Erie factors.
- *Marlow's argument.* California law governs the contract, and a more lenient California standard could change the outcome, inviting forum shopping into whichever court applies the friendlier test.
- *Hollis's argument.* The standard for interim equitable relief is part of how a federal court exercises its own equitable powers, a matter of judicial procedure rather than of the parties' substantive rights. Most federal courts, including the Ninth Circuit, apply the federal standard to preliminary injunctions in diversity cases.
- *Resolution.* The court should apply the *Winter* factors, while using **California substantive law** to judge the likelihood of success on the contract claim. The difference matters little here, because Marlow satisfies either test.

**B. Likelihood of success.** Under California law (Cal. Com. Code, Article 2), a contract to sell a seller's entire output is definite and enforceable; output means the actual output produced in good faith (UCC § 2-306). Hollis's June 15 letter, refusing all further deliveries before the 2025 harvest, is an unequivocal anticipatory repudiation (§ 2-610). Hollis's only excuse is Marlow's two payments, each ten days late, in 2024. This is an installment contract, and a default as to one or more installments ends the whole contract only if it substantially impairs the value of the whole contract (§ 2-612(3)). Two payments ten days late impair nothing, and Hollis accepted the late payments without objection, waiving any right to treat them as grounds for termination. Hollis's real motive, a $5.50 offer, is no excuse. **Marlow is likely to succeed.**

**C. Likely irreparable harm.** Harm is irreparable when money cannot make the plaintiff whole: unique goods, losses too speculative to measure, or the loss of a business's goodwill. *Winter* requires that irreparable harm be likely, not merely possible.
- Silver Lady peaches are grown commercially only by Hollis, and customers can tell the difference. Marlow cannot buy a substitute.
- The flavor is 40% of Marlow's sales and has been its signature for ten years. Losing it for a season risks lost customers and goodwill that cannot be measured with confidence.
- The peaches spoil within three weeks of harvest. Once sold to the chain and consumed, they cannot be recovered.
- Hollis's argument that Marlow can "use other peaches" ignores that Marlow would then be making a different product.

**Irreparable harm is likely.**

**D. Balance of equities and public interest.** Hollis would lose about $100,000 in premium on the 2025 crop, but that is a measurable loss that the bond can cover, and it is the value of a bargain Hollis already gave up by contract. Marlow risks losing its signature product and the customers who buy it. The public interest favors enforcing contracts; the chain and its shoppers have no superior claim to peaches already committed to Marlow. **The balance favors Marlow.**

**E. Status quo; mandatory versus prohibitory relief.** A preliminary injunction usually preserves the status quo ante litem, meaning the last actual, peaceable, uncontested state of affairs before the dispute. Mandatory interim relief, requiring affirmative action, is disfavored, and many courts require a clear showing. Marlow's request (a), barring sales to others, is prohibitory. Request (b), ordering delivery, is mandatory in form. But the last peaceable status was two years of Hollis delivering its whole crop to Marlow, so an order to keep doing so **restores** the status quo rather than changing it. Given Marlow's strong showing, the court may grant both parts, specifying the terms in reasonable detail, including that Marlow pays the contract price within 30 days of each delivery (Rule 65(d)).

**F. Security.** The court may issue a preliminary injunction only if the movant gives security in an amount the court considers proper to pay the costs and damages of a party found to have been wrongfully enjoined (Rule 65(c)). California requires an undertaking under CCP § 529. Rule 65(c) directly addresses security, is valid as a rule of procedure, and therefore controls in federal court (*Hanna*); California practice does not decide the amount. Hollis's realistic loss if wrongfully enjoined is the premium on the 2025 crop, about $100,000 ($2.50 × 40,000 pounds). Hollis's $250,000 request is not tied to any shown loss, and Marlow's request for minimal security ignores a real risk. A wrongfully enjoined defendant's recovery is generally limited to the bond, so the court should set it at about **$100,000**.

**Conclusion:** grant the preliminary injunction, both parts, on a bond of about $100,000.

### 2. The permanent remedy after trial

**A. Erie and the remedy.** Whether Marlow is entitled to specific performance or only to damages is defined by the state law that creates its contract right: California's Commercial Code provides the damages measures (§§ 2712–2713, 2715) and permits specific performance "where the goods are unique or in other proper circumstances" (§ 2716). Displacing those rules would change outcomes and invite forum shopping, so the federal court applies them. Federal courts generally also apply federal equitable principles in deciding whether to issue an injunctive decree, which in federal court means the four-factor *eBay* test: irreparable injury, inadequacy of legal remedies, balance of hardships, and the public interest. Courts are not uniform about how the two fit together, but here the California test and the federal test lead to the same answer.

**B. The damages alternative.** A buyer whose seller fails to deliver may recover the cost of a reasonable substitute purchase (cover) minus the contract price, or the market price minus the contract price, plus incidental and consequential damages (§§ 2-712, 2-713, 2-715). Lost profits must be proven with reasonable certainty, though the amount may be a reasonable estimate (R2d § 352).
- *Market measure.* For each remaining harvest, damages would be about ($5.50 − $3.00) × 40,000 pounds, or $100,000, if the chain's price is the market price. But there is no real market in which Marlow can buy Silver Lady peaches, so this measure does not let Marlow make its product.
- *Cover.* There is no substitute: other peaches make a different flavor.
- *Consequential lost profits.* Marlow could claim lost profits on the Silver Lady flavor. As an established business with sales history, it could show the fact of loss, but quantifying how many customers it would lose permanently, and for how long, would be highly speculative.

Damages are therefore an **inadequate** remedy, which supports equitable relief.

**C. Specific performance or a permanent injunction.** A court may order specific performance of a sale of goods when the goods are unique or in other proper circumstances, such as when the buyer cannot cover, and output contracts with no alternative source are a classic example (§ 2-716). The plaintiff must show a valid contract with certain terms, that it has satisfied or is ready, willing and able to satisfy its own conditions, an inadequate legal remedy, feasibility of enforcement, and no equitable defense. A permanent injunction requires the same inadequacy, a protectable interest, feasibility, and a favorable balance of hardships.
- *Contract and conditions.* The court found a valid contract with definite terms (entire Silver Lady crop, $3 a pound, through 2027). Marlow has paid for every delivery and is ready, willing and able to pay for the remaining harvests.
- *Uniqueness / other proper circumstances.* Hollis is the only commercial source; the peaches cannot be replaced; damages are inadequate (Part B).
- *Feasibility.* Hollis argues that two more harvests of deliveries would require continuing supervision. But the obligation is simple and objective: deliver the entire Silver Lady crop as harvested and accept payment at $3 a pound. It involves no matter of taste or skill, and it ends in 2027. The court can also issue a negative injunction barring Hollis from selling Silver Lady peaches to anyone else, which is easy to enforce by contempt and gives Hollis every incentive to sell to Marlow.
- *Balancing.* Hollis would lose a premium of about $100,000 a year, but that is simply the value of the bargain it made; hardship that results from a contract price the defendant agreed to carries little weight, especially when the defendant breached deliberately to get a better price. Marlow's hardship without relief is the loss of its signature product. The public interest favors enforcing contracts.

**D. Equitable defenses.** Hollis argues that Marlow's late payments bar equitable relief.
- *Unclean hands* requires inequitable conduct directly related to the matter in litigation. Two payments made ten days late, a year before the dispute, without bad faith, are ordinary minor breaches, not inequitable conduct.
- *Waiver.* Hollis accepted the late payments without objection, intentionally relinquishing any right to treat them as grounds for termination.
- *Laches* does not apply: Marlow sued a week after the repudiation.

**Conclusion:** the court should grant specific performance requiring Hollis to sell Marlow the 2026 and 2027 Silver Lady crops at the contract price, together with a permanent injunction barring sales to others. Damages for the 2025 crop, if not already delivered under the preliminary injunction, remain available.

**Common mistakes**
1. Assuming that because California law governs the contract, California procedure governs the injunction. The standard and the security in federal court are federal.
2. Treating the delivery order as a disfavored mandatory injunction without asking what the last peaceable status was.
3. Setting security by reference to CCP § 529 or ignoring it. Rule 65(c) requires security in an amount the court considers proper.
4. Calling any supply of agricultural goods fungible. A single-source heirloom variety with no substitute is unique or presents "other proper circumstances."
5. Giving weight to the defendant's lost premium. Hardship that consists of the bargain the defendant made carries little weight.

**Accuracy notes.** The statement that most federal courts, including the Ninth Circuit, apply the federal preliminary-injunction standard in diversity cases is not in the repo's rule statements (R-CIV-040 does not list it); it reflects widely followed federal practice and is stated as the majority approach. The interplay between state remedial statutes and federal equitable standards for permanent relief is not uniform across courts; the answer avoids relying on either side by showing that both tests produce the same result. Civ. Code §§ 3390–3391 (V-108) are not relied on.
