# E-XO-003 — Bluebell Hollow lavender farm

## Facts

Walt Harmon owned Bluebell Hollow, a 12-acre lavender farm with a farmhouse and a barn. On March 3, 2026, Walt and Ines Duarte signed a two-page "Purchase Agreement" prepared by Walt's real estate agent. It described the property as "Bluebell Hollow, 4410 Ridge Road, approximately 12 acres, including farmhouse, barn and all lavender plantings," set the price at $900,000, and called for closing on June 15, 2026. Ines deposited $45,000 in escrow as earnest money. Paragraph 6 stated: "Buyer's obligation is contingent on Buyer obtaining a written loan commitment for at least $700,000 by May 15, 2026. This contingency is solely for Buyer's benefit." Paragraph 9 stated: "Time is of the essence." The agreement did not include a legal description from the county records. A licensed appraiser valued the farm at $900,000 in March.

Ines, a pastry chef, told Walt during negotiations that she planned to live in the farmhouse and open "Hollow Lavender Co.," making lavender syrups and soaps and hosting farm tours. In April she paid $22,000 for a copper essential-oil still custom-built to fit the barn, and $3,000 for permits and a business plan. A dealer has offered to buy the still for $9,000.

On May 10, Ines's bank told her its loan commitment would be delayed until May 25 because of an appraisal backlog. On May 12, Ines emailed Walt: "I hereby waive the financing contingency in paragraph 6. I will close on June 15 regardless; my parents have committed in writing to lend me whatever the bank does not." She attached her parents' signed commitment letter for $700,000. Walt did not reply.

Meanwhile, in early May the county rezoned land along Ridge Road to allow small-lot residential subdivisions. On May 20, Crestline Homes, a developer, offered Walt $1,050,000 for Bluebell Hollow. Walt's agent told Crestline about the contract with Ines. Crestline replied that its lawyers believed the contract was "dead" because Ines had not obtained a loan commitment by May 15. On May 22, Walt wrote to Ines: "Because you did not obtain a loan commitment by May 15, our agreement is terminated. Your deposit will be returned." The next day Walt signed a contract to sell Bluebell Hollow to Crestline, with closing set for July 1. Crestline plans to remove the lavender fields and farmhouse and build 30 homes.

On May 25, Ines's bank issued a $700,000 loan commitment. On May 26, she wrote to Walt that she remained ready to close on June 15 and demanded that he perform. An appraiser now values Bluebell Hollow at $1,000,000 as of May 22.

On June 1, Ines sued Walt in state court, seeking a temporary restraining order and a preliminary injunction against the conveyance to Crestline, specific performance and, alternatively, damages. Walt argues that the contract is unenforceable because it lacks a legal description; that Ines's failure to satisfy the financing contingency freed him; that money will fully compensate Ines; and that forcing him to sell for $900,000 a farm now worth more would be unfair.

## Call of the Question

1. Did Walt breach the contract? Discuss.
2. Can Ines obtain (a) a temporary restraining order and a preliminary injunction preventing Walt from conveying Bluebell Hollow to Crestline, and (b) specific performance? Discuss.
3. If Ines does not obtain specific performance, what other remedies may she recover? Discuss.

## Model Answer

### 1. Did Walt breach the contract?

**Governing law.** A contract to sell land is governed by the common law, not the UCC.

**Statute of frauds.** A contract for the sale of an interest in land must be evidenced by a writing signed by the party to be charged that reasonably identifies the property, shows that a contract was made and states the essential terms with reasonable certainty. Walt signed the Purchase Agreement, which names the parties, the price ($900,000), the closing date and the property. Walt argues that the lack of a legal description is fatal. But the writing need only identify the land with reasonable certainty, not in surveyor's language. "Bluebell Hollow, 4410 Ridge Road, approximately 12 acres, including farmhouse, barn and all lavender plantings" identifies one specific parcel by name and street address, and county records can supply the legal description to clarify, not invent, the term. **The writing satisfies the statute of frauds.**

**The financing contingency.** An express condition, created by language such as "contingent on," must be strictly satisfied before the conditioned duty becomes due unless it is excused. Paragraph 6 made *Buyer's* obligation contingent on a loan commitment by May 15. It did not occur by that date; the commitment came on May 25.

But the non-occurrence of a condition is excused if the party it protects waives it. A party may waive a condition that exists solely for its own benefit, and a waiver made before the condition's deadline needs no consideration unless the condition was a material part of the other side's agreed exchange (R2d § 84). Paragraph 6 states that the contingency is "solely for Buyer's benefit." It protected Ines from being bound to pay without financing; it did not protect Walt, whose bargained-for performance, $900,000 at closing, was unchanged. On May 12, before the deadline, Ines unequivocally waived it in writing and showed alternative funding.

Walt will argue that the time-of-the-essence clause made May 15 a hard deadline, and that the contingency also assured him that Ines could close. The clause's own words ("solely for Buyer's benefit") defeat the second argument, and Ines acted before May 15, so the time clause was respected. **The condition was effectively waived, and Ines's duty to buy remained in force; Walt was still bound.**

**Anticipatory repudiation.** A clear and unequivocal statement or voluntary act, before performance is due, showing that a party will not perform is an anticipatory repudiation. The non-repudiating party may treat it as a total breach and sue immediately, or demand retraction. Walt's May 22 letter ("our agreement is terminated") and his May 23 contract to sell the same farm to Crestline are an unequivocal statement and an act making his performance impossible, three weeks before the June 15 closing. Ines's May 26 demand that he perform did not waive her rights, and she was entitled to sue on June 1 without first tendering on June 15. **Walt repudiated and committed a total breach.**

### 2. Interim relief and specific performance

#### (a) Protecting Bluebell Hollow pending trial

**Lis pendens and Crestline's status.** A buyer's right to specific performance is cut off if the seller conveys to a bona fide purchaser for value without notice. Ines should immediately record a notice of pendency of action (lis pendens), which gives constructive notice to anyone acquiring an interest later. Crestline already has **actual notice**: Walt's agent told it about Ines's contract, and its lawyers' opinion that the contract was "dead" does not make it a good-faith purchaser. Crestline would therefore take subject to Ines's rights even if Walt conveyed. But Crestline's plan to remove the lavender and the farmhouse creates a risk of physical destruction that notice alone cannot prevent.

**TRO and preliminary injunction.** A preliminary injunction preserves the status quo pending trial. Under the general test, the plaintiff must show (1) likely success on the merits, (2) likely irreparable harm without relief, (3) a balance of hardships in her favor and (4) consistency with the public interest (*Winter*). California courts weigh the likelihood of success and the relative interim harm on a sliding scale (*IT Corp. v. County of Imperial*). A TRO may issue on a shorter showing, even without notice, where irreparable harm will occur before a noticed hearing can be held; the court will require a bond.

- *Likelihood of success.* Strong, for the reasons in call 1.
- *Irreparable harm.* Land is unique. If Walt conveys and Crestline begins clearing the lavender fields and demolishing the farmhouse, the specific farm Ines contracted to live on and work cannot be restored by money or by a later decree.
- *Balance of hardships.* An injunction only delays Walt's sale; Crestline contracted knowing of Ines's claim. Without one, Ines may lose the property permanently.
- *Public interest.* Enforcing land contracts and preventing a party from profiting by repudiation serve the public interest.
- *TRO timing.* Crestline's closing is set for July 1, a month after suit, so a noticed preliminary-injunction hearing can likely be held first. A TRO is appropriate only if Walt tries to close earlier or Crestline starts work.

**A preliminary injunction against conveyance and alteration is likely**, conditioned on Ines posting a bond to cover Walt's damages if the injunction proves wrongful.

#### (b) Specific performance

Specific performance requires (1) a valid contract with definite terms, (2) the plaintiff's conditions satisfied or the plaintiff ready, willing and able to perform, (3) an inadequate legal remedy, (4) feasibility and (5) no equitable defense.

**Definite contract.** The parties, property, price, deposit and closing date are all stated. **Satisfied.**

**Ready, willing and able.** Ines waived the financing contingency, holds a $700,000 bank commitment plus her parents' written $700,000 commitment, has $45,000 in escrow and has demanded performance. Walt's repudiation excuses her from formally tendering on June 15. **Satisfied.**

**Inadequate legal remedy.** Every parcel of land is considered unique, so damages are presumed inadequate when a seller refuses to convey. Here the uniqueness is concrete: Ines planned to live in the farmhouse and build a lavender business around these fields and this barn, for which she had a still custom-built. Walt argues that money will make her whole because she can buy another farm. But no other property has these plantings and buildings. In California, Civil Code § 3387 presumes that money cannot adequately remedy a breach of an agreement to transfer real property; the presumption is conclusive for a single-family dwelling the buyer intends to occupy, which arguably includes the farmhouse Ines planned to live in, and otherwise rebuttable. **Satisfied.**

**Feasibility and mutuality.** A decree ordering Walt to deliver a deed against payment of the price is simple to supervise and enforce by contempt. Mutuality of remedy is not required where, as here, the plaintiff's own performance (payment) can be made a condition of the decree. **Satisfied.**

**Equitable defenses.**

- *Inadequate consideration or unfairness.* Specific performance may be refused if the exchange was grossly unfair, and in California it may not be enforced against a party who did not receive adequate consideration or for whom the contract is not just and reasonable (Civ. Code § 3391). But fairness is measured when the contract was made. In March the price equaled the appraised value ($900,000). The later rise to $1,000,000 came from a rezoning neither party controlled. A rise in value is a risk the seller assumes. **This defense fails.**
- *Laches.* Ines sued ten days after the repudiation and protested within four days. **No unreasonable delay.**
- *Unclean hands.* Ines acted openly and in good faith, waiving the contingency in writing before the deadline. **No misconduct.**
- *Bona fide purchaser.* Crestline has actual notice, as discussed. **No bar.**

**Ines is likely entitled to specific performance**, with the decree conditioned on her payment of the $900,000 price (credited with the $45,000 deposit) and Ines should join Crestline as a defendant so that the decree binds it as a purchaser with notice.

### 3. Remedies if specific performance is denied

**Expectation damages.** The injured party recovers expectation damages that put her where she would have been had the contract been performed. When a seller wrongfully refuses to convey, the buyer recovers the market value at the time of breach minus the contract price, plus return of payments and foreseeable consequential and incidental losses. Market value when Walt repudiated (May 22) was $1,000,000, so the benefit of the bargain is $1,000,000 − $900,000 = **$100,000**, plus the return of her **$45,000 deposit**. (Walt's $1,050,000 deal with Crestline is evidence of value but is not the measure.) In California, Civil Code § 3306 states the buyer's measure by statute: the price paid, title and document expenses, the difference between the contract price and the value at breach, expenses properly incurred in preparing to enter on the land, consequential damages according to proof, and interest, with no good-faith limitation. Walt acted in bad faith by selling to a higher bidder, so even the minority rule limiting a good-faith seller to the buyer's deposit and expenses would not apply.

**Consequential damages: lost business profits.** Consequential losses must be foreseeable at contracting and proved with reasonable certainty. Walt knew of Ines's plans, so lost profits from Hollow Lavender Co. were foreseeable. But the business never opened, Ines is a pastry chef with no lavender-business history, and profits from syrups, soaps and tours are speculative. **Lost profits are likely not recoverable.**

**Reliance alternative.** Where expectation is uncertain, the injured party may instead recover reliance damages: expenditures made in preparing to perform, less any loss the breaching party proves she would have suffered anyway. Ines spent $22,000 on the custom still and $3,000 on permits and a plan in reasonable reliance on the contract. She must mitigate by reselling the still for $9,000, so her net reliance loss is about $16,000. She cannot recover both the full benefit of the bargain and her reliance expenditures for the same loss. Because the $100,000 land-value measure is certain and larger, she will likely elect expectation damages, though she may argue that the net still loss is a separate, foreseeable consequential item that the land-value measure does not compensate, and § 3306's allowance for preparation expenses and consequential damages supports that argument.

**Restitution and other limits.** If Ines instead chose to rescind, she would recover the $45,000 deposit in restitution, but she would give up the $100,000 benefit of the bargain, because rescission unwinds the contract that expectation damages enforce; at common law she must elect. In California, a claim for damages is not inconsistent with rescission: a rescinding party recovers restitution plus any consequential damages, such as her net still and permit costs, but no duplicate or inconsistent items (Civ. Code § 1692). Rescission is still the weaker choice here because it forgoes the $100,000. No punitive damages are available for breach of contract, however deliberate.

**Best remedy.** Ines should seek specific performance, protected by a lis pendens and a preliminary injunction. Failing that, she should elect expectation damages of $100,000 plus her deposit, arguing under § 3306 for her net still and permit costs (about $16,000) as additional preparation or consequential losses.

- **75+:** both subjects fully tested: the condition is waived by its beneficiary before the deadline, repudiation is identified, interim relief and all five specific-performance elements are analyzed with defenses measured at formation, and damages are computed with the right date.
- **65:** contract analysis and specific performance are solid, but the preliminary injunction or the reliance/expectation election is thin.
- **55:** treats the unmet financing deadline as ending the contract, or skips interim relief entirely.
- **45 or below:** discusses remedies without first establishing breach, or uses the Crestline price ($1,050,000) as the damages measure without analysis.

**Common mistakes**
1. Letting Walt rely on a condition that protects only the buyer. The beneficiary may waive it.
2. Measuring "inadequate consideration" when the suit is filed. Fairness is judged at contracting.
3. Using the resale price rather than market value at the time of breach.
4. Adding reliance costs on top of full expectation damages without addressing double recovery.
5. Forgetting the lis pendens and Crestline's notice. A buyer's specific-performance right is lost only to a bona fide purchaser.
