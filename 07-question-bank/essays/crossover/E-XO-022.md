## Facts

Leila and Grant married in 2010. In 2014 Grant, an orthodontist, opened Ahmadi Orthodontics in Pasadena with $80,000 from the couple's joint savings. He has always been its sole owner and its only orthodontist. By 2025 the practice had about 1,800 active patients, received regular referrals from 25 local dentists, and was known throughout the area by its name and logo.

On January 20, 2025, Grant had a free 45-minute consultation with Martin, a sole practitioner who handles family-law cases, about getting a divorce. Grant told Martin the practice's true revenues, said he had been prepaying equipment leases "so the books look thin for a while," and said he planned to "open a second office after we split so the growth is all mine." Martin said he would be glad to help, but Grant hired a lawyer nearer his office.

On February 1, 2025, Grant told Leila he wanted a divorce and moved into an apartment; the date of separation is not disputed. Leila stayed in the family home, bought during the marriage with community funds, with the couple's two children. From February 2025 through January 2026 Leila paid every $4,000 monthly mortgage payment from her own post-separation salary. The home's fair rental value is $6,000 a month. Grant paid child support under a temporary order but no spousal support.

The experts agree that the practice's equipment, receivables and other tangible assets were worth $350,000 at separation; neither knew of the lease prepayments. Leila's expert values the practice's goodwill at separation at $600,000, based on its patient base, referral network and name. Grant's expert says the goodwill is "zero" because "patients come to see Grant; without Grant, there's no practice." In May 2025 Grant opened a second office in Glendale, using $150,000 of practice cash that had accumulated before separation, and began working 60-hour weeks. The practice is expected to be worth $1,300,000 at trial.

In March 2025 Leila retained Martin under a written hourly fee agreement. Martin did not tell Leila about his consultation with Grant and did not seek Grant's consent. He served discovery demanding the practice's equipment-lease prepayment records, which revealed $90,000 of prepayments made in late 2024.

During the summer of 2025 Martin and Leila began dating, and in September 2025 they began a sexual relationship. In January 2026 Grant's lawyer, having learned of the consultation, moved to disqualify Martin. Martin told Leila, "If anyone asks about us, say we're just friends. It's nobody's business." Martin continues to represent Leila.

## Call of the Question

1. What are Leila's and Grant's rights in Ahmadi Orthodontics, and what claims does each have concerning the mortgage payments and Leila's use of the house since separation? Answer according to California law.
2. What ethical violations, if any, has Martin committed? Answer according to California and ABA authorities.

## Model Answer

### 1. Community property

**Framework.** Property acquired during marriage while domiciled in California, including a spouse's earnings and the product of a spouse's labor, is community property (CP) (Fam. Code § 760). Earnings and accumulations after the date of separation (DOS) are the earning spouse's separate property (SP) (§ 771). The DOS is February 1, 2025. The community estate is divided equally, generally valued as near the time of trial as practicable, although the court may use another date between separation and trial for good cause (§§ 2550, 2552).

**A. Ahmadi Orthodontics**

*Character.* Grant opened the practice during the marriage with $80,000 of community savings and built it with his community labor. It is **CP**, even though Grant is the sole owner of record; title does not change character between spouses (*In re Marriage of Valli*).

*Goodwill.* The goodwill of a business or professional practice developed during marriage is a community asset, valued without regard to the spouse's post-separation efforts; but a spouse's personal skill or enhanced earning capacity, standing alone, is not goodwill (*Golden v. Golden*; *In re Marriage of Foster*).
- *Grant.* Patients come for him personally; without him the practice is worth only its equipment. That is personal skill, not a divisible asset.
- *Leila.* Goodwill is the expectation of continued patronage. The practice has 1,800 active patients, a referral network of 25 dentists and a recognized name and logo. Those features exist apart from Grant's daily skill, and California treats a sole practitioner's goodwill as community property when it was built during marriage.
- *Conclusion.* The practice has substantial goodwill developed during the marriage. A court will likely value it near Leila's figure, perhaps discounted for the portion tied to Grant personally. It is CP.

*The prepaid leases.* Grant prepaid $90,000 of equipment leases in late 2024, before separation, to make the books "look thin." The prepayments are community funds converted into a prepaid asset (future use of the equipment) that neither expert counted, and depressed profits also understate the goodwill if it is valued on earnings. The court should add the prepaid value and correct the earnings figures.

*Fiduciary duty.* Spouses must disclose all material facts about community assets and may not take unfair advantage of each other (§§ 721(b), 1100(e)). Deliberately distorting the practice's books before a planned divorce breaches that duty. Leila may seek an accounting and, for any asset concealed or transferred in breach, 50% of its value plus fees, or 100% if the breach involved fraud, oppression or malice (§ 1101(g)–(h)). At a minimum the court will count the $90,000 at full value; a § 1101 award is available if the prepayments were meant to hide community value.

**B. Growth after separation (*Imperato*)**

*Rule.* When a community business grows after separation because of one spouse's post-separation efforts, the increase is apportioned between the community, as a return on its capital, and the working spouse's SP, as a return on post-separation labor, using a reverse Pereira or Van Camp analysis (*In re Marriage of Imperato*; § 771).

*Application.* At separation the practice was worth about $950,000 ($350,000 tangible + $600,000 goodwill), before adding the prepayments. By trial it is expected to be worth $1,300,000, an increase of about $350,000.
- *Grant.* The increase comes from the Glendale office, which he opened after separation and staffs with 60-hour weeks. His post-separation labor is his SP. He told Martin that was his plan.
- *Leila.* The new office was built with $150,000 of **community** cash and draws on the community's patient base, referral network and name. The community should receive a fair return on that capital.
- *Likely result.* The growth was driven chiefly by Grant's personal post-separation work, so a reverse Pereira approach fits: the community receives its DOS value plus a fair return on the community capital (including the $150,000) through trial, and the rest of the increase is Grant's SP. Alternatively, the court may value the practice as of the DOS for good cause (§ 2552(b)). Either way, most of the post-separation increase is likely Grant's, but not all of it.

*Division.* Grant will likely keep the practice and make an equalizing payment to Leila of half the community value.

**C. The house: Epstein credits and Watts charges**

*Epstein.* A spouse who uses separate funds, such as post-separation earnings, to pay a community obligation after separation and before trial is generally entitled to reimbursement from the community, unless the payment was a gift, was in effect a discharge of a support duty she owed, was made under an agreement not to seek reimbursement, or was a payment on an asset she was using exclusively that did not exceed the value of that use (*In re Marriage of Epstein*; Fam. Code § 2626). Leila paid $48,000 (12 × $4,000) of the community mortgage from her SP salary. Nothing suggests a gift or an agreement. But she lived in the house the whole time, and her $4,000 monthly payments were less than its $6,000 monthly rental value, so Grant will argue that the exclusive-use exception bars reimbursement; Leila will answer that she shared the home with the couple's children. **Leila has an Epstein claim of up to $48,000, which may be denied or offset because of her exclusive use.**

*Watts.* A spouse who has exclusive use of a community asset after separation may be charged its reasonable rental value, payable to the community, in the court's discretion (*In re Marriage of Watts*). Leila had exclusive use for 12 months at a fair rental value of $6,000 a month: a potential **$72,000** charge.
- *Grant.* Leila lived rent-free in a community asset; the community should be credited, and the charge more than offsets her Epstein claim.
- *Leila.* She lived there with the couple's two children, and Grant paid no spousal support; her use of the home was in effect support, which weighs against a full charge. The court should also reduce the charge to reflect the children's need for a stable home.
- *Likely result.* The court will consider the two claims together. On a straight offset, the $72,000 Watts charge exceeds the $48,000 Epstein credit, leaving Leila owing the community a net $24,000; the exclusive-use exception to Epstein reaches the same place. But the court has discretion: because the children lived there and Grant paid no spousal support, it may reduce or decline the Watts charge, and if it does, Leila may keep some or all of her Epstein credit. The likely outcome is a net charge to Leila of no more than $24,000, reduced in the court's discretion; a net reimbursement to Leila is possible only if the court declines the Watts charge and rejects Grant's exclusive-use argument.

### 2. Martin's ethical violations

**Sources of law.** California lawyers are governed by the California Rules of Professional Conduct (CRPC) and the State Bar Act (Bus. & Prof. Code); the ABA Model Rules (MR) are persuasive only.

**A. Grant as a prospective client (rule 1.18)**

*Rule.* A person who consults a lawyer about possibly retaining the lawyer is a prospective client. Even if no representation follows, the lawyer may not use or reveal information learned in the consultation, and may not represent a client with materially adverse interests in the same or a substantially related matter if the lawyer received information from the prospective client that is **material to the matter** (California) or **could be significantly harmful** to that person in the matter (ABA). The bar is lifted only by the informed written consent of both persons (California) or informed consent confirmed in writing (ABA), or, for **other lawyers** in the firm, by a timely screen and written notice (CRPC 1.18; MR 1.18).

*Application.* Grant consulted Martin about a divorce, and Martin said he would be glad to help, so Grant was a prospective client. Martin learned the practice's true revenues, the lease prepayments and Grant's plan to time the second office: information plainly material to the property division and capable of significant harm to Grant. Martin then represented Leila against Grant in the same divorce. Neither Grant nor Leila consented. A screen is impossible because Martin is a sole practitioner and is himself the lawyer who received the information. Martin may argue that a free 45-minute meeting created no relationship, but the rule applies precisely to such consultations.

*Conclusion.* **Violation of both rules.** Grant's motion to disqualify will likely be granted: the court protects the integrity of the process, and Martin's possession of Grant's confidences in the same matter is a clear conflict (Code Civ. Proc. § 128(a)(5); *SpeeDee Oil*).

**B. Using Grant's information; not telling Leila**

*Use.* A lawyer may not use a prospective client's information except as permitted for a former client's information. Martin used what Grant told him to target discovery on the lease prepayments. He may argue that lease records are routine discovery, but his demand tracked Grant's confidences exactly. **Likely violation** of CRPC 1.18(b) and MR 1.18(b).

*Leila.* A lawyer must promptly inform the client of any circumstance requiring the client's informed consent and keep her reasonably informed of significant developments (CRPC 1.4; MR 1.4; B&P § 6068(m)). Martin's duties to Grant created a significant risk of materially limiting his representation of Leila, and a disqualification fight was foreseeable, so Leila needed to know before hiring him; California requires informed **written** consent to such a conflict, the ABA informed consent confirmed in writing (CRPC 1.7(b); MR 1.7(a)(2)). Martin told her nothing. Leila now faces losing her lawyer mid-case. **Violation of both rules.**

**C. The sexual relationship**

*Rule.* California and ABA: a lawyer may not have sexual relations with a current client unless a consensual sexual relationship existed when the lawyer-client relationship began (CRPC 1.8.10; MR 1.8(j)). California's statute also makes it cause for discipline to continue representing a client with whom the lawyer has sexual relations if the relationship causes the lawyer to perform incompetently (B&P § 6106.9).

*Application.* Martin and Leila began dating months after he became her lawyer and began a sexual relationship in September 2025. No relationship existed when the representation began. Leila is a client in a divorce, the kind of emotionally vulnerable client the rule most protects. Martin may argue the relationship is consensual between adults, but consent is not an exception. **Violation of both rules.** The relationship also creates a personal-interest conflict (CRPC 1.7(b); MR 1.7(a)(2)): Martin's personal stake may affect his advice about settlement and the disqualification motion, and could make him a witness in the case. If his judgment has been affected, § 6106.9 is also implicated.

**D. "Say we're just friends"**

A lawyer may not counsel or assist a witness to testify falsely (CRPC 3.4; MR 3.4(b)), may not counsel or assist a client in conduct the lawyer knows is fraudulent (California: or a violation of law) (CRPC 1.2.1; MR 1.2(d)), and may not engage in conduct involving dishonesty, deceit or misrepresentation, including through another (CRPC 8.4(a), (c); MR 8.4(a), (c)).
- *Martin's argument.* Leila's private life is irrelevant, and advising a client that she need not volunteer personal matters is legitimate.
- *Why it is likely a violation.* Martin did not tell Leila to decline to answer or to assert a privacy objection; he told her to "say we're just friends," which is false. With a disqualification motion pending, the relationship is likely to come up in declarations or testimony, where the advice would be to give false evidence.

**Likely violation of both rules.**

**E. Withdrawal**

A lawyer must withdraw if the representation will result in a violation of the rules (CRPC 1.16(a); MR 1.16(a)). Continuing to represent Leila perpetuates both the rule 1.18 conflict and the rule 1.8.10 violation, so withdrawal is **mandatory**. Because the divorce is pending, Martin needs the court's permission and must take reasonable steps to avoid prejudice to Leila: notice, time to retain new counsel, and prompt delivery of her file (CRPC 1.16(c)–(e); MR 1.16(c)–(d)). By continuing, he violates both rules.

### Conclusion

The practice, including its goodwill and the prepaid leases, is CP; most of its post-separation growth is likely Grant's SP, subject to a return on the community capital used. Leila's Epstein claim (up to $48,000) and Grant's Watts claim (up to $72,000) will likely be offset, leaving Leila owing the community no more than about $24,000, an amount the court may reduce because the children lived in the home and Grant paid no spousal support. Martin violated the prospective-client rule, used Grant's confidences, failed to disclose the conflict to Leila, began a prohibited sexual relationship with her, counseled her to give a false account, and must withdraw.

**Common mistakes**
1. Treating goodwill as nonexistent because the practice depends on one professional. Personal skill alone is not goodwill, but a patient base, referral network and name are.
2. Treating all post-separation growth as CP (or all as SP). *Imperato* apportions between community capital and post-separation labor.
3. Raising Epstein without Watts, or vice versa. They arise together and are usually offset.
4. Thinking a free, brief consultation creates no duties. Rule 1.18 protects prospective clients, and a solo lawyer cannot screen himself.
5. Treating a consensual relationship with a divorce client as a mere appearance problem. Both rule systems prohibit sexual relations with a current client absent a pre-existing relationship.
