# E-XO-015 — The cancelled pickleball center (Contracts + Remedies)

## Facts

In February 2026, Bayside Pickleball Club, LLC ("Bayside") signed a written contract with Terrace Builders, Inc. ("Terrace") to design and build an indoor pickleball facility on a lot Bayside owned, for a fixed price of $1,200,000. Bayside paid a $300,000 deposit on signing. The contract required Terrace to deliver permit drawings by March 15, to begin construction by May 1, and to finish by December 1. It did not say that time was of the essence. Paragraph 14 provided: "If Owner cancels this contract before completion for any reason other than Contractor's default, Contractor may retain all sums paid as liquidated damages and not as a penalty." Further payments were due monthly as the work progressed. Terrace's bid file estimated its total cost of performance at $1,050,000.

Terrace delivered the permit drawings on March 25, ten days late, because its architect had been ill. Bayside's manager complained but accepted the drawings. The city issued the building permit on April 20, in time for a May 1 start.

On April 28, Bayside's main investor withdrew. On April 30, Bayside wrote to Terrace: "Because you missed the March 15 deadline, Bayside terminates the contract. In any event, the project is cancelled."

By April 30, Terrace had spent $95,000 on design and permitting and $85,000 on clearing and grading the site, a total of $180,000. An appraiser says that the drawings and site work would cost about $210,000 to obtain from another contractor, but that the grading has increased the lot's market value by only $40,000. Bayside has no plans to build.

On May 5, after receiving Bayside's letter, Terrace ordered $40,000 of custom steel roof trusses for the project, hoping Bayside would change its mind. Terrace can resell them only for scrap, for about $5,000.

Steel prices rose sharply in the spring of 2026. Bayside has an expert who will testify that, had the project gone forward, Terrace's actual cost would have been about $1,230,000. Terrace's president will testify that in February Terrace obtained fixed-price quotes for most of its steel and would have earned its planned profit.

Terrace has kept the $300,000 deposit and claims it may keep all of it. Bayside demands its return.

## Call of the Question

1. Did Bayside breach the contract, and did Terrace act properly after receiving Bayside's April 30 letter? Discuss.
2. What can Terrace recover from Bayside? Discuss.
3. Can Bayside recover any part of its $300,000 deposit? Discuss.

## Model Answer

### 1. Breach

**A. Governing law.** This is a contract to design and build a facility, a contract for services and construction, so the common law and the Restatement (Second) of Contracts govern.

**B. Were Terrace's late drawings a material breach?** A breach is material if it deprives the injured party of a substantial part of the benefit it reasonably expected; the injured party may then suspend performance and, if the breach is not cured, terminate. A minor breach gives only a claim for damages. Courts weigh the R2d § 241 factors: the benefit lost, the adequacy of damages, the breaching party's forfeiture, the likelihood of cure, and good faith. Delay is usually minor unless time is of the essence.

- *Benefit lost.* The drawings were ten days late, but the permit issued on April 20, in time for the May 1 start. Bayside lost essentially nothing.
- *Damages and forfeiture.* Any harm from the delay could be compensated in money. Termination, by contrast, would cost Terrace its whole contract.
- *Cure and good faith.* Terrace cured by delivering the drawings, and the delay came from its architect's illness, not bad faith.
- *Time of the essence and waiver.* The contract did not make time of the essence. Bayside complained but accepted the drawings, which waived the right to treat the delay as grounds to terminate.

**Terrace's breach was minor. Bayside had at most a claim for nominal damages, and no right to terminate.**

**C. Bayside's April 30 letter.** An anticipatory repudiation is a clear and unequivocal statement, made before performance is due, that a party will not perform. A party who terminates for a minor breach is itself in material breach. "Bayside terminates the contract... the project is cancelled" is unequivocal, so **Bayside repudiated and committed a total breach.**

That also triggers paragraph 14. Bayside will argue that it cancelled because of "Contractor's default." Read in context, "default" means a breach justifying termination, not a minor delay that was cured and accepted. Bayside's own letter ("In any event, the project is cancelled") shows that the real reason was the loss of its investor. **Bayside cancelled "for a reason other than Contractor's default."**

**D. Did Terrace act properly? Mitigation.** After a repudiation the injured party may treat it as a total breach, stop performing and sue. It may not keep performing to increase its damages, and it cannot recover losses it could have avoided by reasonable effort (*Rockingham County v. Luten Bridge Co.*). Terrace was entitled to stop work and sue. But on May 5, after an unequivocal repudiation, it ordered $40,000 of custom trusses. Hoping Bayside would change its mind did not justify spending more; urging retraction does not permit continued performance. **The trusses were an avoidable loss**: Terrace cannot recover the $40,000 (or the $35,000 net loss after scrap value).

### 2. Terrace's recovery

**A. Liquidated damages or penalty.** A term fixing damages for breach is enforceable if, at the time of contracting, actual damages were difficult to estimate and the amount was a reasonable forecast of the probable loss. A term fixing an unreasonably large amount is an unenforceable penalty, and the injured party is limited to actual damages. The parties' label ("not as a penalty") does not control. A valid clause is usually the exclusive measure of damages for the breach it covers.

- *Difficulty of estimation.* Terrace will argue that, at signing, its loss from a cancellation was hard to predict: it would depend on when Bayside cancelled, how much work was done, and volatile steel prices.
- *Reasonable forecast.* Bayside has the stronger point here. Paragraph 14 lets Terrace keep "all sums paid," however early or late the cancellation and whatever Terrace has spent. Cancelling the day after signing would forfeit $300,000 for almost no loss; cancelling after $900,000 of progress payments would forfeit all of that. An amount that tracks Bayside's payments rather than Terrace's harm is not a forecast of loss. Terrace will respond that the Restatement also tests reasonableness against the actual loss (R2d § 356), and that on its evidence its actual loss, $150,000 profit plus $180,000 in costs, exceeds the $300,000 it holds. But that figure depends on the disputed profit evidence. On Bayside's evidence Terrace's actual loss is about $150,000 ($180,000 in costs less the $30,000 it would have lost), half the deposit, and a clause that matches the loss at one moment only by coincidence is not a forecast.
- *California.* In a commercial contract, a liquidated-damages clause is presumed valid unless the party challenging it proves it was unreasonable under the circumstances existing when the contract was made (Civ. Code § 1671(b)). Bayside bears that burden, which makes the question closer. But the clause's structure, which ties the amount to payments made and not to any estimate of loss, is strong evidence that it was unreasonable when made. Because § 1671(b) judges reasonableness only as of contracting, Terrace's actual-loss argument does not help it in California.

**Paragraph 14 is likely an unenforceable penalty**, leaving Terrace to its actual damages. If it were valid, Terrace would keep the $300,000 and could recover nothing more.

**B. Expectation damages; certainty.** The injured party recovers the value of the promised performance minus costs avoided. When an owner repudiates a construction contract, the builder recovers its expected profit plus the costs it has incurred, minus payments received. Damages must be proved with reasonable certainty: the fact of loss must be certain, but its amount may be a reasonable estimate.

- *Terrace's version.* Profit $150,000 ($1,200,000 − $1,050,000) + costs $180,000 − deposit $300,000 = **$30,000** owed beyond the deposit, for a total entitlement of $330,000.
- *Bayside's version.* If Terrace's total cost would have been $1,230,000, the contract would have lost $30,000. Terrace's expectation interest is then only $150,000: the $1,200,000 price less the $1,050,000 it would still have had to spend (equivalently, $180,000 spent less the $30,000 loss). It already holds $300,000, so it recovers nothing more, and the $150,000 excess goes to Bayside's restitution claim (call 3).
- *Certainty.* Terrace is an established contractor with a bid file and fixed-price steel quotes, which is reliable evidence. Bayside will respond that the job was only about 15% done, steel prices spiked, and the experts directly conflict. A court could find a profit proved if it credits the fixed-price quotes, but the conflicting evidence makes **the lost profit uncertain**. If it is not proved, Terrace turns to reliance or restitution.

**C. Reliance damages.** As an alternative to expectation, the injured party may recover its expenditures in preparing for and performing the contract, less any loss the breaching party proves, with reasonable certainty, that the injured party would have suffered had the contract been performed.

- *Expenditures.* Design and permitting ($95,000) plus site work ($85,000) = **$180,000**. The trusses are excluded as unmitigated post-repudiation spending.
- *Losing-contract deduction.* Bayside may reduce reliance damages by proving that Terrace would have lost $30,000, which would leave $150,000. But the burden is on Bayside, the party in breach, and the same conflicting evidence that makes Terrace's profit uncertain makes Bayside's claimed loss uncertain. Doubts created by the breach are resolved against the breaching party.

**Terrace likely recovers $180,000 in reliance damages** (or $150,000 if Bayside proves the loss).

**D. Restitution as the non-breaching party.** Instead of damages, a party injured by a total breach may recover the reasonable value of the benefit it conferred, measured by what it would cost to obtain the work from someone in the claimant's position or, where more appropriate, by the increase in the defendant's wealth.

- *Measure.* The work would cost about $210,000 to obtain from another contractor, but it increased the lot's value by only $40,000, and Bayside has no plans to build. Bayside will argue for the $40,000 measure because the drawings are now useless to it. Because Bayside requested the work and is the party in breach, courts usually award the reasonable value of the requested performance, here **$210,000**.
- *Losing contract.* Courts divide on whether restitution may exceed what the claimant would have netted had the contract been performed. Many allow it, capping it only at the contract price.

**Restitution of $210,000 is likely Terrace's best measure if its lost profit cannot be proved.**

### 3. Bayside's claim to the deposit

**A. Restitution for the breaching party.** A party in material breach that cannot recover on the contract may still recover in restitution the benefit it conferred in excess of the loss its breach caused (R2d § 374; *Britton v. Turner*). Some courts deny recovery to a willful breacher, but the modern trend allows it, and in California even a willfully defaulting buyer may recover payments that exceed the other party's damages (*Freedman v. Rector*). A valid liquidated-damages clause or forfeitable deposit limits the breaching party's recovery.

Bayside's breach was deliberate: it cancelled because its investor withdrew and gave a pretextual reason. Under the modern rule, and in California, that does not bar restitution. If paragraph 14 is valid, Bayside recovers nothing. If, as is likely, it is a penalty, Bayside recovers the deposit in excess of what Terrace is entitled to for Bayside's breach.

**B. Election and net result.** A plaintiff may plead alternative remedies but must elect one and may not recover twice for the same loss. Terrace cannot combine reliance damages with restitution, or restitution with expectation.

| Terrace's measure | Terrace keeps | Bayside recovers |
|---|---|---|
| Paragraph 14 valid | $300,000 | $0 |
| Expectation proved ($330,000) | $300,000, plus $30,000 more from Bayside | $0 |
| Restitution ($210,000) | $210,000 | $90,000 |
| Reliance ($180,000) | $180,000 | $120,000 |
| Reliance less a proved loss, or expectation on Bayside's evidence ($150,000) | $150,000 | $150,000 |

**Most likely result:** paragraph 14 is a penalty and Terrace's profit is too contested to prove, so Terrace elects restitution, keeps $210,000, and must return **$90,000** to Bayside. That figure nets the parties' two restitution claims. Because § 374 measures Bayside's recovery by the excess over the loss its breach caused, a court could instead deduct only Terrace's damages (reliance, $180,000) and return $120,000; the point is unsettled. Terrace absorbs its own loss on the trusses. If the court credits Terrace's fixed-price quotes, Terrace instead keeps the whole deposit and recovers $30,000 more.

**Common mistakes**
1. Treating any missed deadline as a material breach. Without a time-of-essence clause, apply the § 241 factors.
2. Letting Terrace recover the post-repudiation truss order.
3. Accepting the clause's "not as a penalty" label, or forgetting that California puts the burden on the challenger under § 1671(b).
4. Combining reliance and restitution, or reliance and expectation, for the same loss.
5. Denying Bayside any restitution because its breach was willful, without noting the modern rule and *Freedman*.
