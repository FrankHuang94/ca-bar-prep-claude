## Facts

Edith Marlowe, a widow, died on March 10, 2026, domiciled in California. Her only child died in 2019; Edith's only descendant is her grandson, Sam. In 2019, Edith executed a will, properly signed and witnessed, that provides:

> 1. I give my cabin at Shaver Lake to my niece, Nell.
> 2. I give my duplex on Oak Street to my friend, Basil.
> 3. I give my house on Pine Street to my nephew, Pierce.
> 4. I direct my executor to pay all my just debts.
> 5. I give the residue of my estate to my grandson, Sam.

**The cabin.** In 2005, Edith and her brother Hugo bought the Shaver Lake cabin together, taking title as "Edith Marlowe and Hugo Marlowe, as joint tenants with right of survivorship." In 2024, after a falling-out with Hugo, Edith signed and had notarized a deed transferring her interest "from Edith Marlowe, joint tenant, to Edith Marlowe, as tenant in common." She did not tell Hugo. She put the deed in her safe deposit box and never recorded it. Her executor found the deed and recorded it on March 30, 2026, twenty days after her death.

**The duplex.** In 2023, Edith sold the Oak Street duplex to Rhea for $700,000. Rhea paid $140,000 down and signed a promissory note to Edith for $560,000, secured by a deed of trust on the duplex naming Edith as beneficiary. Before Edith's death, Rhea paid $60,000 of principal, which Edith deposited in her bank account along with the down payment. At Edith's death, $500,000 remained owing on the note.

**The Fresno lot.** In 2025, Edith signed and had notarized a grant deed conveying a vacant lot in Fresno to her friend and neighbor, Joy. She handed the deed to her lawyer, Mr. Ortega, saying, "Give this to Joy and record it after I die." Two months later she telephoned Ortega and said, "Keep holding Joy's deed. I may change my mind about who gets the lot." Ortega still had the deed when Edith died. Joy says Edith often told her, "That lot is yours."

**The Pine Street house.** In 2022, Edith borrowed $200,000 from Valley Bank, secured by a deed of trust on the Pine Street house. She signed the note alone. The full $200,000 was still owed at her death. Pierce insists that, under Article 4 of the will, the estate must pay off the loan before he takes the house.

At her death, Edith also had $350,000 in bank accounts.

## Call of the Question

Answer according to California law.

1. What rights do Hugo and Nell have in the Shaver Lake cabin?
2. What rights does Basil have regarding the Oak Street duplex and the obligations relating to it?
3. What rights do Joy and Sam have in the Fresno lot?
4. What rights does Pierce have in the Pine Street house, and who must pay the loan secured by it?

## Model Answer

**The will.** Edith's 2019 will was properly signed and witnessed, so it is a valid attested will (Prob. Code § 6110). A will passes the property the testator owns at death (§ 21105). It does not pass property that goes to others outside probate, such as by joint-tenancy survivorship. The devises of the cabin, the duplex and the house are **specific** gifts of identified land; the gift to Sam is **residuary** (§ 21117). Land in California passes under California law.

### 1. The Shaver Lake cabin: Hugo vs. Nell

**A. Joint tenancy and survivorship.** A joint tenancy is concurrent ownership with a right of survivorship: when one joint tenant dies, her interest ends and the survivor owns the whole (Civ. Code § 683). Edith and Hugo took title in 2005 "as joint tenants with right of survivorship," acquiring their interests by the same deed at the same time, in equal shares with equal rights of possession. A valid joint tenancy was created. Joint-tenancy property passes by survivorship outside probate and is not subject to the decedent's will. A will cannot sever a joint tenancy, because it takes effect only at death, the same moment the survivor's right vests (*Estate of England*). Edith's 2019 devise to Nell therefore cannot, by itself, defeat Hugo's survivorship.

**B. Did Edith sever by deeding to herself?** A joint tenant may sever unilaterally, without the other's consent or knowledge, by conveying her interest during life. Severance converts her share into a tenancy in common, which has no right of survivorship and passes by will. The traditional rule required a conveyance to a third party, so a joint tenant had to use a "straw man." California allows a joint tenant to sever by a **deed to herself** (*Riddle v. Harmon*; Civ. Code § 683.2). Edith's 2024 deed, from herself as joint tenant to herself as tenant in common, is the kind of self-conveyance *Riddle* approved. Secrecy does not matter; Hugo's consent was not required.

**C. The recording requirement.** In California, a unilateral severance is effective against the other joint tenant's right of survivorship only if the severing deed is **recorded before the severing tenant's death**, or is executed and acknowledged no earlier than three days before death and recorded within seven days after death (Civ. Code § 683.2(c)). The rule prevents a joint tenant from secretly severing, keeping the deed in a drawer, and gambling on who dies first.
- *Nell's argument.* Edith signed and notarized the deed in 2024 and clearly intended to end Hugo's survivorship after their falling-out. The deed was eventually recorded.
- *Hugo's argument.* The deed was executed in 2024, years before death, so the short post-death window does not apply. It was recorded twenty days after Edith died. A secret, unrecorded severance kept in a safe deposit box is exactly what § 683.2 makes ineffective against the survivor.

**Conclusion.** The severance is ineffective against Hugo's survivorship. **Hugo owns the entire cabin** by right of survivorship. Because Edith's interest ended at her death, nothing passes to the estate under Article 1. Nell's specific gift fails for lack of property, and Nell takes nothing.

### 2. The Oak Street duplex: Basil

**A. Ademption by extinction.** A specific gift is adeemed if the property is not in the estate at death. Under the traditional identity theory, the gift simply fails. Edith sold the duplex to Rhea in 2023, so Sam, the residuary beneficiary, will argue that Basil's gift is adeemed and that everything Edith received from the sale belongs to the residue.

**B. California's statutory exception.** California disfavors ademption. By statute, the recipient of a specific gift takes, in addition to whatever of the property remains, **any unpaid balance of the purchase price** owed to the testator from her sale of the property, with any security interest (§ 21133). At Edith's death Rhea still owed $500,000 on the purchase-money note. That balance is the unpaid purchase price of the specifically devised duplex. **Basil takes the $500,000 note.**

**C. The deed of trust.** A deed of trust, like a mortgage, secures an obligation (here, Rhea's note). California follows the lien theory: the beneficiary holds a lien, not title, and Rhea owns the duplex. The security follows the obligation it secures, so whoever holds the note also holds the benefit of the deed of trust. Basil therefore takes both the right to Rhea's remaining payments and the deed of trust on the duplex. If Rhea defaults, Basil may foreclose.

**D. Amounts Edith received before death.** § 21133 covers only amounts *unpaid* at death. The $140,000 down payment and the $60,000 of principal Edith received and deposited in her bank account are no longer the duplex itself; absent tracing, they are part of the general estate and pass under the residuary clause to Sam. Basil may argue that California disfavors ademption, so proceeds of a specific gift that can still be traced should follow it (*Estate of Mason*; § 21139) [VERIFY V-534]. But Edith herself sold the duplex and deposited the payments in her general bank account; unless Basil can identify part of the $350,000 she held at death as those proceeds, they cannot be traced, and § 21133 reaches only the balance unpaid at death.

**Conclusion.** Basil does not take the duplex itself, which belongs to Rhea. He takes Rhea's $500,000 note and the deed of trust securing it. The $200,000 Edith collected before death goes to Sam.

### 3. The Fresno lot: Joy vs. Sam

**A. Delivery.** A deed must be in writing, signed by the grantor, name the grantee, describe the land and show an intent to transfer (Civ. Code §§ 1091, 1092). Edith's grant deed meets these requirements. But a deed passes title only when **delivered**: the grantor must, by words or conduct, intend the deed to be presently operative, even if the grantee's enjoyment is postponed. Delivery is a question of intent, not of physical transfer. A grantor who keeps the deed, or keeps control over it, is presumed not to have delivered it.

**B. Delivery to a third party: the death escrow.** A grantor may deliver a deed to a third party with irrevocable instructions to give it to the grantee at the grantor's death. If the grantor gives up all right to recall the deed, the delivery is effective at that moment, and the grantee takes a present future interest that becomes possessory at death. If the grantor keeps the right or power to recall the deed, there is no delivery, and the attempted transfer is testamentary.
- *Joy's argument.* Edith handed the signed, notarized deed to her own lawyer with instructions to give it to Joy and record it after her death. She often told Joy, "That lot is yours." That is a classic death escrow, and Edith gave up physical control. If the delivery to Ortega was complete when Edith handed him the deed with an unconditional instruction, her later call could not undo it; a grantor cannot recall a deed already delivered.
- *Sam's argument.* Ortega was Edith's own lawyer and agent, holding the deed subject to her instructions. Two months later Edith exercised exactly the control a grantor in an effective escrow gives up: she told Ortega to "keep holding Joy's deed" because she "may change [her] mind about who gets the lot." That shows she never intended the deed to be presently operative or irrevocable. Her statements to Joy were statements of present intention, not a delivery.

The decisive question is whether Edith gave up the power to recall the deed when she handed it over. Ortega was her own lawyer, bound to follow her instructions, and she never said that her instruction was irrevocable. Her later call cannot undo a completed delivery, but it is strong evidence that she never meant to part with control. **There was no valid delivery, and title to the lot did not pass to Joy.**

**C. Can the deed take effect as a will?** A writing intended to transfer property only at death is testamentary and is valid only if executed with will formalities. The deed was signed and notarized but not witnessed by two people, and a notary's acknowledgment is not attestation (§ 6110). It is typed, so it is not a holograph (§ 6111). Joy might invoke harmless error (§ 6110(c)(2)), which excuses witnessing defects on clear and convincing evidence that the decedent intended the document to be her will. But Edith intended a deed, and she later expressly reserved the right to change her mind. Joy is unlikely to prove by clear and convincing evidence that the deed was meant as a will.

**Conclusion.** The lot was still Edith's property at death. The will does not specifically devise it, so it passes under the residuary clause to **Sam**. Joy takes nothing.

### 4. The Pine Street house: Pierce

**A. Nonexoneration.** A specific gift passes subject to any mortgage, deed of trust or other lien existing at the testator's death, and the devisee has no right to have the estate pay it off, **regardless of a general direction in the will to pay debts** (§ 21131). Under the old common-law exoneration doctrine, Pierce could have demanded that the estate pay off the loan, but California rejects it.
- *Pierce's argument.* Article 4 tells the executor to pay "all my just debts," and the Valley Bank loan is one of Edith's debts.
- *Answer.* § 21131 expressly provides that a general direction to pay debts does not exonerate specifically devised property. Article 4 does not mention the Pine Street loan or say that Pierce is to take free of it.

**Pierce takes the house subject to the $200,000 deed of trust.**

**B. Who pays the loan?** A transferee of encumbered land who takes "subject to" an existing deed of trust is not personally liable on the debt; the lender can look only to the land and to the original borrower. A transferee is personally liable only if he expressly **assumes** the debt. Pierce takes by devise and has assumed nothing, so he is not personally liable on the note. Valley Bank may enforce its deed of trust against the house. Edith's personal liability on the note was a debt of her estate, but as between the estate and Pierce, § 21131 means Pierce cannot require the estate to pay off the lien for his benefit. Practically, Pierce must keep the loan current if he wants to keep the house free of foreclosure.

**Common mistakes**
1. Letting a will sever a joint tenancy, or forgetting California's recording requirement for a self-conveyance.
2. Treating the sale of the duplex as a complete ademption without applying § 21133, or giving Basil the payments Edith received before death.
3. Finding delivery merely because Edith handed the deed to someone else, without asking whether she kept the power to recall it.
4. Treating a notarized deed as satisfying the will formalities.
5. Applying common-law exoneration, or making Pierce personally liable on a note he never assumed.
