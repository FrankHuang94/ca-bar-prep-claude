## Facts

Margaret "Peg" Ellison, an 84-year-old widow, lived in her Fresno home, worth $600,000, and had $400,000 in a credit-union account. Her 2019 will, prepared by her longtime lawyer, left her estate equally to her children, Dale and Laurel. Laurel is a physician in Boston. Dale, who lives in Fresno, has struggled with debt and owes $18,000 in unpaid child support to his former wife, Rhonda, for their daughter, Mia, age 10.

In 2023 Peg's doctor diagnosed mild cognitive impairment. Peg could no longer drive, bathe or cook on her own, and the family hired Tessa, a home health aide, who was paid $4,000 a month to care for Peg five days a week. In 2024 Dale moved into Peg's house, took over her mail and bills under a power of attorney, and began screening her phone calls. Laurel complained that she could rarely reach her mother.

In March 2025 Dale and Tessa drove Peg to the office of Hal, a lawyer Dale had found online. Dale described to Hal "what Mom wants" while Tessa sat with Peg in the waiting room. Hal then met with Peg alone for 20 minutes. Peg told him she owned her house and had "some savings at the credit union"; she named both children and her granddaughter; and she said Laurel "doesn't need anything" and Dale "can't handle money." Hal's notes record that she twice asked what day it was.

The following week Peg signed a new will in Hal's office. Hal and Tessa watched her sign and then signed as witnesses. The will revokes all prior wills and provides:

> 1. $50,000 to Tessa "for her kindness."
> 2. $50,000 to my daughter, Laurel.
> 3. The residue to my trustee, Tessa, to hold in trust for my son Dale for his lifetime. The trustee shall pay Dale all net income at least quarterly and may pay him principal in her sole discretion for his health and support. On Dale's death the trust property shall pass to my granddaughter, Mia. No beneficiary may assign, sell or encumber any interest in this trust, and no interest shall be subject to the claims of any creditor before it is paid to the beneficiary.
> 4. Any beneficiary who contests this will shall take nothing under it.

No lawyer other than Hal advised Peg, and no one gave her a certificate of independent review. Peg died in January 2026. Laurel has filed a contest alleging lack of capacity and undue influence by Dale and Tessa.

In February 2026 Dale signed an agreement assigning to QuickCash Funding his right to trust income for the next five years in exchange for an immediate payment of $40,000. Rhonda has obtained a judgment for the $18,000 of child-support arrears and wants to collect it from Dale's interest in the trust.

## Call of the Question

Answer according to California law.

1. Is Peg's 2025 will valid, and are its gifts to Tessa and to the trust effective?
2. If Laurel's contest fails, does she lose her $50,000 gift?
3. Assuming the trust in Article 3 is valid, (a) may Tessa serve as trustee, and (b) what rights, if any, do QuickCash and Rhonda have against Dale's interest in the trust?

## Model Answer

### 1. Validity of the 2025 will and its gifts

**A. Execution; Tessa as a witness.** An attested will must be in writing, signed by the testator, and signed by at least two witnesses who were present at the same time, witnessed the signing, and understood that the instrument was the testator's will (Prob. Code § 6110). Peg signed in front of Hal and Tessa together, and both signed. **The will was duly executed.**

A will is not invalid because a beneficiary witnessed it. But unless there are two other disinterested subscribing witnesses, a gift to a witness creates a presumption that the witness procured it by duress, menace, fraud or undue influence; if not rebutted, the witness takes no more than she would have received had the will not been established (§ 6112). Tessa receives $50,000, and the only other witness is Hal, so the presumption applies to her gift (see D below). Her appointment as trustee is not a gift to her personally and is not affected by § 6112.

**B. Testamentary capacity.** A testator lacks capacity if, when making the will, she cannot understand the nature of the testamentary act, understand and recollect the nature and situation of her property, or remember and understand her relations to her living descendants, spouse and parents and to others whose interests are affected (§ 6100.5(a)(1)). Capacity is measured at execution; the contestant bears the burden (§ 8252).
- *Laurel.* Peg had a diagnosed cognitive impairment, depended on others for daily care, twice asked Hal what day it was, and described $400,000 only as "some savings."
- *Proponents.* Mild impairment, forgetfulness and confusion about dates are not incapacity. Alone with Hal, Peg described her two main assets, named both children and her granddaughter, and explained her reasons for treating them differently, reasons that match reality (Laurel is a successful physician; Dale has money troubles).
- *Conclusion.* Peg understood what she owned and who her family was. **Laurel likely cannot prove lack of capacity.**

**C. Undue influence by Dale**

*Rule.* A will or any part of it is ineffective to the extent it was procured by undue influence (§ 6104): pressure that overcomes the testator's free will and substitutes the influencer's wishes, judged by the victim's vulnerability, the influencer's apparent authority, the tactics used and the equity of the result (§ 86; *Rice v. Clark*). A presumption of undue influence arises if the contestant shows (1) a confidential relationship, (2) the beneficiary's active participation in procuring the will, and (3) an undue benefit to the beneficiary; the burden then shifts to the proponent.

*Presumption.*
1. *Confidential relationship.* Dale lived with Peg, held her power of attorney and controlled her mail and bills. **Met.**
2. *Active participation.* Dale found the lawyer, drove Peg there and told Hal "what Mom wants." **Met.**
3. *Undue benefit.* Under the 2019 will Dale and Laurel shared equally; under the 2025 will the residue, about $900,000, is held for Dale and Laurel receives only $50,000. **Arguably met.**

The presumption likely arises. The § 86 factors add weight: Peg was cognitively impaired and dependent; Dale had authority over her finances; and he isolated her by screening Laurel's calls.

*Rebuttal.* Dale will show that Hal met Peg alone and that she gave coherent reasons of her own. More tellingly, the will does not give Dale what an influencer would want for himself: he receives only income and principal in another person's discretion, cannot assign or borrow against his interest, and the property passes at his death to Mia. A plan that restrains Dale and protects his child reflects Peg's own judgment that he "can't handle money," not Dale's wishes.

*Conclusion.* This is close, but because the dispositive plan restrains Dale and matches Peg's independently stated reasons, **Dale can likely rebut the presumption**, and the residuary trust stands.

**D. Tessa's $50,000 gift**

*Rule.* A donative transfer to a care custodian of a dependent adult, made in an instrument executed during the period of care or within 90 days before or after it, is presumed to be the product of fraud or undue influence; the presumption may be rebutted only by clear and convincing evidence (§ 21380). A care custodian is a person who provides health or social services to a dependent adult (§ 21362). A dependent adult is a person who, when the instrument was executed, was 65 or older (or 18 or older) and could not provide properly for her personal needs or, because of deficits in mental function, had difficulty managing her finances or resisting fraud or undue influence (§ 21366). The presumption does not apply to a transferee who is a relative within the fourth degree or a cohabitant of the transferor, to gifts of $5,000 or less from larger estates, or where an independent attorney counsels the transferor alone and signs a certificate of independent review (§§ 21382, 21384).

*Application.* Peg was 84, could not drive, bathe or cook on her own, and had a cognitive impairment: a dependent adult. Tessa was paid to provide her daily care, and the will was signed while that care continued: Tessa is a care custodian. No exception applies: Tessa is not a relative, the gift exceeds $5,000, and no certificate was obtained. Tessa will rely on Peg's stated motive ("for her kindness") and on Hal's private meeting with Peg. But Tessa rode with Peg to the lawyer Dale had chosen, waited with her while Dale told Hal "what Mom wants," and then served as a witness to her own gift; Peg received no independent advice. That falls well short of clear and convincing evidence that the gift was free of undue influence. The § 6112 presumption points the same way.

*Effect.* When a transfer fails under § 21380, the instrument operates as if the beneficiary had predeceased the transferor without spouse, domestic partner or issue (§ 21386). **Tessa's gift fails.**

**E. Where the property goes.** Tessa's $50,000 falls into the residue (§ 21111) and so passes to the trust for Dale. Laurel's $50,000 gift stands. If instead Laurel proved that the whole will was the product of Dale's undue influence, the will, including its revocation clause, would be ineffective (§ 6104), and the 2019 will would govern: Dale and Laurel would share the estate equally and outright, and there would be no trust.

### 2. The no-contest clause

A no-contest clause is enforced only against a **direct contest** brought **without probable cause** (§ 21311). A direct contest is a pleading alleging the invalidity of a protected instrument or its terms on listed grounds, including lack of capacity, undue influence and disqualification of a beneficiary (§ 21310). Probable cause exists if the facts known to the contestant would cause a reasonable person to believe there is a reasonable likelihood that the requested relief will be granted after further investigation or discovery.

Laurel's petition alleging incapacity and undue influence is a direct contest, and the will contains a no-contest clause. But she had probable cause: a medical diagnosis of cognitive impairment, Dale's control of Peg's finances and calls, Dale's selection of the lawyer, and a care custodian who witnessed her own gift. Her challenge to Tessa's gift will in fact succeed. **Even if the rest of her contest fails, Laurel keeps her $50,000.**

### 3. The trust

**A. Validity; may Tessa serve?**

*Validity.* A trust requires a settlor with capacity, intent to create a trust, trust property, an identified beneficiary and a lawful purpose; a testamentary trust is created by a valid will (Prob. Code §§ 15200–15205). Article 3 shows clear intent, identifies the residue as the property and names Dale and Mia as beneficiaries. **It is a valid testamentary trust**, and it would not fail for want of a trustee in any event.

*Tessa as trustee.* A sole trustee who is a person described in § 21380(a), such as a care custodian of a dependent-adult settlor, may be removed on that ground unless the court finds that the settlor intended that person to serve and that the intent was not the product of fraud or undue influence (§ 15642(b)(6)). Tessa is such a person. Dale may argue that Peg reasonably wanted someone other than Dale to control the money because he "can't handle money." But nothing shows Peg independently chose Tessa: Tessa was part of the visit Dale arranged, witnessed the will, and is losing her own gift under the undue-influence presumption. The appointment also gives Tessa control of about $950,000 for Dale's lifetime. **The court will likely remove Tessa** and appoint a successor trustee, such as a professional fiduciary, on a beneficiary's petition (§ 17200).

**B. QuickCash and Rhonda**

*The spendthrift clause.* If the trust instrument provides that a beneficiary's interest in income or principal is not subject to voluntary or involuntary transfer, the restraint is valid: the beneficiary cannot assign it, and creditors cannot reach it before it is paid, subject to statutory exceptions (§§ 15300, 15301(a)). Article 3 bars both assignment ("assign, sell or encumber") and creditors' claims. **The clause is valid.** Principal is payable only in the trustee's sole discretion, so it is also protected as a discretionary interest: no transferee or creditor can compel the trustee to pay it (§ 15303). (Section 15302 separately protects a trust that directs payments for a beneficiary's support, to the extent needed for support; Peg's "health and support" language only guides the trustee's discretion.)

*QuickCash.* Dale's assignment of five years of income for $40,000 is a voluntary transfer of exactly the kind the clause forbids. **The assignment is ineffective against the trust**, and the trustee should keep paying the income to Dale. QuickCash's remedy is a contract claim against Dale personally. Once income is actually paid to Dale, it is his own property, and any creditor, including QuickCash if it obtains a judgment, may reach it in his hands.

*Rhonda.* Notwithstanding a spendthrift clause, a beneficiary's interest may be reached to satisfy a judgment for the support of the beneficiary's child, spouse or former spouse. The court may order the trustee to satisfy the judgment out of income or principal that is payable to the beneficiary, or that the trustee in its discretion has decided to pay, as is equitable and reasonable (§ 15305).
- *Income.* Dale's income is mandatory ("shall pay ... at least quarterly"), so it is payable. The court may order the trustee to pay Rhonda from the income until the $18,000 judgment is satisfied, and to pay ongoing support as it comes due.
- *Principal.* Principal is payable only in the trustee's "sole discretion" for Dale's health and support. Rhonda cannot compel the trustee to invade principal; only if the trustee decides to make a payment can the court direct it to the support claim.
- *Equity.* Dale will argue he needs the income to live on, which the court may weigh. But paying support for Mia, who is also the trust's remainder beneficiary, is consistent with Peg's plan.

**Rhonda can reach Dale's income** to satisfy the $18,000 judgment, but not undistributed discretionary principal. Her claim rests on the support exception, not on the separate (and more limited) route available to ordinary judgment creditors.

### Conclusion

The 2025 will was validly executed, and Peg had capacity; Dale can likely rebut the presumption of undue influence, so the trust stands. Tessa's $50,000 gift fails under §§ 21380 and 6112 and falls into the residue. Laurel keeps her $50,000 because she contested with probable cause. Tessa will likely be removed as trustee. The spendthrift clause defeats QuickCash's assignment, but Rhonda may reach Dale's mandatory income for child support.

**Common mistakes**
1. Treating forgetfulness or a dementia diagnosis as automatic incapacity. The § 6100.5 test is functional and measured at execution.
2. Forgetting that a presumption of undue influence can be rebutted, and that the shape of the dispositive plan is evidence.
3. Applying § 21380 to Tessa's appointment as trustee. The presumption governs donative transfers; the trusteeship is attacked through § 15642.
4. Enforcing the no-contest clause against a contestant who had probable cause.
5. Letting the spendthrift clause bar a child-support judgment, or letting the support creditor compel discretionary principal.
