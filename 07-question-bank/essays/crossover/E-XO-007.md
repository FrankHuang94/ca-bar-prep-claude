# E-XO-007 — The pawnshop back door

## Facts

Vera Novak, 68, owned Pawn & Prosper, a pawnshop on the ground floor of a two-story building. Vera lived alone in the apartment upstairs. An interior staircase led from the shop's back room to her apartment; the apartment also had its own street door. The shop closed at 7 p.m.

At about 10:30 p.m. on October 3, 2025, Dex Hale walked down the alley behind the shop. The back door was closed but unlocked. Dex opened it and stepped into the back room, where the shop's cash box sat on a desk. Vera heard a noise and came down the stairs. She saw Dex holding the cash box and grabbed his arm. Vera fell backward against the stairs and broke her wrist. Dex ran out the back door with the box, which held $2,300.

At 10:34 p.m., Vera called 911. On the recording she is crying and breathing hard: "A man just knocked me down and took my cash box! He's tall, wearing a red hoodie, he's running toward the river! Please hurry, my wrist is broken!" The operator asked, "Do you know him?" Vera answered, "I think he's the man who pawned a guitar here last week—Dex something."

The next afternoon, Detective Ruiz visited Vera in the hospital and showed her six photographs. She pointed to Dex's photo and said, "That's him—the man who shoved me." Ruiz recorded this in his report.

Two days later, police arrested Dex and, under a valid warrant, searched his phone. They found a text sent from it at 10:41 p.m. on October 3 to a contact saved as "Mo": "Had to get rough with the old lady but got the box. Lay low." The phone account is in Dex's name, the phone holds Dex's photos and messages, and "Mo" is the number of Dex's brother, Morris.

Dex was charged with burglary and robbery. Vera died of a stroke in December 2025, before trial, for reasons unrelated to the incident. The prosecution gave written pretrial notice that it would offer evidence of an earlier incident. At trial, over defense objections, the court admitted:

(a) the recording of Vera's 911 call;
(b) Detective Ruiz's testimony that Vera picked Dex's photograph and said, "That's him—the man who shoved me";
(c) the October 3 text message, through a police technician who extracted it from Dex's phone;
(d) testimony by the owner of Elm Street Pawn that in August 2025, after closing, Dex came in through her shop's unlocked back door and took $900 from the register. Dex was never charged with that incident. The prosecution offered the evidence to prove identity and intent.

Dex testified in his own defense. He said he went to the back door to ask Vera for a $200 loan on his pawn ticket, found the door unlocked, stepped in "to call up to her," saw the cash box, "panicked" and grabbed it. He said Vera "slipped on the stairs" and that he never touched her. On cross-examination, over objection, the court allowed the prosecutor to impeach him with:

(e) (i) a 2019 felony conviction for burglary, for which Dex was released from prison in 2021; and (ii) a 2023 misdemeanor conviction for shoplifting.

The jurisdiction's burglary statute follows the common law, except that it applies to "any building."

## Call of the Question

1. Is Dex guilty of burglary? Of robbery? Discuss.
2. Applying the Federal Rules of Evidence, and considering any issues under the United States Constitution, did the court properly admit items (a) through (e)? Discuss.

## Model Answer

### 1. Dex's crimes

#### Burglary

Common-law burglary is the breaking and entering of the dwelling of another at nighttime with the intent to commit a felony inside; the intent must exist at the moment of entry. Modern statutes, like this one, extend burglary to other buildings.

- **Breaking.** Breaking requires creating or enlarging an opening by at least slight force. Walking through an open door is not a breaking, but opening a closed door, even an unlocked one, is. Dex "opened" the closed back door. **Satisfied.**
- **Entering.** Dex stepped into the back room. **Satisfied.**
- **Dwelling.** At common law the structure must be one regularly used for sleeping. The pawnshop itself is commercial, but Vera lived upstairs in the same building, connected by an interior staircase, and she used that staircase to reach the shop. Dex will argue that he entered only the shop, which has separate business hours and a separate street door from the apartment. The prosecution will respond that a building with living quarters connected internally to the shop is a dwelling, and the danger the dwelling element addresses, a confrontation with a resident at night, is exactly what happened. This element is close, but **the statute applies to "any building," so it does not matter here.**
- **Of another.** The shop was Vera's. **Satisfied.**
- **Nighttime.** 10:30 p.m. **Satisfied.**

**Intent at the time of entry.** Burglary requires the intent to commit a felony (here, larceny of $2,300, a felony-level theft) when the defendant enters; intent formed afterward does not suffice. Dex testified that he entered to ask Vera for a loan and decided to take the box only after he saw it. The prosecution will point to the circumstances: he came at 10:30 p.m., more than three hours after closing, through the alley and the back door rather than Vera's front door, and he did not knock or call before entering; and the Elm Street incident (if admissible, see below) shows that he entered another pawnshop's unlocked back door after hours and stole cash. A person seeking a loan from a 68-year-old woman would ordinarily ring her apartment door during reasonable hours. This is a question for the jury, but **the circumstantial evidence strongly supports intent to steal at entry, and a burglary conviction is likely.**

#### Robbery

Robbery is larceny from the victim's person or presence accomplished by force or by intimidation.

- **Larceny.** Larceny is the trespassory taking and carrying away of another's personal property with intent to permanently deprive. Dex took Vera's cash box without consent, carried it out of the building, and ran with it, which shows an intent to keep the $2,300. **Satisfied.**
- **Person or presence.** Property is in the victim's presence if it is within her reach or control. Vera confronted Dex in the back room while he held the box and grabbed his arm. **Satisfied.**
- **Force.** The force must be more than the effort needed to take the property. Dex claims Vera slipped and he never touched her. But Vera told the 911 operator that a man "knocked me down," and Dex texted that he "had to get rough with the old lady but got the box." A shove that broke Vera's wrist is far more than incidental force.
- **Timing of the force.** At strict common law, the force or intimidation must be used to obtain the property; force used afterward, to keep it or escape, does not convert larceny into robbery. Dex will argue that he had already picked up the box before any contact. Under the modern rule (and California's), robbery is a continuing offense, and force used to retain the property or escape while still in the victim's presence suffices. Even many common-law courts treat the taking as continuing until the thief leaves the victim's presence, and here Dex used force while Vera was physically trying to stop him from carrying the box away.

**Dex is likely guilty of robbery**, certainly under the modern rule.

**Lesser offenses and merger.** The larceny and the battery (the shove, an intentional application of force causing injury) are lesser included offenses that merge into robbery. Burglary does not merge with the crime committed inside, so Dex may be convicted of both burglary and robbery.

### 2. The evidentiary rulings

Each item must be relevant (FRE 401–402), not substantially outweighed by unfair prejudice (FRE 403), and admissible under the hearsay and other rules. Because this is a criminal case, testimonial hearsay also implicates the Sixth Amendment Confrontation Clause (*Crawford*).

#### (a) The 911 call

**Relevance and hearsay.** The call tends to show a theft by force, its time, and the attacker's identity. Hearsay is an out-of-court statement offered to prove the truth of the matter asserted (FRE 801(c)). Vera's statements are offered to prove that a man knocked her down, took the box, and was Dex, so they are hearsay unless an exception applies.

- *Excited utterance (FRE 803(2)).* A statement relating to a startling event, made while the declarant was under the stress of excitement it caused, is admissible. Being knocked down and robbed is startling; Vera called four minutes later, crying and breathing hard, in pain from a broken wrist, and pleading "Please hurry." Her answer to the operator's question was part of the same excited exchange and related to the event. **Admissible.**
- *Present sense impression (FRE 803(1)).* "He's running toward the river" may also qualify if Vera saw him flee and described it immediately afterward, but the facts do not show she could still see him, and four minutes is at the outer edge of "immediately after," so the excited-utterance exception is the stronger ground.
- *Personal knowledge and opinion.* Dex will argue "I think he's the man who pawned a guitar" is speculation. But Vera saw the man face to face and recognized him from a transaction a week earlier; "I think" expresses ordinary uncertainty, which goes to weight, not admissibility.

**Confrontation Clause.** Vera is unavailable (she died) and was never cross-examined, so her statements are barred if they are testimonial. A statement is testimonial if its primary purpose, viewed objectively, is to establish past events for later prosecution; it is nontestimonial if made to enable police to meet an ongoing emergency (*Davis*; *Michigan v. Bryant*). Vera called within minutes, while injured and alone, describing an assailant who was still fleeing nearby and asking for help. Identifying the fleeing assailant helps police find him and assess the danger. Dex will argue that "Dex something" answered a question about who did it, which looks like evidence-gathering. But in *Davis*, a 911 caller's identification of an assailant during an ongoing emergency was nontestimonial, and these statements were similarly informal and urgent. **Nontestimonial; no Confrontation problem. Properly admitted.**

#### (b) Vera's photo-array identification

**Hearsay.** Detective Ruiz repeats Vera's statement "That's him—the man who shoved me" to prove that Dex was the attacker, so it is hearsay.

- *Prior identification (FRE 801(d)(1)(C)).* A statement identifying a person the declarant perceived earlier is not hearsay, but only if the declarant **testifies and is subject to cross-examination** about it. Vera died before trial, so this exclusion is unavailable.
- *Other exceptions.* The statement was made the next afternoon, in a calm hospital interview, so it is not an excited utterance. Vera was not dying from her injury, so it is not a dying declaration. The residual exception (FRE 807) requires sufficient guarantees of trustworthiness and greater probative value than other reasonably available evidence; the 911 call and the text are other evidence of identity, so the proponent will have difficulty meeting that requirement.

**Confrontation Clause.** Even if a hearsay exception applied, the statement is testimonial: Vera made it to a detective, the day after the crime, in a formal photo-array procedure whose purpose was to identify a suspect for prosecution. There was no ongoing emergency. Vera is unavailable and Dex never had an opportunity to cross-examine her. **The admission of item (b) was error** under both the hearsay rule and *Crawford*. Because the 911 call and the text independently identify Dex, the prosecution may argue the error was harmless.

#### (c) The text message

**Authentication.** The proponent must produce evidence sufficient to support a finding that the item is what it claims to be (FRE 901(a)), a low threshold decided under FRE 104(b). Distinctive characteristics and circumstances can suffice (FRE 901(b)(4)). The phone is registered to Dex and holds his photos and messages; the text went to his brother Morris; it was sent seven minutes after the crime; and its content ("get rough with the old lady," "got the box") matches facts few others would know. A police technician can testify to extracting it. Dex may argue someone else could have used his phone, but that goes to weight. **Authenticated.**

**Hearsay.** A statement offered against a party that was made by that party is not hearsay (FRE 801(d)(2)(A)). The text is Dex's own statement offered by the prosecution, and his own statement raises no Confrontation issue.

**Relevance and 403.** The text is highly probative of force and intent, and its prejudice is the legitimate kind that comes from probative evidence. **Properly admitted.**

#### (d) The Elm Street incident

**The rule.** Evidence of other crimes, wrongs or acts is inadmissible to prove a person's character in order to show that he acted in conformity with it, but it may be admitted for another purpose, such as intent, plan, knowledge or identity (FRE 404(b)(2)). The court must find that a jury could reasonably conclude that the defendant committed the other act (*Huddleston*; FRE 104(b)), weigh the evidence under FRE 403, and, on request, give a limiting instruction (FRE 105). In a criminal case the prosecution must give reasonable notice, which it did.

- *Proof of the act.* The Elm Street owner's first-hand testimony would let a jury find that Dex committed it; the lack of a charge does not matter.
- *Identity.* Using another act to prove identity requires a modus operandi so distinctive that it works like a signature. Entering an unlocked back door after closing and taking cash is a common way to commit a store theft, not a signature. In any event, Dex's own testimony conceded that he was in the shop and took the box. **Identity is not a proper purpose here.**
- *Intent.* Intent at entry is the central disputed issue in the burglary charge: Dex claims he entered innocently to ask for a loan. Proof that two months earlier he entered another pawnshop through an unlocked back door after hours and took cash makes his innocent explanation less likely. Proving intent requires less similarity than proving identity, and the two incidents are closely similar (pawnshops, after hours, unlocked back doors, cash). Dex will argue that the inference still runs through character ("he's a thief, so he meant to steal"). That risk is real, but the evidence rebuts a specific claim of innocent purpose, which is a permissible non-propensity use.
- *Rule 403.* The prejudice is significant, because the jury may treat Dex as a habitual thief. But the probative value on the disputed issue of intent is high, the incident is recent, and a limiting instruction can confine the jury to intent.

**Properly admitted to prove intent**, with a limiting instruction; admitting it for identity would be error.

#### (e) Impeachment with prior convictions

**(i) The 2019 burglary felony.** A conviction for a crime punishable by more than one year may be used to impeach a witness, but when the witness is the criminal defendant, it is admitted only if its probative value outweighs its prejudicial effect *to the defendant* (FRE 609(a)(1)(B)), a test that favors exclusion. The ten-year limit of FRE 609(b) runs from conviction or release, whichever is later; Dex was released in 2021, so the conviction is not too old.

Courts commonly weigh the impeachment value of the crime, its recency, the similarity to the charged crime, and the importance of the defendant's credibility.

- *For admission.* Dex's credibility is central: his testimony that he entered to ask for a loan and that Vera "slipped" is the whole defense. The conviction is recent.
- *Against admission.* Burglary is not a crime of dishonesty in the sense of false statement, so its bearing on truthfulness is modest. It is the **same crime** charged, which creates a strong risk that the jury will reason "once a burglar, always a burglar," the propensity inference Rule 609 is meant to avoid.

Because the conviction is identical to the charged offense and has limited bearing on truthfulness, the prejudice likely outweighs the probative value. **Admitting it was likely an abuse of discretion**, though a court could reasonably reach the opposite result given how central Dex's credibility was. If admitted, the impeachment should be limited to the name, date and sentence.

**(ii) The 2023 shoplifting misdemeanor.** A misdemeanor is admissible to impeach only under FRE 609(a)(2), which requires that proving its elements required a dishonest act or false statement, such as perjury, fraud or false pretenses. Most federal courts hold that theft offenses such as shoplifting, while morally dishonest, do not require proof of deceit or falsehood. A misdemeanor fails 609(a)(1), which requires a crime punishable by more than one year. **Admitting the shoplifting conviction was error.**

**Overall:** items (a), (c) and (d) (for intent) were properly admitted; (b) and both convictions in (e) were likely erroneous, and on appeal the question will be whether the errors were harmless given the 911 call and the text message.

**Common mistakes**
1. Admitting a prior identification when the declarant does not testify. FRE 801(d)(1)(C) requires a testifying declarant.
2. Treating every 911 call as nontestimonial (or every statement to police as testimonial). Apply the primary-purpose test to the facts.
3. Admitting other-act evidence for "identity" without a signature-level similarity.
4. Using the ordinary FRE 403 test for a testifying defendant's felony. The defendant gets the more protective 609(a)(1)(B) balance.
5. Treating theft as a crime of dishonesty under 609(a)(2). It requires deceit or false statement as an element.
