# E-XO-017 — The neighbor's dealership (Torts + Remedies)

## Facts

Iris Tanaka owns a small vineyard. Her neighbor, Dmitri Volkov, owns and runs Volkov Farm Equipment, a dealership that sells and repairs new and used tractors.

In May 2026 Iris left her restored 1948 tractor, a collector's model, at Dmitri's dealership for an engine repair. Dmitri displays used tractors for sale on a lot next to his repair shop. On June 2, before the repair was finished, Dmitri sold Iris's tractor from his lot for $30,000 to Ken, a collector from another state. Ken paid in full, believed Dmitri owned the tractor, and took it home. Dmitri told Iris the tractor had been "stolen from our lot." Similar restored tractors sell for $28,000 to $32,000.

Dmitri deposited the $30,000 into his business checking account, which then held $5,000 of his own money. On June 4 he withdrew $28,000 and bought a 1970 Ford Bronco, a collectible vehicle now worth $40,000. On June 20 he withdrew $6,000 for a vacation, leaving $1,000 in the account. He later deposited $9,000 of sales receipts; the account now holds $10,000.

In July Iris lent Dmitri her grandfather's hand-carved oak wine press, built in 1921, so that he could display it at his booth at the county harvest fair. Iris's family has used the press every harvest for a century. An antiques dealer values it at $6,000. After the fair, Iris asked for the press back. Dmitri replied that she had "abandoned it" at the fair and that it was now his. On September 1 Iris learned that Dmitri had listed the press on an online auction site. Bidding closes on September 15.

Also in July, while Iris was away, Dmitri took her flatbed trailer from her open barn, without asking, to haul hay for a weekend. He returned it with a bent axle. The repair cost $1,800, and Iris paid $400 to rent a replacement trailer for the four days the repair took.

In August Ken's insurer told Iris that Dmitri had sold the tractor, not lost it. When Iris confronted Dmitri, he laughed and said, "Prove it." Dmitri's net worth is about $1.5 million.

It is now September 3. Iris has hired a lawyer and intends to sue immediately.

## Call of the Question

1. What tort claims does Iris have against Dmitri, and can she recover the tractor from Ken? Discuss.
2. What remedies can Iris obtain for the tractor, the wine press and the trailer? Discuss.
3. Can Iris recover punitive damages? Discuss.

## Model Answer

### 1. Iris's tort claims; Ken

**A. The tractor: conversion.** A defendant is liable for conversion if he intentionally exercises dominion or control over a chattel that so seriously interferes with another's right to control it that he may justly be required to pay its full value. Selling or otherwise transferring another's chattel is a classic serious interference, and good faith is no defense.

Iris delivered the tractor to Dmitri only for repair, a bailment. On June 2 Dmitri sold it as his own and kept the price, then lied that it was "stolen." That is an intentional, complete and permanent interference with Iris's ownership. **Dmitri converted the tractor on June 2.**

**B. Can Iris recover the tractor from Ken?** A buyer generally gets only the title its seller had, but entrusting goods to a merchant who deals in goods of that kind gives the merchant power to transfer all the entruster's rights to a buyer in the ordinary course of business (UCC § 2-403(2)). Entrusting includes any delivery to the merchant, regardless of any condition the parties agreed on, such as "for repair only."

- *Entrustment to a merchant.* Iris left the tractor at a dealership that sells used tractors from a lot next to its repair shop.
- *Goods of that kind.* Iris may argue that a restored collector's tractor differs from the dealership's ordinary stock. But it is still a used tractor, the very kind of goods Dmitri sells.
- *Buyer in the ordinary course.* Ken bought from the dealer's sales lot, paid full market price, and honestly believed Dmitri owned the tractor.

**Ken took Iris's title.** Iris cannot replevy the tractor from Ken, and Ken is not a converter. Her remedies are against Dmitri.

**C. The wine press: conversion.** A bailee who refuses to return a chattel after a proper demand, claims to own it, or tries to sell it exercises serious dominion over it. Dmitri did all three: he refused Iris's request, claimed she had "abandoned" the press, and listed it for auction. Abandonment requires an intent to give up ownership, but Iris lent the press for a fair display and asked for it back afterward, and it is a century-old family heirloom she uses every year. **Dmitri converted the press.**

**D. The trailer: trespass to chattels.** A defendant is liable for trespass to chattels if he intentionally dispossesses another of a chattel or uses or intermeddles with it, causing actual harm: damage, loss of use for a substantial time, or other harm. Dmitri took the trailer without permission and used it for a weekend, which is intentional dispossession. He returned it with a bent axle, and Iris lost its use for four more days. Iris may argue conversion, but Dmitri returned the trailer after a short use with damage costing $1,800 to repair. That is not serious enough to require a forced sale. **This is trespass to chattels, not conversion.**

### 2. Remedies

**A. The tractor: damages or restitution of the proceeds.** For conversion, the plaintiff recovers the chattel's fair market value at the time and place of conversion, plus interest; California presumes the same measure (Civ. Code § 3336). Ken's $30,000 arm's-length purchase, within the $28,000–$32,000 range, is the best evidence of value on June 2: **$30,000 plus interest.** Alternatively, Iris may waive the tort and recover Dmitri's gain from the wrong, the $30,000 in sale proceeds. Both measures produce about $30,000, so Iris should look to the property Dmitri bought with the money.

**B. Constructive trust over the Bronco.** A constructive trust compels a defendant who holds specific property acquired by wrongdoing, or its traceable product, to convey it to the plaintiff. Because the plaintiff takes the property itself, she captures any increase in value and takes priority over the defendant's unsecured creditors. The plaintiff must trace her property into the asset, and a bona fide purchaser cuts off the claim.

*Tracing.* When a wrongdoer mixes the claimant's money with his own, he is presumed to spend his own money first, and the claimant's claim against the account cannot exceed its lowest intermediate balance. But if the wrongdoer buys an asset from the mixed fund and then dissipates the rest, the claimant may trace into the asset.

| Date | Transaction | Balance |
|---|---|---|
| June 2 | Deposit of tractor proceeds ($30,000) onto Dmitri's $5,000 | $35,000 |
| June 4 | Bronco purchase ($28,000) | $7,000 |
| June 20 | Vacation ($6,000) | $1,000 (lowest balance) |
| Later | Dmitri's sales receipts ($9,000) | $10,000 |

- *The Bronco.* If Dmitri could use the "own money first" presumption, the Bronco would be partly his ($5,000) and partly Iris's ($23,000). But the presumption exists to protect the claimant, not the wrongdoer. Because Dmitri later spent the rest of the fund, Iris may treat the $28,000 that bought the Bronco as her money and claim the **whole Bronco, now worth $40,000**. A court that instead divides the asset proportionally would give her about 86% of it ($30,000 of the $35,000 fund), about $34,300.
- *The account.* Iris's claim against the account is capped at the lowest intermediate balance, **$1,000**. Dmitri's later $9,000 deposit of his own receipts does not restore her money unless he intended it as repayment.
- *Other requirements.* Dmitri is a conscious wrongdoer, who must give up the appreciation. He still holds the Bronco, so no bona fide purchaser cuts off the trace. Dmitri can pay a $30,000 judgment, so the reason to prefer equity here is the $12,000 appreciation, which a conscious wrongdoer may not keep.
- *Equitable lien.* An equitable lien secures a money claim on the traced asset and is better when the asset has lost value, because the plaintiff can sell it and still sue for any shortfall. The Bronco has gained value, so a constructive trust is better.

**Iris should seek a constructive trust over the Bronco ($40,000) plus $1,000 from the account, rather than $30,000 in damages.**

**C. The wine press: replevin.** Replevin (in California, claim and delivery) is a legal action to recover possession of specific personal property wrongfully held, plus damages for its detention. A writ of possession may issue before judgment after notice and a hearing at which the plaintiff shows the probable validity of its claim, usually on posting an undertaking, and the sheriff enforces it.

- *Right to immediate possession.* The bailment ended when Iris demanded the press back.
- *Wrongful detention.* Dmitri refuses to return it and claims to own it.
- *Specific chattel.* The press is identifiable and still in Dmitri's possession.

Market-value damages of $6,000 would not replace a 100-year-old press her family has used every harvest, which is exactly why getting the thing back matters. **Iris should seek a writ of possession and damages for the period of detention.**

**D. The wine press: TRO and preliminary injunction against the auction.** A writ of possession requires a noticed hearing, and bidding closes on September 15, twelve days from now. Iris should first ask for a temporary restraining order and then a preliminary injunction barring the sale.

- *Irreparable harm.* Once sold online, the press may go to an unknown bidder in another state. A buyer could try to claim good title, although § 2-403(2) likely would not apply because Dmitri does not deal in antique wine presses; even so, recovering the press from a distant buyer would be slow and uncertain. A one-of-a-kind heirloom cannot be replaced with money.
- *Likelihood of success.* Strong: Iris lent the press, demanded it back, and Dmitri's abandonment claim is implausible.
- *Balance of hardships and public interest.* An injunction costs Dmitri only an auction sale of property he does not own; denying it risks Iris losing the press permanently. No public interest favors the sale.
- *California standard; status quo.* A California court weighs the likelihood of success against the relative interim harm on a sliding scale (*IT Corp.*). The order sought, "do not sell," is prohibitory and preserves the status quo, so no heightened showing is needed.
- *Procedure.* A TRO may issue without notice only on a showing that great or irreparable injury will result before a noticed hearing can be held. Iris must post an undertaking when the injunction issues (CCP § 529).

**A TRO and then a preliminary injunction are likely**, followed by replevin or a permanent order for the return of the press.

**E. The trailer: damages.** For trespass to chattels, the plaintiff recovers the cost of repair or the diminution in value, plus the value of lost use. **Iris recovers $1,800 for repair plus $400 for the rental: $2,200.**

**F. Election of remedies.** A plaintiff may not recover twice for the same loss. Iris must choose, for the tractor, between $30,000 in damages (or restitution of the proceeds) and the constructive trust over the Bronco and the $1,000; she cannot have both. She may combine that choice with replevin of the press, the trailer damages and punitive damages, because each covers a different harm.

### 3. Punitive damages

Punitive damages may be awarded for tortious conduct that is malicious, oppressive, fraudulent or in reckless disregard of the plaintiff's rights; they are not available for breach of contract. In California, the plaintiff must prove oppression, fraud or malice by clear and convincing evidence (Civ. Code § 3294). Malice includes despicable conduct carried on with a willful and conscious disregard of others' rights, and fraud includes intentional concealment of a material fact intended to deprive a person of property.

- *Tort and mental state.* Conversion and trespass to chattels are torts. Dmitri sold an entrusted tractor, falsely told Iris it was stolen to conceal the sale, claimed her heirloom as "abandoned" and listed it for sale, took her trailer without asking, and responded to her confrontation with "Prove it." That is a deliberate pattern of taking her property and lying about it: malice and fraud, provable by clear and convincing evidence.
- *Actual damages.* California requires actual damages as a predicate. Iris has them on the trailer ($2,200), and on the conversion and detention claims. If she elects a constructive trust for the tractor, she should still obtain compensatory damages on at least one tort claim so the punitive award has a base.
- *Financial condition.* California requires evidence of the defendant's financial condition (*Adams v. Murakami*). Dmitri's net worth of about $1.5 million supplies it.
- *Due process.* An award is reviewed for reprehensibility, its ratio to the harm, and comparable civil penalties (*BMW v. Gore*; *State Farm v. Campbell*). The harm is economic, which lowers reprehensibility, but the conduct was intentional, deceitful and repeated. A single-digit ratio to Iris's compensatory damages would likely survive review.

**Iris can likely recover punitive damages.**

**Common mistakes**
1. Treating Ken as a converter. Entrustment to a dealer in tractors gave Ken good title.
2. Letting the wrongdoer's "own money first" presumption defeat tracing into the Bronco.
3. Ignoring the lowest intermediate balance and claiming the whole $10,000 account.
4. Seeking only replevin for the press without interim relief against a sale twelve days away.
5. Combining full tractor damages with a constructive trust over the Bronco.
