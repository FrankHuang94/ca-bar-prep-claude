## Facts

Eleanor, a 79-year-old retired teacher and widow, had used Graham as her lawyer for 15 years, and the two were close friends. Graham's daughter, Fiona, visited Eleanor weekly and ran errands for her; Eleanor often called Fiona "the granddaughter I never had." Eleanor's closest relatives were her two nephews, Paul and Simon.

In April 2024 Eleanor asked Graham to prepare a revocable living trust. She said, "I'd like to leave something to Fiona." Graham replied, "Seventy-five thousand dollars would pay off her student loans," and Eleanor agreed. Graham drafted the trust to provide that on Eleanor's death the trustee would distribute $75,000 to Fiona and the rest equally to Paul and Simon. The trust names Graham as sole successor trustee, to be paid "for all services as trustee at the trustee's usual hourly rate as an attorney," which is $500 an hour. Graham did not discuss any other choice of trustee with Eleanor, did not explain how much the trusteeship might pay him, and obtained no written consent from her about it.

Before the signing, Graham asked Nina, a lawyer who shares his office suite and with whom he regularly exchanges referrals, to review the trust with Eleanor. Fiona drove Eleanor to Nina's office and sat in on the 20-minute meeting "to help Eleanor remember things." Nina then signed and gave Eleanor a document titled "Certificate of Independent Review." Eleanor, who was fully competent, signed the trust in May 2024 and transferred her house and investments, worth $1,500,000, into it.

Eleanor died in March 2026, and Graham became trustee. In the first six months he paid himself $40,000 in trustee fees and paid his own law firm $60,000 for "legal services to the trust." Both invoices bill time for many of the same tasks, including collecting Eleanor's accounts and listing the house for sale. He also distributed $75,000 to Fiona.

When Paul and Simon asked about the fees and the gift to Fiona, Graham told them, "I'm the lawyer for the trust, so I'm looking out for both of you. There's no need for you to hire your own lawyers. The gift to Fiona is perfectly legal because an independent lawyer signed off on it." Paul and Simon have now petitioned the court to remove Graham and to recover the payments.

## Call of the Question

1. Is the $75,000 gift to Fiona effective? May Graham continue as trustee, and may he keep the $40,000 trustee fee and the $60,000 his firm billed? Answer according to California law.
2. What ethical violations, if any, has Graham committed? Answer according to California and ABA authorities.

## Model Answer

### 1. The trust

**A. The gift to Fiona**

*Rule.* A donative transfer in a will or trust to the person who drafted the instrument, or to a person related by blood or marriage to the drafter, is presumed to be the product of fraud or undue influence (Prob. Code § 21380(a)). The presumption ordinarily may be rebutted only by clear and convincing evidence, but for the drafter and persons related to or associated with the drafter it is **conclusive** (§ 21380(c)). These rules apply to trust instruments as they do to wills.

*Application.* Graham drafted the trust, and Fiona is his daughter. The gift to her is presumed to be the product of fraud or undue influence, and because she is the drafter's relative the presumption is conclusive: evidence of Eleanor's genuine affection for Fiona ("the granddaughter I never had") cannot save the gift. The amount came from Graham's own suggestion, which underlines the risk the statute addresses.

**B. Exceptions**

*Relationship to the transferor.* The presumption does not apply to a transferee related to the transferor within the fourth degree, or to an instrument drafted by such a relative (§ 21382). Fiona and Graham are not related to Eleanor; "like a granddaughter" is not a legal relationship. The gift ($75,000) is far above the small-gift exception.

*Certificate of independent review.* The presumption does not apply if an independent attorney reviews the instrument, counsels the transferor about the nature and consequences of the transfer outside the presence of the beneficiary, attempts to determine whether it is the product of fraud or undue influence, and signs and delivers the statutory certificate (§ 21384).
- *Fiona's argument.* Nina reviewed the trust with Eleanor and signed a certificate.
- *Why it fails.* Fiona, the very beneficiary, sat in on the meeting, so Eleanor was not counseled outside the beneficiary's presence. Nina's independence is also doubtful: she shares Graham's suite and exchanges referrals with him, so she had a professional relationship with the beneficiary's father, the drafter. A 20-minute meeting in front of the beneficiary is not the independent counseling the statute requires.

**No exception applies; the gift fails.**

**C. Effect.** A transfer that fails under § 21380 passes as if the beneficiary had predeceased the transferor without spouse, domestic partner or issue (§ 21386), so the $75,000 falls into the remainder and passes equally to **Paul and Simon**. Graham distributed the money to Fiona anyway. A beneficiary who receives an improper distribution must restore it, so Fiona must repay $75,000 to the trust; Graham is also liable for the loss if it cannot be recovered from her. A beneficiary who unsuccessfully defends a presumptively invalid gift may also bear the costs and attorney's fees of the proceeding (§ 21380(d)).

**D. May Graham continue as trustee?**

*Rule.* A sole trustee who is a person described in § 21380(a), such as the drafter of the instrument, may be removed on that ground unless the court finds that the settlor intended that person to serve and that this intent was not the product of fraud or undue influence (§ 15642). Breach of trust and excessive compensation are also grounds for removal.

*Application.* Graham drafted the trust that names him sole trustee. He will argue that Eleanor, a competent client of 15 years and a close friend, naturally wanted him to serve. But Graham never discussed alternatives (a professional trustee, a bank, one of the nephews), never explained the cost of paying an attorney's hourly rate for trustee work, and obtained no independent advice about the appointment; the "independent review" concerned the gift and was itself defective. Even apart from his status, Graham has paid himself duplicative fees and made an improper $75,000 distribution.

**The court will likely remove Graham** and appoint a successor trustee.

**E. The $40,000 and the $60,000**

*Dual compensation.* Notwithstanding any contrary trust provision, a trustee who is an attorney may not receive both trustee compensation and compensation for legal services to the trust (including through his law firm) unless he waives trustee compensation or obtains approval of the dual compensation after full disclosure (§ 15687). Graham took $40,000 as trustee and had his own firm bill $60,000 for legal work, without a waiver or approval. **He may not keep both.** At most he may keep one form of compensation, and only to the extent it is reasonable.

*Loyalty.* A trustee must administer the trust solely in the beneficiaries' interest (§ 16002), and hiring his own firm, which profits from the engagement, is a conflict of interest that requires authorization, informed beneficiary consent or court approval (§ 16004). The trust authorizes trustee compensation, not legal work by his firm. And because both invoices charge for the same tasks, at least part of the billing is a double charge for one service.

*Conclusion.* Graham must restore the duplicated charges; the court will likely require him to disgorge the $60,000 of legal fees, or the trustee fees, and reduce what remains to reasonable compensation for work actually done once.

**F. Remedies.** On the nephews' petition the court may remove Graham, compel him to restore the $75,000 (if Fiona does not) and the improper fees with interest, and reduce or deny his compensation (§§ 16420, 16440).

### 2. Graham's ethical violations

**Sources of law.** California lawyers are governed by the California Rules of Professional Conduct (CRPC), the State Bar Act (Bus. & Prof. Code) and California case law; the ABA Model Rules (MR) are persuasive only.

**A. The gift to Fiona**

*ABA (MR 1.8(c)).* A lawyer may not solicit a substantial gift from a client, including a testamentary gift, for the lawyer or a person related to the lawyer, or prepare an instrument giving the lawyer or a related person a substantial gift, unless the recipient is related to the client. A lawyer's child is a related person.

*California (CRPC 1.8.3).* The same prohibitions apply, but a lawyer may prepare such an instrument if the client has been advised by an independent lawyer who has provided a certificate of independent review that complies with Probate Code § 21384.

*Application.*
- *Solicitation.* Eleanor raised the idea of a gift to Fiona, so Graham did not initiate it. But by naming the amount ("$75,000 would pay off her student loans"), he shaped the size of a substantial gift to his own daughter. That is arguably solicitation, at least of the amount.
- *Preparing the instrument.* Graham himself drafted the trust giving his daughter $75,000, a substantial gift. Fiona is not related to Eleanor. Under the ABA rule that ends the analysis. Under California's rule, Graham can rely only on the certificate route, and the certificate does not comply with § 21384 because the beneficiary was present and the reviewer's independence is doubtful.

*Conclusion.* **Graham violated MR 1.8(c) and CRPC 1.8.3.**

**B. Naming himself trustee at his hourly rate**

*Rule.* A lawyer has a material-limitation conflict when there is a significant risk that the lawyer's own interests will limit the representation; the lawyer may proceed only with the client's informed written consent (California) or informed consent confirmed in writing (ABA) (CRPC 1.7(b); MR 1.7(a)(2)). Neither system flatly bars a lawyer from seeking appointment as a client's fiduciary, but the ABA comment to MR 1.8 explains that, when the lawyer's interest in the appointment may limit his advice about whom to choose, the lawyer must disclose the nature and extent of his financial interest and the availability of alternative candidates.

*Application.* Graham's financial interest in being named sole trustee at $500 an hour was obvious, and it created a significant risk that his advice about who should serve, and on what terms, would be shaped by his own interest. He discussed no alternatives, did not explain what the appointment might cost the trust, and obtained no consent in writing. **Violation of both rules.** His long friendship with Eleanor does not cure the conflict; it made independent advice more important.

**C. The overlapping bills**

*Rule.* ABA: a lawyer may not charge or collect an unreasonable fee (MR 1.5(a)). California: a lawyer may not charge or collect an unconscionable or illegal fee (CRPC 1.5(a)–(b)). Billing twice for the same work is also conduct involving dishonesty or misrepresentation (MR 8.4(c); CRPC 8.4(c); B&P § 6106).

*Application.* Graham's trustee invoices and his firm's legal invoices charge for the same tasks. Charging twice for one service is unreasonable under the ABA standard and, because it is illegal under § 15687 and involves concealed duplication, likely unconscionable under California's higher threshold. Graham may argue that trustee work and legal work are different roles, but identical tasks billed twice are not two services. **Likely violation of both rules**, and possibly an act of moral turpitude in California if the duplication was knowing.

**D. Statements to Paul and Simon**

*Unrepresented persons.* In dealing on behalf of a client with an unrepresented person, a lawyer may not state or imply that he is disinterested, must correct any misunderstanding about his role, and may not give legal advice, other than the advice to obtain counsel, when the person's interests conflict or may conflict with the client's (CRPC 4.3; MR 4.3). As trustee, Graham owes fiduciary duties to the nephews; but as a lawyer he represents himself as trustee, not them, and his interests in keeping his fees and defending Fiona's gift are directly adverse to theirs. By saying he was "looking out for both of you" and that there was "no need" for lawyers, he implied he was disinterested and advised unrepresented persons with conflicting interests not to get counsel. **Violation of both rules.**

*Truthfulness.* A lawyer may not knowingly make a false statement of material fact or law to a third person (CRPC 4.1; MR 4.1) or engage in dishonesty, fraud, deceit or (California) reckless or intentional misrepresentation (CRPC 8.4(c); MR 8.4(c)). Telling the nephews that the gift was "perfectly legal because an independent lawyer signed off" misstated the law: a gift to the drafter's daughter is conclusively presumed invalid unless a compliant certificate exists, and Graham, an experienced estate lawyer who arranged the review, knew how it was conducted. **Likely violation of both rules**, at least under California's "reckless misrepresentation" standard.

### Conclusion

The gift to Fiona fails under § 21380 because she is the drafter's daughter and the certificate is defective; the $75,000 goes to Paul and Simon, and Fiona must repay it. Graham will likely be removed as a disqualified sole trustee who has breached his duties, and he cannot keep both his trustee fees and his firm's legal fees. Ethically, Graham violated the gift rules (MR 1.8(c); CRPC 1.8.3), the personal-interest conflict rules, the fee rules, and the rules on dealing with unrepresented persons and truthfulness.

**Common mistakes**
1. Accepting any document labeled "Certificate of Independent Review." The independent lawyer must counsel the transferor outside the beneficiary's presence.
2. Treating Fiona as a relative of Eleanor because Eleanor called her a granddaughter.
3. Assuming a lawyer may never serve as a client's trustee. The problem is the undisclosed conflict and, under the Probate Code, the removal ground and the dual-compensation bar.
4. Applying California's certificate exception to the ABA analysis. MR 1.8(c) has no such exception.
5. Forgetting that Graham, as lawyer for himself as trustee, does not represent the beneficiaries and must not give them legal advice.
