# E-XO-005 — Orchid Robotics (Business Associations + Professional Responsibility)

## Facts

Orchid Robotics, Inc. ("Orchid") is a privately held California corporation with about 40 shareholders. It designs robotic arms used in warehouses. Its five directors are: Ray, the CEO, who owns 30% of the shares; Grace, a partner at the law firm Liu & Park LLP, which is Orchid's outside general counsel; Hal and Ines, outside investors; and Joe, Orchid's vice president of engineering, who reports to Ray and is married to Ray's sister. In 2023 Orchid issued Grace 2% of its shares in place of $50,000 of unpaid legal fees; Grace prepared the paperwork herself.

**The lease.** Ray owns 100% of TanBuild LLC, which owns a warehouse. In March 2025 Ray proposed that Orchid lease TanBuild's warehouse for ten years at $40,000 per month. Before the board meeting Ray asked Grace to "look over the lease for legal form," and she spent about ten minutes on it. At the 20-minute meeting Ray said he "has an interest in the landlord" but did not mention that a broker had recently told him comparable space nearby rents for about $28,000 per month. No director asked about market rent. Ray abstained, and the other four directors, including Grace and Joe, approved the lease.

**The Medix contract.** In June 2025, Medix Corp., a hospital-equipment maker, telephoned Ray at Orchid's office, asking whether Orchid could design robotic arms for surgical carts. Orchid had never sold medical products, but its engineers had the core technology. Ray formed a new company, TanMed LLC, which he owns, and signed a $3 million design contract with Medix. He did not tell the board. Two Orchid engineers worked on the TanMed project on weekends using Orchid's software licenses.

**Grace's involvement.** In August 2025 Orchid's vice president of sales told Grace, in her capacity as Orchid's counsel, what he had learned about TanMed. Grace confronted Ray, who said: "Keep this between us. TanMed is going to need a lawyer, and your firm can have all of its legal work." Liu & Park then agreed to represent TanMed in negotiating a parts-supply agreement for the Medix project. Grace did not tell the board about TanMed or about her firm's new engagement.

**The shareholder demand.** In January 2026, Sue, an Orchid shareholder, learned of the lease and the Medix contract and sent the board a written demand that Orchid sue Ray. Ray asked Grace to represent him personally in defending against Sue's threatened derivative suit while Liu & Park continued as Orchid's general counsel. Grace agreed, without consulting the other directors.

## Call of the Question

1. Did Ray and Grace breach any fiduciary duties they owe Orchid as directors? Discuss.
2. What ethical violations, if any, has Grace committed? Answer according to California and ABA authorities.

## Model Answer

### Call 1 — Directors' fiduciary duties

Directors owe the corporation duties of care and loyalty. Breach claims belong to Orchid and would be asserted derivatively (as Sue proposes).

#### A. Ray — the TanBuild lease (self-dealing)

**Rule.** A director breaches the duty of loyalty by using his position to benefit himself at the corporation's expense. A transaction between the corporation and a director (or an entity in which the director has a material financial interest) is protected from challenge on the ground of the interest only if, after **full disclosure of the material facts**, it is approved by a majority of disinterested (qualified) directors or by disinterested shareholders, or if it is **fair** to the corporation (MBCA §§ 8.60–8.63; *Benihana*). California's Corp. Code § 310 is similar but stricter: board approval protects the transaction only if the material facts were fully disclosed to the board, the vote was sufficient without the interested director, and the transaction was **just and reasonable** to the corporation when approved.

**Application.**
- *Interest.* Ray owns 100% of TanBuild, the landlord, so he stands on both sides.
- *Disclosure.* Ray disclosed only that he "has an interest in the landlord." Material facts include anything a reasonable director would consider important, and the market rent (about $28,000 versus $40,000) is the most important fact about a lease. By withholding the broker's information, Ray failed to make full disclosure.
- *Disinterested approval.* Hal, Ines and Grace had no financial interest in the lease, but Joe is Ray's brother-in-law and his subordinate, so he is not independent. Even counting only Hal, Ines and Grace, their approval cannot cleanse a transaction approved without full disclosure.
- *Fairness.* The rent is about 43% above market, roughly $144,000 a year and $1.44 million over ten years. Ray cannot show the lease was fair or, under § 310, just and reasonable.

**Conclusion:** Ray breached his duty of loyalty. Orchid may rescind the lease or recover the excess rent, and the business judgment rule does not protect a conflicted transaction.

#### B. Ray — the Medix contract (corporate opportunity)

**Rule.** A director or officer may not usurp a business opportunity that belongs to the corporation. Under the line-of-business test (*Guth v. Loft*), an opportunity belongs to the corporation if it is within the corporation's line of business, the corporation has an interest or expectancy in it, and it is financially able to take it. Under the ALI/MBCA approach, an opportunity offered to a senior executive in that capacity, or closely related to the corporation's business, belongs to the corporation. The fiduciary must first offer the opportunity to the corporation with full disclosure and may take it only if the corporation, through disinterested decision-makers, rejects it.

**Application.**
- Medix called Ray *at Orchid's office* asking whether *Orchid* could do the work, so the opportunity came to Ray in his corporate capacity.
- Ray will argue that Orchid had never sold medical products, so surgical-cart arms are outside its line of business. But Orchid designs robotic arms and its engineers had the core technology; adapting it for carts is a natural extension, closely related to its business.
- Nothing suggests Orchid lacked the financial ability to take a design contract; Ray, as CEO, never gave it the chance.
- Ray never disclosed or offered the opportunity to the board. He also diverted Orchid's resources (two engineers and software licenses) to TanMed.

**Conclusion:** Ray usurped a corporate opportunity and misused corporate property. Orchid may obtain a constructive trust over the Medix contract and TanMed's profits, or damages.

#### C. Grace — duty of care and the business judgment rule

**Rule.** A director must act in good faith, in the corporation's best interests, and with the care, **including reasonable inquiry**, that an ordinarily prudent person in a like position would use (MBCA § 8.30; Cal. Corp. Code § 309). Directors may rely on officers and experts only if reliance is reasonable and they lack knowledge making it unwarranted. The business judgment rule presumes that disinterested directors made an informed, good-faith decision; it is rebutted by a failure to become reasonably informed (gross negligence, *Smith v. Van Gorkom*).

**Application.** Grace knew Ray was on the other side of the lease, which is the moment when a director must ask questions. She spent ten minutes reviewing a ten-year, $4.8 million lease for "legal form" only, and at a 20-minute meeting she did not ask the most obvious question (what is market rent?). Her reliance on Ray was unwarranted because he was the interested party. Grace may argue that she was asked only to check legal form and that she relied in good faith on management, but a director cannot shift her own duty of inquiry to the conflicted officer. The business judgment rule does not protect an uninformed approval of a conflicted transaction.

**Exculpation.** If Orchid's articles contain an exculpation clause, it could shield Grace from money damages for ordinary breaches of care, but under California's § 204(a)(10) exculpation does not cover reckless disregard of a serious risk to the corporation or approval of § 310 transactions, and no exculpation clause covers loyalty breaches.

**Conclusion:** Grace likely breached her duty of care in approving the lease.

#### D. Grace — duty of loyalty and good faith

**Rule.** The duty of loyalty requires a director to act in good faith for the corporation and not to use her position for personal benefit; a conscious failure to act in the face of a known duty is bad faith (*Stone v. Ritter*).

**Application.** In August 2025 Grace learned, as a director and as Orchid's counsel, that the CEO was diverting a corporate opportunity and corporate resources. Instead of informing the board, she agreed to keep it "between us," and her firm accepted TanMed's legal work, a personal financial benefit from the very scheme that was harming Orchid. Grace may argue that she simply took on a new client, but accepting business from the wrongdoer in exchange for silence puts her interest ahead of Orchid's and helps Ray continue the usurpation.

**Conclusion:** Grace breached her duty of loyalty and good faith. Exculpation would not protect her.

### Call 2 — Grace's ethical violations

California lawyers are governed by the California Rules of Professional Conduct (CRPC) and the State Bar Act; the ABA Model Rules (MR) are persuasive.

#### A. Who is the client; reporting up and reporting out

**ABA:** Under MR 1.13(a), a lawyer retained by an organization represents the organization acting through its authorized constituents. Under MR 1.13(b), if the lawyer knows that an officer is acting in a matter related to the representation in a way that violates a legal obligation to the organization and is likely to cause it substantial injury, the lawyer must proceed as reasonably necessary in the organization's best interest, ordinarily by referring the matter to higher authority, including the board. Under MR 1.13(c), if the highest authority fails to act on a clear violation, the lawyer *may* reveal information outside the organization to the extent reasonably necessary to prevent substantial injury to it.

**California:** Rule 1.13(a)–(b) are the same on reporting up. But California rule 1.13(c) forbids revealing information protected by B&P § 6068(e); a California lawyer may not report out, and her options are to keep urging the right course and to withdraw.

**Application.** Orchid, not Ray, is Grace's client. The vice president's report concerned Ray's breach of his fiduciary duties to Orchid (a violation of a legal obligation to the organization), which is causing substantial injury (a lost $3 million contract and misused resources). Grace was required at least to report it to the board, which was the highest authority able to act. Ray's request to "keep this between us" does not bind her: the information belongs to the client Orchid, and telling Orchid's board is not a breach of confidentiality. She reported nothing. The reporting-out difference does not matter here because Grace never reached that step, but if the board had refused to act, she could have reported out under the ABA rule (if the other conditions were met) and could not under California's rule 1.13(c) and § 6068(e).

**Conclusion:** Grace violated MR 1.13(b) and CRPC rule 1.13(b).

#### B. Conflict — representing TanMed

**ABA:** MR 1.7(a) forbids a representation that is directly adverse to a current client or that carries a significant risk of being materially limited by duties to another client or by the lawyer's own interests, unless the lawyer reasonably believes she can represent each client competently and diligently and each gives informed consent, confirmed in writing. MR 1.8(b) forbids using information relating to a client's representation to the client's disadvantage without informed consent.

**California:** Rules 1.7(a)–(b) and 1.8.2 are the same in substance, but consent must be informed *written* consent.

**Application.** TanMed is exploiting an opportunity that belongs to Orchid; Orchid has claims against TanMed and Ray for the Medix contract and profits. Helping TanMed perform the Medix deal is adverse to Orchid's interests, and Liu & Park's representation of Orchid is materially limited by its new duties to TanMed and by Grace's own interest in the new fees. Grace learned of TanMed through Orchid's vice president, in confidence, and then used that knowledge to her firm's benefit and to Orchid's disadvantage. No consent was sought from Orchid, and none could properly come from Ray. The conflict is likely not consentable, since Grace could not reasonably believe she could protect Orchid while helping the entity that took its opportunity.

**Conclusion:** Grace violated MR 1.7 and 1.8(b), and CRPC rules 1.7 and 1.8.2. Accepting the work as the price of silence also reflects dishonesty (MR 8.4(c); CRPC rule 8.4(c)).

#### C. Dual representation of Orchid and Ray in the derivative dispute

**ABA:** Under MR 1.13(g), a lawyer for an organization may also represent a director or officer, subject to MR 1.7; if the organization's consent is required, it must be given by an appropriate official other than the individual to be represented. The comments to MR 1.13 note that when a derivative claim involves serious charges of wrongdoing by those in control, the organization's lawyer's duties to the organization conflict with representing the accused insiders, and MR 1.7 governs.

**California:** Rule 1.13 likewise permits dual representation subject to rule 1.7, with consent from an appropriate constituent other than the individual, and informed written consent.

**Application.** Sue's demand accuses Ray of self-dealing and usurpation, serious charges. Orchid's interest is to evaluate and, if warranted, pursue those claims against Ray; Ray's interest is to defeat them. A lawyer cannot advise Orchid's board on whether to sue Ray while defending Ray. Grace agreed without consulting the other directors, so no disinterested official consented, and the conflict is likely nonconsentable in any event. Her own exposure as a director who approved the lease adds a personal-interest conflict.

**Conclusion:** Grace violated MR 1.7 and 1.13(g), and CRPC rules 1.7 and 1.13.

#### D. Stock in place of fees — business transaction with a client

**ABA:** MR 1.8(a) forbids a lawyer from entering a business transaction with a client or knowingly acquiring an ownership interest adverse to the client unless the terms are fair and reasonable and fully disclosed in writing, the client is advised in writing to seek independent counsel and given a reasonable opportunity to do so, and the client gives informed consent in a writing signed by the client.

**California:** Rule 1.8.1 is the same, with informed written consent.

**Application.** Taking 2% of Orchid's shares for $50,000 of fees is a business transaction with a client. Grace prepared the paperwork herself; nothing suggests she advised Orchid in writing to consult independent counsel or obtained written consent to the essential terms, or that anyone checked whether 2% was a fair price for $50,000 of fees. Owning shares also gives her a personal stake in Orchid's affairs that must be considered under rule 1.7.

**Conclusion:** Unless the 2023 transaction complied with these formalities, Grace violated MR 1.8(a) and CRPC rule 1.8.1.

#### E. Lawyer as director; competence and candid advice

A lawyer may serve as a director of a client, but the dual role can compromise independence and privilege (MR 1.7 cmt.). Here it did: Grace approved, as a director, a lease she had reviewed as counsel, and she is now defending the officer whose conduct she approved. As Orchid's lawyer, she also owed competent representation and candid advice (MR 1.1, 2.1; CRPC rules 1.1, 2.1). Advising on a conflicted lease without telling the board what a conflicted transaction requires (full disclosure, disinterested approval, fairness) was at least a lapse in competence. Under the ABA, a single serious lapse can violate MR 1.1; California disciplines only intentional, reckless or repeated failures, which may be met here given the pattern.

### Summary

- **Ray** breached the duty of loyalty (self-dealing lease; Medix opportunity).
- **Grace** breached the duty of care (uninformed approval) and the duty of loyalty (concealment for her firm's benefit).
- **Grace's ethics:** failure to report up (both systems); conflicts in representing TanMed and Ray (both; California requires written consent); misuse of Orchid's information; a likely noncompliant stock-for-fees transaction; and competence lapses.

**Common mistakes**
1. Treating disclosure of "an interest" as full disclosure. The material facts include the terms' fairness (market rent).
2. Counting Joe as a disinterested director. A brother-in-law who reports to Ray is not independent.
3. Saying Grace owed Ray confidentiality. Her client is Orchid; reporting to Orchid's board is not a disclosure outside the client.
4. Saying California permits reporting out after the board refuses to act. California rule 1.13(c) forbids it.
5. Allowing the corporation's lawyer to defend the accused CEO in a derivative dispute with Ray's own consent. Consent must come from an official other than Ray, and the conflict is likely nonconsentable.
