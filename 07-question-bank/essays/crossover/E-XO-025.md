# E-XO-025 — Oak Hollow (Real Property + Remedies)

## Facts

Paloma Reyes owns Oak Hollow, a 40-acre ranch with a farmhouse, which she inherited from her father in 2012. On May 1, 2026, Paloma and Grant Liu signed a written contract for the sale of Oak Hollow to Grant for $1,600,000, with closing on June 30. Comparable sales show that the ranch was then worth about $1,650,000. Grant paid a $100,000 deposit into escrow. The contract requires Paloma to convey "marketable title by grant deed, free of all encumbrances except recorded easements for public utilities." Grant, a retired engineer, intends to live in the farmhouse.

Grant's title report, received on May 20, showed two problems. First, a recorded 2009 deed from Paloma's father granted the owner of the neighboring Ruiz farm "a perpetual easement for an underground irrigation pipeline" across the north end of Oak Hollow. The pipeline is buried, there is no surface sign of it, and Grant did not know of it. Second, the record owner of a three-acre strip along Oak Hollow's west side is Hector Ruiz, the neighbor, whose family has always paid the property taxes assessed on the strip. Grant's surveyor confirmed on May 22 that the old fence Paloma and her father treated as the western boundary sits 200 feet inside Ruiz's land.

Paloma's father fenced the strip in 2005 and grazed cattle on it continuously from then until his death; Paloma has done the same since 2012. Neither had anyone's permission; both believed the strip was theirs. The jurisdiction's statute of limitations for recovering possession of land is ten years. On May 25, after seeing the survey stakes, Ruiz cut the old fence and moved his cattle onto the strip, where they remain.

An appraiser says that the pipeline easement reduces Oak Hollow's value by $30,000 and that the west strip is worth $120,000.

On June 10, a wildfire caused by lightning destroyed Oak Hollow's barn. Grant had not taken possession. The barn was worth $80,000, and Paloma's fire insurer has paid her $80,000.

On June 15, after another buyer offered her $1,900,000, Paloma notified Grant that "because of the title problems and the fire, the contract is void" and that she would not close. Grant replied that he was ready to close and would accept whatever title Paloma could give, with a reduction in the price. Oak Hollow, with all 40 acres and the barn, would now be worth $1,850,000.

## Call of the Question

1. Can Paloma convey the title the contract requires? Discuss.
2. As between Paloma and Grant, who bears the loss of the barn? Discuss.
3. What remedies does Grant have against Paloma, and what remedies does Paloma have against Ruiz? Discuss.

## Model Answer

### 1. Can Paloma convey the title the contract requires?

**A. The marketable-title standard.** Every land-sale contract carries an implied covenant that the seller will convey marketable title at closing, meaning title reasonably free from doubt that a prudent buyer would accept without fear of litigation. Title is unmarketable if it has defects in the chain of title, including title resting on adverse possession not yet established by a quiet-title judgment, or encumbrances such as easements. Here the contract also expressly requires marketable title "free of all encumbrances except recorded easements for public utilities." Title must be marketable at closing (June 30), so the seller may cure defects before then.

**B. The irrigation-pipeline easement.** An easement is a nonpossessory right to use another's land; this one is an express, recorded easement appurtenant to the Ruiz farm. Easements are encumbrances that make title unmarketable unless the buyer agreed to take subject to them.

- *The contract exception.* Paloma will argue that a recorded pipeline easement is a "utility" easement. Grant will respond that "public utilities" means utility providers serving the public, while this is a private easement for a neighbor's farm irrigation. Exceptions to a title covenant are read narrowly.
- *Visible-easement doctrine.* Some courts treat a visible, beneficial easement that the buyer knew of as not making title unmarketable. This pipeline is buried, shows no surface sign, and Grant did not know of it.
- *Cure.* The easement is "perpetual," and only Ruiz can release it.

**The easement makes Paloma's title unmarketable, and she cannot cure it by June 30.**

**C. The west strip.** Ruiz holds record title to the three acres, so Paloma can claim them only by adverse possession. Possession that is actual, open and notorious, exclusive, adverse (without the owner's permission) and continuous for the statutory period vests title in the possessor. Successive possessors may tack their periods if they are in privity.

- *Actual, open and notorious, exclusive.* Fencing and grazing are the uses an average owner of grazing land would make, and a fence and cattle are plainly visible. Only Paloma's family used the strip.
- *Adverse.* Neither had permission. Under the majority objective test, their belief that the strip was theirs does not matter.
- *Continuous for ten years; tacking.* Paloma's father possessed from 2005 to 2012, and Paloma inherited and continued, which is privity. Ten years ran by 2015, long before Ruiz's 2026 entry, so Ruiz's entry did not interrupt the period.

Under general principles, **Paloma likely owns the strip by adverse possession.** But that title has never been established by a quiet-title judgment, and Ruiz disputes it. A buyer need not take title that invites a lawsuit, so **title to the strip is unmarketable** unless Paloma obtains a judgment before closing, which she cannot do in six weeks.

*California.* Adverse possession requires five years of possession plus timely payment of all taxes levied and assessed on the land (CCP § 325). The strip was assessed to Ruiz, whose family paid the taxes. **In California, Paloma's claim fails entirely**: Ruiz owns the strip, and Paloma has no title to convey. A prescriptive easement, which needs no tax payment, would not give her ownership, and courts are reluctant to grant a prescriptive easement that excludes the owner.

**Conclusion on call 1:** Paloma cannot convey marketable title to all 40 acres free of the easement.

### 2. Who bears the loss of the barn?

Once a specifically enforceable land contract is signed, equitable conversion makes the buyer the equitable owner, and the seller holds legal title as security for the price. Under the majority common-law rule, the risk of a casualty loss not caused by the seller passes to the buyer at signing, but the buyer gets credit for any insurance proceeds the seller collects. California has adopted the Uniform Vendor and Purchaser Risk Act (Civ. Code § 1662): until legal title or possession passes to the buyer, the seller bears the risk, and if all or a material part of the property is destroyed without the buyer's fault, the seller cannot enforce the contract and the buyer may recover what it paid.

- *Majority rule.* The signed May 1 contract made Grant the equitable owner, and the lightning fire was not Paloma's fault. Grant bears the loss and must still pay the price, but he is credited with Paloma's $80,000 of insurance proceeds, so Paloma does not recover twice.
- *UVPRA (California).* Grant had neither title nor possession, so the Act applies. An $80,000 barn is about 5% of a $1,600,000 ranch whose land and farmhouse are intact, so it is likely not a "material part," and Grant cannot rescind because of it. The Act's text addresses only destruction of all or a material part. It does not say who bears an immaterial loss before title or possession passes. The better argument, consistent with the Act's premise that the seller keeps the risk until then, is that Paloma cannot charge full price for what was lost, so Grant should receive an $80,000 reduction or the insurance proceeds; California authority on this point has not been confirmed [VERIFY V-831]. If a court instead found the barn a material part, the Act would bar Paloma from enforcing the contract and entitle Grant to recover his deposit, and California authority suggests he could not then compel conveyance with a price abatement (*Dixon v. Salvation Army* (1983) 142 Cal.App.3d 463) [VERIFY V-831].
- *Paloma's argument.* Paloma claims the fire "voided" the contract. Under the majority rule the risk was Grant's, so the fire cannot excuse Paloma. Under the UVPRA only the buyer may refuse to proceed, and only for a material loss.

**Either way, the fire does not let Paloma escape the contract. Under the majority rule Grant gets the $80,000 insurance credit; under the UVPRA he most likely gets a matching reduction, though that point is unsettled.**

### 3. Remedies

**A. Paloma's breach; Grant's alternatives.** Paloma's June 15 notice that she "would not close" was an unequivocal repudiation before performance was due. It excused Grant from tendering the price, and Grant stated that he was ready to close. Grant may choose among:
- *Rescission and restitution.* Return of his $100,000 deposit.
- *Damages.* Market value at breach minus the contract price ($1,850,000 − $1,600,000 = $250,000), plus the deposit and incidental costs. Under the minority "English rule," a seller who in good faith cannot convey because of a title defect owes only the deposit and expenses, but Paloma's refusal was driven by a $1,900,000 offer, so even that rule would not protect her. In California, Civ. Code § 3306 gives the benefit-of-the-bargain measure without a good-faith limitation [S-1221].
- *Specific performance with abatement*, which Grant has chosen.

**B. Specific performance.** Specific performance requires (1) a valid contract with definite terms; (2) the plaintiff's performance of its conditions, or its readiness, willingness and ability to perform; (3) an inadequate legal remedy; (4) feasible enforcement; and (5) no equitable defense.

1. *Contract.* A signed writing identifies the parties, the land and the price, satisfying the statute of frauds.
2. *Conditions.* Grant is ready, willing and able, and Paloma's repudiation excused a formal tender.
3. *Inadequate legal remedy.* Land is treated as unique. In California, the breach of an agreement to transfer real property is presumed not to be adequately relieved by money, and the presumption is conclusive for a single-family dwelling the buyer intends to occupy (Civ. Code § 3387). Grant intends to live in the farmhouse. Paloma may argue that a 40-acre ranch is not a "single-family dwelling," but even then the rebuttable presumption applies and she cannot overcome it.
4. *Feasibility.* An order to convey by deed and to pay the price is simple to enforce.
5. *Defenses.* Adequacy of consideration is measured when the contract was made (Civ. Code § 3391 requires adequate consideration). $1,600,000 against a $1,650,000 market value was fair. Paloma's loss of a $1,900,000 offer is a hardship she chose by repudiating, not a defense. Grant has acted promptly and fairly, so there is no laches or unclean hands.

**Grant is entitled to specific performance.**

**C. Abatement.** When the seller cannot convey exactly what it promised, the buyer may elect to take what the seller can convey with a reduction in price reflecting the deficiency. The seller cannot force a buyer to take a materially different property, but the election belongs to the buyer.

| Item | Reduction |
|---|---|
| Pipeline easement (lost value) | $30,000 |
| West strip (cannot be conveyed with marketable title) | $120,000 |
| Barn (destroyed; insurance proceeds) | $80,000 |
| **Total abatement** | **$230,000** |

Grant would pay **$1,600,000 − $230,000 = $1,370,000** (crediting his $100,000 deposit) for 37 acres, subject to the easement, without the barn. Paloma will argue that a court should not force her to sell a different property at a sharply reduced price. But the deficiencies are modest relative to the whole (three of 40 acres, an underground easement and a barn), and Grant, not Paloma, chose to accept them. Some courts measure abatement as a proportion of the contract price rather than by appraised values, which would slightly change the figures. The barn reduction rests on the risk-of-loss analysis in call 2. The decree could also give Grant the option to buy the strip for $120,000 if Paloma later quiets title to it.

**D. Paloma v. Ruiz: quiet title.** An action to quiet title establishes the plaintiff's title against adverse claims and removes clouds on title (in California, CCP § 760.010 et seq.). Paloma would sue Ruiz to establish her adverse-possession title to the strip by proving the elements above, including tacking her father's possession. Under general principles she would likely win, and the judgment would make her title marketable. In California, the tax-payment requirement defeats her, and the court would instead confirm Ruiz's title.

**E. Paloma v. Ruiz: ejectment and mesne profits.** Ejectment is a legal action by one entitled to possession to recover land from a person wrongfully in possession, together with mesne profits, the fair rental value during the wrongful occupation. If Paloma owns the strip by adverse possession, Ruiz's May 25 entry, cutting the fence and moving his cattle in, is wrongful possession. Paloma may eject him and recover the strip's rental value since May 25, plus damages for the cut fence as a trespass. Ejectment, enforced by the sheriff, is an adequate legal remedy, so she does not need an injunction. **In California, Ruiz owns the strip, so Paloma has no ejectment claim, and Ruiz's removal of the fence on his own land was lawful.**

**Best course for Grant:** specific performance with a $230,000 abatement (paying $1,370,000), rather than rescission or $250,000 in damages, because he gets the unique property he wants at a substantially reduced price.

- **45 or below:** discusses land-sale remedies in general terms without the title, risk-of-loss or abatement analysis.

**Common mistakes**
1. Treating a recorded private easement as within a "public utilities" exception.
2. Concluding that because adverse possession has run, title is marketable. Without a judgment, the buyer would be buying a lawsuit.
3. Letting the seller use the fire to escape the contract. The risk rules allocate the loss; they do not free a seller who still wants out.
4. Ignoring the insurance credit under the majority rule, which lets the seller recover twice.
5. Seeking an injunction against Ruiz without considering ejectment, the legal remedy for recovering possession of land.
