## Facts

Viktor and June married in 2008 in State X, which is not a community property state; under State X law, property acquired during marriage belongs to the spouse who holds title. While they lived in State X, Viktor used his salary to buy 5,000 shares of BigCo stock in his own name, now worth $500,000, and June used her salary to buy a certificate of deposit in her own name, now worth $80,000.

In 2016 they moved to California, where they lived until Viktor's death. In 2016 they bought a house on Alder Court with savings from their California salaries and took title as "Viktor and June, husband and wife, as community property." It is now worth $1,200,000. Viktor also saved part of his California salary in a brokerage account in his own name, now worth $400,000. In 2019 Viktor inherited a rental condominium from his father, now worth $1,200,000, which he kept in his own name.

In 2017 Viktor signed a valid will:

> 1. I give our family home on Alder Court to my son, Sam, who will need a place to raise his own family.
> 2. I give the rest of my estate to my wife, June.

Sam is Viktor's adult son from his first marriage. When the will was signed, Sam was married, expecting his first child and renting a small apartment nearby. June has lived in the Alder Court house since 2016 and has told the family she intends to stay there.

In 2018 Viktor opened a savings account at Golden State Bank in his name alone, "payable on death to Ingrid," his sister. He funded it entirely from his California salary; it holds $90,000. June never signed anything about the account.

In 2020 Viktor and June had a daughter, Poppy. Viktor never changed his will. In 2022 he told a friend, "June will take care of Poppy. That's why most of my estate goes to her."

In 2023, without telling June, Viktor had the BigCo shares re-registered as "Viktor and Sam, joint tenants with right of survivorship." Sam paid nothing. Viktor continued to receive all the dividends.

Viktor died in 2026, domiciled in California, survived by June, Sam, Poppy and Ingrid. His estate has no debts.

## Call of the Question

Answer according to California law.

1. What are June's rights in (a) the Alder Court house, (b) the BigCo shares, (c) the Golden State Bank account, and (d) her certificate of deposit?
2. Must June choose between her rights under Viktor's will and her interest in the Alder Court house?
3. Is Poppy entitled to a share of Viktor's estate and, if so, how much and from whom?

## Model Answer

### 1. June's rights in the property

**Framework.** Property acquired by a married person during marriage while domiciled in California is community property (CP) (Fam. Code § 760). Property acquired while domiciled elsewhere that would have been CP had the acquiring spouse been domiciled in California is quasi-community property (QCP) (Prob. Code § 66). When a married person dies, half of the CP belongs to the surviving spouse and half to the decedent, and half of the decedent's QCP belongs to the surviving spouse (Prob. Code §§ 100, 101). The decedent may dispose by will only of his separate property (SP), his half of the CP and his half of his QCP (§ 6101).

Viktor's brokerage account holds his California salary, so it is CP despite being in his name alone; title does not change character without a transmutation (*In re Marriage of Valli*). June owns half ($200,000); Viktor's half ($200,000) passes under his will.

**(a) The Alder Court house.** The house was bought during marriage with California salaries and titled "as community property." It is CP. June owns half outright ($600,000). Viktor could dispose of only his half by will, so Article 1 passes at most his half to Sam, unless June is put to an election (Call 2).

**(b) The BigCo shares**

*Character.* Viktor bought the shares with his salary while domiciled in State X. Had he been domiciled in California, salary would have been CP, so the shares are **QCP**. At death, QCP includes personal property wherever located (§ 66). June therefore has a right to half of the shares under § 101.

*The joint-tenancy transfer and recapture.* Viktor put the shares in joint tenancy with Sam, so on his death Sam takes them by survivorship, outside the will. But the surviving spouse may require a transferee to restore one-half of QCP (or its proceeds or value) if (1) the decedent died domiciled in California; (2) he transferred the property to someone other than the spouse without receiving substantial consideration and without the spouse's written consent or joinder; and (3) he retained at death possession, enjoyment or the income of the property, a power to revoke, or the property was held with the transferee with a right of survivorship (§ 102).
1. Viktor died domiciled in California.
2. He transferred the shares into joint tenancy with Sam, who paid nothing; June never consented and did not know.
3. Viktor kept all the dividends and held the shares with Sam with a right of survivorship.

Sam may argue that Viktor was free to manage the shares, which stood in his name; during life, the acquiring spouse does manage QCP. But § 102 exists precisely to protect the survivor's half against such will substitutes. **June may require Sam to restore one-half of the shares, worth $250,000.** Sam keeps the other half, Viktor's share, by survivorship.

**(c) The Golden State Bank account**

*Character.* The account was funded entirely with Viktor's California salary during marriage, so it is CP; Viktor's name on the account does not change that.

*Nonprobate transfer.* A payable-on-death designation is a nonprobate transfer that is valid without will formalities (§ 5000), and at death the balance normally belongs to the POD payee (§ 5302). But a nonprobate transfer of CP made without the other spouse's written consent is not effective against that spouse's interest, and the court will set it aside to that extent (§§ 5020, 5021). June never consented in writing. **June recovers her half, $45,000, from Ingrid**; Ingrid keeps Viktor's half, $45,000.

**(d) June's certificate of deposit.** June bought the CD with her own State X salary, so it is QCP, but it is **June's** QCP. At a spouse's death only the **decedent's** QCP is divided (§ 101); the survivor's own QCP remains hers. Viktor had no interest in the CD that he could pass by will. **June keeps the full $80,000.**

**The condominium.** Viktor inherited the condominium during marriage. Property acquired by inheritance is SP (Fam. Code § 770). June has no community interest in it; it passes under Viktor's residuary clause, which leaves it to June (subject to Poppy's claim, Call 3).

### 2. Must June elect?

*Rule.* If a will purports to dispose of the surviving spouse's half of CP and also gives the survivor benefits, the survivor must elect: either take under the will and let her half pass as the will directs, or keep her half and give up the will's benefits. But a testator is presumed to know that he can dispose only of his own half and to intend only that; the survivor is put to an election only if the will clearly shows an intent to dispose of the whole community property, and ambiguity is resolved in favor of disposing only of the testator's half (*Estate of Wolfe*).

*Sam's argument (election required).* Article 1 gives "our family home," not "my interest," and explains that Sam "will need a place to raise his own family." Sam was then expecting his first child and renting a small apartment; half of a house shared with a stepmother is not "a place to raise his own family," so Viktor must have meant the whole house. Viktor also balanced the gift by leaving June the residue, worth far more.

*June's argument (no election).* "Our family home" identifies the property; it does not say Viktor is giving away June's interest. The will never mentions June's half or says that her residuary gift is in exchange for it. Under *Wolfe*, Viktor is presumed to have disposed only of what he could, and the explanatory phrase states a motive, not a clear disposition of her property. Extrinsic evidence may be considered to resolve an ambiguity (§ 21102), and the surrounding facts cut against Sam: June had lived in the house since 2016, and it is unlikely Viktor intended his widow to lose her home to his son.

*Conclusion.* The language is suggestive but not the clear manifestation *Wolfe* requires. **June likely need not elect.** Sam takes Viktor's half of the house as a tenant in common with June, and June keeps her half and also takes the residue.

*If an election were required.* June would compare her half of the house ($600,000) with the residue she receives under the will ($1,400,000 before Poppy's share). She would likely elect to take under the will, and Sam would receive the whole house.

### 3. Poppy

**A. Is Poppy an omitted child?** A child born or adopted after the execution of all of the decedent's testamentary instruments, and not provided for in them, receives the share she would have received had the decedent died without any testamentary instrument (§ 21620). Poppy was born in 2020, three years after Viktor's only will, which does not mention her. She qualifies unless an exception applies.

**B. Exceptions (§ 21621).** The child takes nothing if:
1. *The omission was intentional and appears in the will.* The 2017 will says nothing about later children. Viktor's 2022 statement to a friend is not in a testamentary instrument. **Not met.**
2. *The decedent had one or more children and devised or otherwise directed the disposition of substantially all of his estate to the omitted child's other parent.* Viktor had a child (Sam) when he signed the will, and June is Poppy's mother. The question is whether the residue is "substantially all" of the estate. Viktor's estate (the property he could pass) is his half of the house ($600,000), his half of the brokerage account ($200,000) and the condominium ($1,200,000): $2,000,000. June receives the residue, $1,400,000, or 70%; Sam receives $600,000, or 30%.
   - *June and Sam's argument.* The exception's purpose is met: Viktor left most of his estate to Poppy's mother, trusting her to provide for their child, and he said exactly that in 2022 ("June will take care of Poppy").
   - *Poppy's argument.* The statute requires **substantially all**, not "most." Viktor himself used the word "most." Diverting 30% of the estate, including the family home, to another child is not leaving substantially all to the other parent.
   - *Conclusion.* The statute sets no percentage, and no California case fixing one was located. Seventy percent is closer to "most" than to "all" (and if the nonprobate transfers Viktor "otherwise directed" are counted, his half of the BigCo shares to Sam and his half of the POD account to Ingrid, June's share falls to about 61%), so **the exception likely fails**. But a court focused on the exception's purpose, a parent who leaves the bulk of the estate to the other parent to provide for their child, could find it met; Poppy would then take nothing, and Sam's and June's gifts would stand undiminished.
3. *Transfer outside the will in lieu of a testamentary provision.* Viktor made no transfer to Poppy outside the will. **Not met.**

**Poppy is an omitted child.**

**C. Poppy's share.** Poppy receives her intestate share of Viktor's estate. Had Viktor died intestate, June would take his half of the CP, his half of his QCP, and one-third of his SP, because he left more than one child; the remaining two-thirds of the SP would pass equally to his children (§§ 6401, 6402). Poppy would take nothing from the CP or QCP and one-third of the SP. Viktor's SP is the $1,200,000 condominium, so **Poppy's share is $400,000.** The nonprobate property (the joint-tenancy shares and the POD account) is not part of the estate for this purpose.

**D. Who pays.** The share is satisfied first from estate property not disposed of by the will and then from all the will's beneficiaries in proportion to the value each receives, measured at death, unless the court orders a different allocation to preserve the decedent's plan (§ 21623). The residuary clause disposes of everything, so Sam and June contribute pro rata:
- Sam: $600,000 ÷ $2,000,000 = 30% × $400,000 = **$120,000**
- June: $1,400,000 ÷ $2,000,000 = 70% × $400,000 = **$280,000**

June may argue that the court should take the whole share from Sam's gift to preserve Viktor's evident plan of providing for Poppy through her, but the statutory default is pro rata.

### Summary (assuming no election)

| Person | Takes |
|---|---|
| June | Her half of the house ($600,000) and of the brokerage account ($200,000); residue of $1,400,000 less $280,000 for Poppy; $250,000 restored from the BigCo shares; $45,000 from the POD account; her own CD ($80,000) |
| Sam | Viktor's half of the house ($600,000) less $120,000 for Poppy; half of the BigCo shares by survivorship ($250,000) |
| Ingrid | $45,000 from the POD account |
| Poppy | $400,000 omitted-child share |

**Common mistakes**
1. Treating the BigCo shares as Sam's because they passed by survivorship. Section 102 lets June recapture her half of QCP put into joint tenancy without her consent.
2. Dividing June's own QCP at Viktor's death. Only the decedent's QCP is split.
3. Assuming any will that names "our home" forces an election. California presumes the testator disposed only of his half unless the will clearly shows otherwise.
4. Giving Poppy a share of the CP. In intestacy the decedent's half of the CP and QCP goes to the surviving spouse; the children share only in the SP.
5. Funding the omitted child's share entirely from the residue. § 21623 apportions it pro rata among the beneficiaries.
