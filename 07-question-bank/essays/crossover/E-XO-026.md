## Facts

In January 2026, Mia Kent and Ollie Park agreed to start a kayak-tour business in Santa Cruz as equal co-owners, planning to run it as a corporation called Tidewater Adventures, Inc. ("Tidewater"). Each deposited $20,000 in a joint bank account titled "Mia Kent & Ollie Park dba Tidewater Adventures." They began taking bookings online and running weekend tours in rented kayaks, splitting the profits equally.

On February 10, Ollie telephoned Coastal Craft Boats, Inc. ("Coastal"), a kayak manufacturer, and ordered 20 tandem touring kayaks at Coastal's list price of $1,450 each ($29,000), for delivery April 1. He told Coastal's sales manager that the buyer would be "Tidewater Adventures, Inc., which we're forming now." The next day Coastal emailed Ollie a confirmation signed by its sales manager: "This confirms your order for Tidewater Adventures, Inc. (in formation): 20 tandem touring kayaks at $1,450 each, delivery April 1, payment due on delivery." Ollie did not reply. Mia knew of the order and approved it.

On March 1, Mia and Ollie filed articles of incorporation for Tidewater. Each received 50% of the shares, and they became its two directors; Ollie is president. The board never voted on the kayak order. On March 10, Ollie emailed Coastal: "Please deliver Tidewater's 20 kayaks to the Harbor dock instead of our office. — Ollie Park, President, Tidewater Adventures, Inc." Tidewater's website announced, "Our new fleet of Coastal tandems arrives April 1!"

On March 20, a competitor offered Tidewater similar kayaks for $1,100 each. Ollie emailed Coastal: "Tidewater will not take the kayaks. We never had a contract with you." Coastal, which has the capacity to build far more kayaks than it can sell, resold the 20 kayaks in April to a resort at $1,450 each. Each kayak costs Coastal $950 to build. Coastal spent $600 storing and reshipping the kayaks.

In April, Tidewater hired Rae Lin as "Operations Manager." The board told her in writing that she could make purchases of up to $2,000 without board approval. Rae had earlier arranged two $800 social-media advertising jobs with WaveAds LLC, which Tidewater paid. On May 1, Rae signed, as "Rae Lin, Operations Manager, Tidewater Adventures, Inc.," a 10-month contract with WaveAds for social-media advertising at $1,500 a month ($15,000 in total). When WaveAds' owner asked whether she had authority, Rae said, "Yes, I handle all our vendors." In early June, Rae's monthly report to the board described the WaveAds contract and its terms. Neither director objected. Tidewater ran the WaveAds campaigns all summer and paid the invoices for May through August. In September, after the tourist season, the board told WaveAds that Rae "never had authority" and that Tidewater would pay nothing more. WaveAds can take on as many clients as it wants; its cost of performing the Tidewater work is $300 a month.

## Call of the Question

1. What claims does Coastal have against Tidewater, Ollie and Mia for the kayaks, and what can it recover? Discuss.
2. Is Tidewater bound by the WaveAds contract, and what may WaveAds recover? Discuss.

## Model Answer

### 1. Coastal's claims for the kayaks

**Governing law.** A sale of kayaks is a sale of goods, so UCC Article 2 governs (Cal. Com. Code § 2101 et seq.), supplemented by common law.

**A. Statute of frauds.** A contract for the sale of goods for $500 or more is not enforceable unless there is a writing sufficient to indicate that a contract was made, signed by the party against whom enforcement is sought, and stating a quantity (UCC § 2-201(1)). Between merchants, a written confirmation sent within a reasonable time, sufficient against the sender, satisfies the statute against a recipient with reason to know its contents unless the recipient objects in writing within 10 days (§ 2-201(2)).
- *The deal.* $29,000 for 20 kayaks is well over $500. Ollie's order was oral, and Ollie signed nothing in February.
- *Merchant's confirmation.* Coastal's signed email states the quantity (20) and confirms an order, so it is sufficient against Coastal. Ollie received it and did not object at all. The question is whether Ollie (and the business he was buying for) is a "merchant." Ollie will argue that a tour operator does not deal in kayaks. But for the confirmation rule, almost any businessperson qualifies, because answering business mail is a general business practice (§ 2-104(1), (3) and cmt. 2). Ollie was buying equipment for an operating tour business. He is likely a merchant for this purpose, so the statute is satisfied against him.
- *Writing signed for Tidewater.* Ollie's March 10 email, signed "Ollie Park, President, Tidewater Adventures, Inc.," refers to "Tidewater's 20 kayaks" and asks for delivery to a new location. It indicates a contract, states the quantity, and is signed by Tidewater's president, so it satisfies § 2-201(1) against Tidewater.

**Conclusion:** the statute of frauds is no defense. Ollie's March 20 claim that "we never had a contract" is contradicted by these writings.

**B. Ollie: promoter liability.** A promoter who contracts on behalf of a corporation not yet formed is personally liable on the contract, and remains liable after the corporation is formed and adopts it, unless the parties agreed that the other party would look only to the corporation, or there is a novation (MBCA § 2.04; common law).
- *Ollie's arguments.* Coastal knew Tidewater was "in formation," and the confirmation named Tidewater, not Ollie, as buyer. He may also argue that the arrangement was only an offer to the future corporation, which bound no one until the corporation accepted.
- *Coastal's response.* Both parties' knowledge that the corporation did not yet exist is the ordinary promoter situation and does not itself release the promoter. Nothing said Coastal would look only to the corporation. The confirmation describes a present order with a fixed delivery date, not an offer left open for a future corporation. No novation occurred: Coastal never agreed to release Ollie.

**Conclusion:** Ollie is personally liable. He may seek indemnity from Tidewater if it adopted the contract.

**C. Tidewater: adoption.** A corporation is not bound by a pre-incorporation contract until it adopts it, expressly or impliedly, with knowledge of its material terms; adoption takes effect when made and does not relate back. Officers bind the corporation within their actual or apparent authority, and a president has implied authority over ordinary business matters.
- The board never voted. But on March 10, Ollie, as president, wrote to Coastal treating the order as "Tidewater's 20 kayaks" and redirected the delivery, and Tidewater's website announced the arriving fleet. Buying the tour boats that the business was formed to use is an ordinary business matter within a president's authority. Mia, the only other director, knew of and approved the order.
- Tidewater will argue that it never received the kayaks or any benefit. But adoption can be express as well as implied; the president's written affirmation, with the other director's knowledge, is an express adoption.

**Conclusion:** Tidewater adopted the contract on March 10 and is liable to Coastal, together with Ollie.

**D. Mia: liability as a partner in the pre-incorporation business.** A general partnership is formed by the association of two or more persons to carry on as co-owners a business for profit, whether or not they intend a partnership; sharing profits creates a presumption of partnership (Corp. Code § 16202; RUPA § 202). Each partner is an agent of the partnership, and a partner's act for apparently carrying on the partnership business in the ordinary course binds the partnership (§ 16301(1)). All partners are jointly and severally liable for partnership obligations (§ 16306).
- *Partnership.* From January to March, Mia and Ollie pooled money, ran tours, took bookings and split the profits equally. They were carrying on a business as co-owners, not merely planning one. They were general partners, whatever corporate plans they had.
- *Partnership obligation.* Buying kayaks is squarely in the ordinary course of a kayak-tour business. Mia will argue that Ollie expressly bought for the future corporation, not the partnership, and that Coastal never relied on her. Coastal will respond that Ollie was acting for the business the two of them were already running, that Mia approved the order, and that the corporation did not exist to be bound when the contract was made. Courts commonly hold co-promoters who are carrying on a business together liable as partners for obligations incurred before incorporation, and incorporation does not discharge those obligations.
- *Exhaustion.* Coastal must generally obtain a judgment against the partnership and exhaust partnership assets before reaching Mia's personal assets, unless an exception applies (§ 16307).

**Conclusion:** Mia is likely liable as a partner, jointly and severally with Ollie, subject to the exhaustion rule.

**E. Anticipatory repudiation.** A clear, unequivocal statement before performance is due that a party will not perform is an anticipatory repudiation, which the other party may treat as a total breach (UCC § 2-610). Ollie's March 20 email ("Tidewater will not take the kayaks") came before the April 1 delivery date and was unequivocal. Coastal could cancel, stop delivery, resell, and sue immediately. A better price elsewhere is no excuse.

**F. Coastal's damages.** A seller whose buyer repudiates may recover the contract price minus the resale price in a commercially reasonable resale (§ 2-706), or the contract price minus market price (§ 2-708(1)), plus incidental damages, minus expenses saved. If those measures would not put the seller in as good a position as performance, it recovers the **profit** it would have made, including reasonable overhead, plus incidental damages (§ 2-708(2)). A seller with the capacity to make both the broken sale and the resale is a **lost-volume seller** (*Neri v. Retail Marine*).
- *Resale measure.* Coastal resold at $1,450, the contract price, so § 2-706 gives only the $600 of incidental expenses. Tidewater will argue that Coastal therefore lost nothing beyond $600.
- *Lost volume.* Coastal can build far more kayaks than it can sell. The resort sale would have happened anyway; Coastal would then have made both sales. The resale did not replace the lost sale. Coastal therefore lost its profit on Tidewater's 20 kayaks: ($1,450 − $950) × 20 = **$10,000**.
- *Incidental damages.* $600 for storing and reshipping.

**Conclusion:** Coastal recovers **$10,600** from Tidewater, Ollie and Mia, jointly and severally, but may collect it only once.

### 2. The WaveAds contract

**A. Actual authority.** An agent has actual authority to take actions she reasonably believes, from the principal's manifestations to her, that the principal wants her to take. Implied authority covers acts reasonably necessary or customary to carry out express authority (R3d Agency §§ 2.01–2.02). The board told Rae in writing she could make purchases up to $2,000. A $15,000 commitment exceeds that express limit, and implied authority cannot override an express limit the agent knows. **Rae had no actual authority.**

**B. Apparent authority.** A principal is bound when a third party reasonably believes the agent has authority, and that belief is traceable to the principal's own manifestations to the third party (R3d Agency § 2.03; California calls it ostensible authority, Civ. Code § 2317). An agent cannot create her own apparent authority.
- *Tidewater's manifestations.* Tidewater gave Rae the title "Operations Manager," a position that ordinarily includes buying routine services for the business. It also paid two earlier WaveAds jobs that Rae arranged, which told WaveAds that Tidewater stood behind Rae's dealings with it.
- *Tidewater's arguments.* Rae's statement that she "handle[s] all our vendors" is her own manifestation, not Tidewater's, and counts for nothing. The two prior jobs were small ($800 each); a 10-month, $15,000 commitment is different in kind, and a reasonable vendor would have checked with an owner.
- *WaveAds' response.* $1,500 a month for advertising is a modest, routine expense for an operating tour business, and WaveAds did inquire. Nothing told WaveAds of the $2,000 limit, and a secret internal limit does not bind a third party without notice.

This is close. Apparent authority is plausible but not certain, because the size and length of the contract go beyond the prior course of dealing. Ratification makes the question academic.

**C. Ratification.** A principal ratifies an unauthorized act done on its behalf by manifesting assent or by conduct justifying a reasonable assumption that it consents, including knowingly accepting the benefits, with knowledge of the material facts. Ratification relates back to the original act, and a principal cannot ratify part of a transaction and reject the rest (R3d Agency §§ 4.01–4.08).
- *Knowledge.* In early June, Rae's report gave the board the contract's terms, including its 10-month length and price.
- *Affirmance.* Neither director objected. Tidewater ran the campaigns all summer and paid four monthly invoices. That is affirmance by conduct and by knowingly accepting benefits.
- *All or nothing.* Tidewater cannot keep the summer advertising and disclaim the off-season months. Having ratified, it is bound to the entire 10-month contract as of May 1. Its September claim that Rae "never had authority" comes too late.

**Conclusion:** Tidewater is bound, at least by ratification and possibly by apparent authority.

**D. WaveAds' damages.** Expectation damages put the injured party where full performance would have put it: the loss in value of the other party's performance, minus costs avoided (R2d § 347). Six months remain (September through February), at $1,500 a month, for $9,000. WaveAds saves $300 a month in performance costs, $1,800 in all. Its damages are **$7,200**.

*Mitigation.* An injured party cannot recover losses it could reasonably have avoided. Tidewater will argue that WaveAds should have found replacement clients for the freed capacity. But WaveAds can serve as many clients as it wants, so any new client would have been additional business, not a substitute for Tidewater's. Like a lost-volume seller, it has nothing to deduct for mitigation. Tidewater's September notice also means WaveAds must stop performing and may not run up further costs.

**Conclusion:** WaveAds recovers $7,200 from Tidewater. Rae may be liable to Tidewater for exceeding her authority, but she is not liable to WaveAds, because the principal is bound.

- **75+:** each defendant analyzed separately (Ollie as promoter, Tidewater by adoption, Mia as pre-incorporation partner) after a statute-of-frauds threshold; the lost-volume computation done; agency answered actual, then apparent, then ratification, with damages computed.

- **55–60:** treats adoption as releasing Ollie, or treats Rae's own statement as creating apparent authority.
- **45–50:** misses the statute of frauds and the lost-volume issue, or lets Tidewater ratify only part of the WaveAds contract.

**Common mistakes**
1. Releasing the promoter because the corporation later adopted the contract. Only a novation or an agreement to look solely to the corporation releases him.
2. Forgetting the partnership that existed before incorporation, which makes Mia personally liable.
3. Awarding Coastal only incidental damages because it resold at the same price. A lost-volume seller recovers its lost profit.
4. Building apparent authority on the agent's own words. The belief must be traceable to the principal.
5. Allowing a principal to ratify the beneficial part of a contract and reject the rest.

**Accuracy notes.** The Restatement (Third) of Agency section numbers are cited as in the repo's rule statements (V-701), and California's Civil Code agency sections as in R-BUS-009 (V-702); the analysis does not depend on them. The California Commercial Code may now say "record" rather than "writing" in § 2201 (V-101); the emails satisfy either term. Whether the § 2-708(2) "due credit for proceeds of resale" language reduces a lost-volume seller's recovery is resolved, as in *Neri* and R-CTR-100, against such a credit.
