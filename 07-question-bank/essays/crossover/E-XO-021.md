## Facts

In March 2025, Hana Kim was injured when a delivery truck owned by Rapid Freight, Inc. ran a red light and struck her car in Fresno. She met with Ben Ortiz, a California personal-injury lawyer. Ben said: "I'll take your case on a contingency: one-third of any recovery if we settle before trial, 40% if we go to trial. I'll advance the costs and get them back from your recovery." Hana replied, "That's fair. Let's do it." They shook hands, and Ben began work that day.

A week later, Ben's paralegal emailed Hana a one-page document titled "Contingency Fee Agreement," stating: "Attorney's fee: 40% of any recovery. Costs advanced by Attorney will be deducted from Client's share after the fee is calculated." Ben had not signed it. The cover email said, "Please sign and return." Hana never signed or replied, and no one followed up. Ben kept working on the case.

In November 2025, Rapid Freight's insurer offered $150,000. On Ben's advice, Hana rejected it. Trial was set for September 14, 2026. Rapid Freight's lawyers litigated aggressively, taking many depositions and retaining three experts. By late August 2026 Ben had spent about 180 hours on the case and advanced $9,000 in costs. Experienced personal-injury lawyers in Fresno typically bill about $400 an hour.

On August 31, 2026, Ben met Hana at his office and said: "This case has taken far more work than I expected. Unless you agree to raise my fee to 50% of any recovery, I'll have to withdraw. You won't find another lawyer willing to try this case in two weeks." Hana, who was still in pain from her injuries and anxious about the trial, signed a "Fee Amendment" that Ben had prepared, setting his fee at 50% of any recovery. Ben also signed it. The amendment contains all the statements required by Business and Professions Code § 6147. Ben did not suggest that Hana consult another lawyer, and she did not.

The next day Hana talked to a friend who is a lawyer. On September 3, she sent Ben a letter discharging him and hired Carla Diaz, who obtained a continuance of the trial. In October 2026, Rapid Freight settled for $300,000, which Carla deposited in her client trust account. Ben wrote to Carla demanding $150,000 (50%) plus his costs, "or at the very least 40%." Hana insists that Ben should get nothing because of the way he treated her.

## Call of the Question

Answer calls 1, 2 and 4 according to California law.

1. Was an enforceable fee agreement formed between Hana and Ben in March 2025, and if so, on what terms? Discuss.
2. Is the August 31, 2026 Fee Amendment enforceable against Hana? Discuss.
3. Did Ben violate any rules of professional conduct? Answer according to California and ABA authorities.
4. What, if anything, is Ben entitled to recover, and how should Carla handle the settlement funds? Discuss.

## Model Answer

### 1. The March 2025 fee agreement

**A. Formation.** A contract requires mutual assent, usually an offer and an acceptance, judged objectively, supported by consideration (Civ. Code §§ 1550, 1565; R2d §§ 17, 24). An offer is a manifestation of willingness to bargain that justifies the offeree in understanding that her assent will conclude the deal.
- *Offer.* Ben's statement ("I'll take your case on a contingency: one-third ... if we settle before trial, 40% if we go to trial. I'll advance the costs and get them back from your recovery") is a definite commitment with the key terms: the services, the fee percentages and the treatment of costs.
- *Acceptance.* Hana's reply, "That's fair. Let's do it," and the handshake are unequivocal assent.
- *Consideration.* Ben's promise of legal services and advanced costs was exchanged for Hana's promise of a share of any recovery. That is a bargained-for exchange.

A contract was formed orally on those terms.

**B. The unsigned "40%" document.** After a contract is formed, one party cannot change its terms by sending a different writing. The paralegal's document, a flat 40% with costs deducted from Hana's share after the fee, is at most an **offer to modify** the agreement. Silence is generally not acceptance. It operates as acceptance only if (1) the offeree takes the benefit of offered services with a reasonable opportunity to reject them and reason to know that compensation on those terms is expected, (2) the offeror said silence would suffice and the offeree intended to accept, or (3) prior dealings make notice of rejection reasonable (R2d § 69; Civ. Code § 1589).
- *Ben's argument.* Hana received the document and then kept accepting his services for 17 months, so she accepted the 40% terms by conduct.
- *Hana's response.* Ben was already obligated to provide those services under the oral agreement, so her accepting them shows nothing about the new terms. The cover email asked her to "sign and return," so Ben himself made signature the method of acceptance, not silence. There were no prior dealings.

Hana's silence was not acceptance. **The oral terms govern:** one-third if the case settles before trial, 40% if it is tried, costs repaid from the recovery.

**C. Enforceability under Business and Professions Code § 6147.** A California contingency-fee contract must be in writing, signed by both the lawyer and the client, with a copy to the client, and must state the contingency rate; how costs and disbursements will affect the fee and the client's recovery; the extent to which the client may have to pay for related matters not covered by the agreement; and (except in matters governed by § 6146) that the fee is not set by law and is negotiable. If the lawyer fails to comply, the agreement is **voidable at the client's option**, and the lawyer is then entitled to a reasonable fee (§ 6147(b)).

The March agreement was oral, so it fails every requirement. The paralegal's document was never signed by either party and also omits the negotiability statement and the related-matters term. The contract is therefore **valid but voidable by Hana**. If she voids it, Ben is limited to a reasonable fee.

### 2. The August 31, 2026 Fee Amendment

**A. Consideration.**
- *General rule.* At common law, a modification needs new consideration. A promise to pay more for the same performance that the promisee is already bound to give is not consideration (pre-existing duty rule; R2d § 73). Ben was already bound to try the case for 40%. The amendment gave him 50% for doing nothing new. A modification is enforceable without consideration if it is fair and equitable in light of circumstances the parties did not anticipate (R2d § 89(a)), but aggressive defense tactics, depositions and experts are ordinary risks of personal-injury litigation that a contingency lawyer assumes. Under general principles the amendment fails for lack of consideration.
- *California.* California has a different rule for oral contracts: "A contract not in writing may be modified in any respect by consent of the parties, in writing, without a new consideration" (Civ. Code § 1697). The March agreement was oral, and the amendment is a writing signed by both parties. In California, therefore, the absence of new consideration does not defeat the amendment. (Section 1698 governs modifications of written contracts and does not apply here.)

The amendment survives the consideration challenge in California, so Hana must rely on the defenses below.

**B. Economic duress.** A contract is voidable if a party's assent was induced by an improper threat that left no reasonable alternative. A bad-faith threat to breach an existing contract is improper (R2d §§ 175–176; Civ. Code §§ 1569–1570).
- *Improper threat.* Ben threatened to abandon the case two weeks before trial unless Hana raised his fee. He had no right to withdraw for that reason (Part 3), so the threat was effectively a threat to breach his contractual and fiduciary obligations to extract more money. That is a bad-faith threat.
- *No reasonable alternative.* Ben told Hana she would not find another lawyer in two weeks, and she was in pain and anxious. Ben will point out that within three days she found Carla, who obtained a continuance, so a reasonable alternative existed. But the test looks at the alternatives reasonably apparent to Hana when she signed, in Ben's office, under his assurance that no one would take the case.
- *Inducement and prompt avoidance.* Hana signed because of the threat and repudiated the arrangement within three days by discharging Ben, so she did not ratify it.

The amendment is likely **voidable for economic duress**.

**C. Undue influence.** Undue influence is unfair persuasion of a party who, because of a relationship of trust, is justified in assuming the persuader will not act against her welfare (R2d § 177; Civ. Code § 1575). Signs of unfair persuasion include an unusual time or place, a demand for an immediate decision, vulnerability, and the absence of independent advice.
- A lawyer-client relationship is a classic confidential relationship.
- Ben pressed Hana, who was in pain and anxious, at his own office, on the eve of trial, with a demand for an immediate decision, using a document he drafted, without suggesting independent advice. The change benefited only Ben.
- Ben will argue that Hana is an adult who signed a clear document containing the § 6147 disclosures, including that the fee was negotiable. But a negotiability statement is little protection when the client is told the alternative is losing her lawyer before trial.

The amendment is also **voidable for undue influence**.

**Conclusion:** Hana may avoid the Fee Amendment.

### 3. Ben's professional-responsibility violations

**A. The March fee agreement.**
- *ABA:* A contingent-fee agreement must be in a writing signed by the client, stating the method of calculating the fee, including the percentages for settlement, trial and appeal, the expenses deducted and whether they are deducted before or after the fee, and the expenses the client must pay regardless of outcome (MR 1.5(c)).
- *California:* the writing requirements are statutory. Section 6147 imposes the requirements in Part 1.C, with voidability as the civil consequence. California's rule 1.5 forbids unconscionable or illegal fees but, unlike MR 1.5(b)–(c), contains no writing or fee-communication requirement.
- *Application:* Ben relied on a handshake and an unsigned paralegal form, so he violated MR 1.5(c) and § 6147. Under the ABA rule the failure is a disciplinary violation; in California its principal consequence is that the agreement is voidable by Hana.

**B. The 50% fee: unconscionable or unreasonable.**
- *ABA (MR 1.5(a)):* A fee may not be unreasonable, judged by factors including the time and labor, the amount involved and results, the customary fee and whether the fee is contingent.
- *California (rule 1.5(a)–(b)):* A fee may not be unconscionable, judged at the time the agreement is made, considering factors including whether the lawyer engaged in fraud or overreaching, the amount of the fee in proportion to the value of the services, the relative sophistication of lawyer and client, and whether the client gave informed consent.
- *Application:* Contingent fees of a third to 40% are customary. Raising the fee to 50% after most of the work was done, on a case already worth at least the rejected $150,000 offer, shifts no new risk to Ben and transfers value to him. The process was overreaching: a threat to withdraw, a vulnerable client, no time to reflect and no advice to consult counsel. As Hana's fiduciary, Ben could not use his position to obtain a fee change in his own favor without fairness and full disclosure.

The 50% fee is likely **unreasonable under the ABA rule and unconscionable under California's**, chiefly because of the overreaching.

**C. The threatened withdrawal.**
- *Rules:* A lawyer may withdraw only on a recognized ground. California's permissive grounds (rule 1.16(b)) include a client's breach of a material term of the fee agreement after a reasonable warning, but not a lawyer's wish for a better deal. The ABA allows withdrawal if it can be done without material adverse effect on the client, or if the representation will result in an unreasonable financial burden on the lawyer (MR 1.16(b)(1), (6)). In either system, a lawyer in pending litigation needs the court's permission and must take reasonable steps to avoid prejudice to the client (rule 1.16(c)–(d); MR 1.16(c)–(d)).
- *Application:* Hana breached nothing. Withdrawal two weeks before trial would cause obvious material adverse effect. Ben might invoke MR 1.16(b)(6) (unreasonable financial burden), but the extra work of contested litigation is the risk a contingency lawyer accepts. A court would likely refuse permission to withdraw on the eve of trial for that reason. Using a threat of withdrawal that he likely could not carry out, as leverage to raise his fee, is itself overreaching and a breach of his duty of loyalty.

**Conclusion:** Ben violated MR 1.5(c) and § 6147, charged an unreasonable/unconscionable fee through overreaching, and misused the threat of withdrawal, under both California and ABA rules.

### 4. Ben's recovery and the settlement funds

**A. Hana's right to discharge Ben; quantum meruit.** A client may discharge a lawyer at any time, with or without cause. A discharged contingency-fee lawyer may not recover the contract fee. He may recover the **reasonable value of his services (quantum meruit)**, payable only if and when the contingency occurs (*Fracasse v. Brent* (Cal. 1972)). Courts commonly also cap that recovery at the fee the contract would have provided. Quantum meruit is a restitution remedy for the reasonable value of a benefit knowingly accepted.
- *Which contract?* The Fee Amendment is voidable for duress and undue influence, so Ben cannot rely on 50%. The March oral agreement is voidable under § 6147; if Hana voids it, § 6147 likewise limits Ben to a reasonable fee. Either way, Ben's claim is for a reasonable fee, not 50% or 40%.
- *Contingency occurred.* The case settled for $300,000 in October 2026.
- *Measure.* Ben spent about 180 hours. At the customary $400 rate, the reasonable value is roughly $72,000. His work (discovery, preparation, and advice that led Hana to reject $150,000) contributed to the doubled recovery. If the oral agreement is treated as the cap, it is one-third, because the case settled before trial: $100,000. The reasonable value is below the cap. Ben is also entitled to reimbursement of the $9,000 in costs he advanced, which the parties agreed would be repaid from the recovery.

**B. Should Ben forfeit his fee?** Hana argues that Ben should get nothing. A lawyer who breaches fiduciary duties may have fees reduced or forfeited. A rule violation does not automatically bar quantum meruit (*Huskinson & Brown v. Wolf*), but quantum meruit may be denied where the violation is egregious (*Sheppard, Mullin*). Ben's attempt to extract a 50% fee by threatening to abandon a vulnerable client is serious misconduct. But it lasted three days, did not succeed in harming the recovery, and followed 17 months of genuine work that benefited Hana. A court is more likely to **reduce** Ben's quantum meruit award, or at least deny him any premium, than to forfeit it entirely. A likely outcome is a reasonable fee in the range of $72,000 or somewhat less, plus $9,000 in costs.

**C. Fee arbitration.** Before or when suing Hana for fees, Ben must notify her of her right to arbitrate the dispute under the Mandatory Fee Arbitration Act; arbitration is nonbinding unless the parties agree otherwise after the dispute arises (B&P § 6201). He cannot simply take his fee from the settlement.

**D. Carla's handling of the funds.** When a client and a third person (here, the former lawyer) dispute entitlement to funds held in trust, the lawyer must keep the disputed portion in the trust account until the dispute is resolved and promptly distribute the undisputed portion (rule 1.15; MR 1.15(e)). Ben claims up to $150,000 plus costs, and Hana disputes all of it. Carla should hold at least the amount Ben claims ($159,000) in trust, pay Hana the remainder (after Carla's own agreed fee and costs) promptly, and not release Ben's claimed share until the parties agree, an arbitrator decides, or a court rules.

**Common mistakes**
1. Treating the paralegal's unsigned document as the contract because Hana "kept working with Ben." Silence and continued receipt of services already owed are not acceptance.
2. Defeating the amendment on consideration grounds in California without addressing Civ. Code § 1697.
3. Overlooking duress and undue influence because the amendment contains the § 6147 disclosures.
4. Giving a discharged contingency lawyer his contract percentage. *Fracasse* limits him to quantum meruit, payable on the contingency and capped by the contract.
5. Letting Carla pay Ben, or pay out the whole settlement to Hana, while the fee is disputed.
