# E-XO-029 — The auto-belay

## Facts

Summit Climb is an indoor climbing gym. Its walls are equipped with AutoGuard 3 auto-belays made by VertiCo, Inc. An auto-belay is a wall-mounted device with a retractable cable: a climber clips the cable to her harness and climbs, and when she lets go, the device's magnetic brake lowers her slowly to the floor. The AutoGuard 3 manual lists an operating range of 5°C to 40°C, tells owners to remove a unit from service immediately if it makes "any abnormal noise," and requires inspection by a certified technician every 12 months. It says nothing about heat affecting the brake.

Pia Shah, 31, joined Summit Climb in 2025 and signed a membership agreement stating: "Member releases Summit Climb from liability for any injury caused by the ordinary negligence of Summit Climb, including negligent inspection or maintenance of equipment."

On July 10 and July 14, 2026, members told the front desk that auto-belay #4 was making a grinding noise. Each time, a clerk wrote the report in the gym's maintenance-request log. Unit #4 stayed in service. It had last been inspected in February 2025, 17 months earlier.

On July 18, 2026, the gym's air conditioning failed, and its thermostat log recorded 34°C (93°F) that afternoon. Pia clipped into unit #4, performed the "tug test" described on a sign at each wall, and climbed 40 feet. When she let go, the device lowered her normally for about ten feet and then let the cable run free. She fell about 25 feet, breaking both ankles and her pelvis. The gym's security video shows her harness correctly clipped.

Pia sued VertiCo and Summit Climb in federal district court on diversity jurisdiction. Discovery showed that a 2022 VertiCo test report, written before unit #4 was sold to Summit Climb in 2023, noted "reduced braking torque above 30°C." In May 2026 VertiCo began making a redesigned brake that holds its torque up to 45°C and costs $40 more per unit; it did not notify existing owners. In August 2026, after Pia's fall, VertiCo sent all owners a safety notice limiting use of the AutoGuard 3 to temperatures below 28°C and offering free brake retrofits. VertiCo concedes that the redesigned brake was technically feasible but contends that the original design was safe when properly maintained.

At trial the following evidence was offered:

**(a)** Pia offered evidence of VertiCo's May 2026 redesign.

**(b)** Pia offered VertiCo's August 2026 safety notice and retrofit offer.

**(c)** Pia offered the maintenance-request log through the gym's office manager, who testified that clerks must enter every member report in the log when it is received. The July 10 entry reads: "Member says #4 grinding when lowering." The July 14 entry reads: "#4 grinding again per member — told Ben."

**(d)** Summit Climb called Ben, its floor manager, who has climbed for 15 years and has no engineering training. He testified that on the morning of July 18 he rode unit #4 down twice and "it sounded normal and lowered smoothly." He added that "the cable must have jumped the spool because her harness loop was twisted, which strips the brake."

**(e)** Pia called Dr. Lena Ortiz, a mechanical engineer who specializes in fall-protection equipment. Using the standard method for measuring braking torque, she tested three AutoGuard 3 units made in 2023 in a temperature chamber and found that braking torque fell about 40% at 34°C, enough to let the cable run free under the weight of an adult of Pia's size. Relying on the thermostat log and the video, she opined that heat-induced brake fade, not a harness problem, caused the fall. She did not test unit #4, which VertiCo had taken apart in its own laboratory.

## Call of the Question

1. What claims may Pia assert against VertiCo and against Summit Climb, and what defenses may each raise? Discuss.
2. Assuming all appropriate objections were made, should the court admit items (a) through (e)? Discuss. Answer according to the Federal Rules of Evidence.

## Model Answer

The call does not specify state law for the tort claims, so general principles apply; the evidence questions are governed by the Federal Rules of Evidence.

### 1. Pia's claims and the defenses

#### A. Pia v. VertiCo: strict products liability

**Prima facie case.** A commercial seller is strictly liable for physical harm caused by a product that was defective when it left the seller's control, if it reached the user without substantial change. VertiCo manufactures and sells auto-belays; unit #4 reached the gym in 2023 and was used as designed; Pia suffered serious physical injury. The contested element is defect.

**Design defect.** A product is defectively designed if it fails to perform as safely as an ordinary consumer would expect when used in an intended or reasonably foreseeable way, or if the risks of the design outweigh its benefits, usually shown by a reasonable alternative design (R2d § 402A; R3d PL § 2(b)).
- *Consumer expectations.* A climber expects an auto-belay to lower her safely at the temperatures the manufacturer says it can operate in. The manual lists a range up to 40°C; the brake failed at 34°C. A safety device that free-falls in a hot room fails ordinary expectations. VertiCo will argue that consumers cannot form expectations about magnetic-brake engineering, but the expectation here is simple: the device should not drop a climber.
- *Risk-utility.* The danger is grave (falls of 25 feet or more) and foreseeable (an indoor gym's cooling can fail in summer, as it did here). VertiCo's own 2022 report identified the problem. A safer alternative design existed: the May 2026 brake holds torque to 45°C, costs only $40 more, and VertiCo concedes it was technically feasible. Nothing suggests the redesign made the product less useful. VertiCo will say the original design was safe "when properly maintained," but maintenance does not cure a brake that loses 40% of its torque from heat alone.

VertiCo will add that the 2026 brake did not exist when it sold unit #4 in 2023, and design defect is judged at the time of distribution. Pia will respond that VertiCo concedes the redesign was technically feasible, that it knew of the heat problem from its own 2022 report, and that the fix is a $40 component; the state of the art bears on feasibility, and nothing suggests the fix was beyond reach in 2023.

**The design was likely defective when it left VertiCo in 2023.**

**Warning defect.** A product is defective for inadequate warnings when its foreseeable risks, known or reasonably knowable at distribution, could have been reduced by reasonable warnings. The 2022 report shows VertiCo knew of reduced braking above 30°C before it sold unit #4, yet the manual said nothing about heat and affirmatively stated an operating range up to 40°C, which was misleading. A warning was cheap to give. Causation requires that an adequate warning would have been heeded: had the manual limited use above 30°C, a gym whose air conditioning failed on a 34°C afternoon would likely have closed the auto-belay walls. **A warning defect is likely established.**

**VertiCo's defenses.**
- *Comparative fault and misuse.* Pia performed the tug test and was correctly clipped, as the video shows. Using the device at 34°C was within the manual's stated range, so it was not misuse at all, let alone unforeseeable misuse.
- *Alteration or intervening cause.* VertiCo will blame the gym's failure to inspect and to remove unit #4. That is not a substantial alteration of the product, and owners' maintenance lapses are foreseeable; at most the gym's fault is apportioned to the gym.
- *Release and assumption of risk.* The release names only Summit Climb, so it does not protect VertiCo. Falling may be an inherent risk of climbing, but the failure of the device designed to prevent falls is not a risk Pia knowingly accepted.

**Pia is likely to prevail against VertiCo on design and warning theories.**

#### B. Pia v. Summit Climb: negligence

**Duty.** Pia, a paying member, was a business invitee. A possessor owes invitees reasonable care, including a duty to inspect for and discover dangerous conditions and then to repair them or warn.

**Breach.** Members reported a grinding noise from unit #4 twice, on July 10 and July 14; the manufacturer's manual required removal "immediately" for "any abnormal noise"; and the July 14 entry says the floor manager was told. The unit was also five months overdue for its annual inspection. Keeping a life-safety device in service in those circumstances falls below reasonable care. Summit Climb will argue that Ben rode the unit twice on the morning of July 18 and it worked normally, and that it had no reason to know of a heat problem the manual did not disclose. That explains the heat issue, but not the decision to ignore reports the manufacturer said required immediate removal.

**Causation.** Summit Climb will argue that the brake failed from heat fade, a design defect, not from whatever caused the grinding. But under the but-for test, if the gym had removed unit #4 as the manual required, Pia would not have used it on July 18. Even if both the design and the gym's lapses contributed, each was a substantial factor in an indivisible injury. A fall from a malfunctioning belay is exactly the risk that made removal necessary, so proximate cause is satisfied.

**Apportionment.** Because the injury is indivisible, the traditional rule makes VertiCo and Summit Climb jointly and severally liable, subject to the jurisdiction's comparative-fault and contribution rules.

**The release (express assumption of risk).** A plaintiff who expressly agrees in advance to release the defendant from liability for its negligence is barred if the agreement is clear and unambiguous and covers the conduct, unless it violates public policy (for example, an essential public service under the *Tunkl* factors) or the defendant's conduct was grossly negligent, reckless or intentional.
- *Clarity and scope.* The release expressly covers "ordinary negligence ... including negligent inspection or maintenance of equipment," the very conduct at issue.
- *Public policy.* A recreational climbing gym is not an essential service, and courts generally enforce such releases for ordinary negligence.
- *Gross negligence.* Pia will argue that ignoring two specific reports about a life-safety device, despite the manufacturer's instruction to remove it immediately, with the floor manager informed and the inspection five months overdue, is an extreme departure from ordinary care. Summit Climb will respond that it responded reasonably by having Ben test the unit that morning, and that a missed inspection deadline is ordinary negligence.

A jury could reasonably find gross negligence. **The release likely bars Pia's ordinary-negligence claim, but not a claim for gross negligence, which presents a jury question on these facts.**

### 2. The evidentiary rulings

**Relevance.** Each item bears on defect, notice, breach or causation, so each is relevant (FRE 401–402) unless a specific rule excludes it.

**(a) The May 2026 redesign.** FRE 407 excludes measures taken *after* an injury that would have made it less likely, when offered to prove negligence, a product defect or a need for a warning. VertiCo redesigned the brake in May 2026, two months *before* Pia's July 18 fall. A pre-accident measure is not a subsequent remedial measure, and the policy of encouraging post-accident repairs does not apply. The redesign is strong evidence of a reasonable alternative design, its cost and its practicality, and its timing (without notice to existing owners) is relevant to the warning claim. Its probative value is not substantially outweighed by unfair prejudice (FRE 403). **Admissible.**

**(b) The August 2026 safety notice and retrofit offer.** These measures came after Pia's fall and would have made her injury less likely. Since 1997, FRE 407 expressly bars such evidence when offered to prove "a defect in a product or its design, or a need for a warning or instruction," so it applies to Pia's strict-liability claims as well as negligence. The rule permits the evidence for another purpose, such as impeachment or, if disputed, ownership, control or feasibility. VertiCo concedes feasibility, and ownership and control are not disputed. The impeachment exception would open only if a VertiCo witness testified, for example, that the AutoGuard 3 was safe at any temperature up to 40°C; VertiCo's general position that the design was safe "when properly maintained" should not, by itself, be enough, because courts read the exception narrowly. (Under the California Evidence Code, *Ault* would admit post-accident measures to prove a defect in strict liability, but the call names the FRE.) **Excluded, unless VertiCo opens the door; if admitted for impeachment, the court should give a limiting instruction (FRE 105).**

**(c) The maintenance-request log.**
- *Authentication.* The office manager's testimony about how the log is kept is sufficient to support a finding that it is Summit Climb's log (FRE 901).
- *Layers.* Each entry contains two statements: the clerk's written entry and the member's oral report (FRE 805).
- *Against Summit Climb, to show notice.* Pia offers the entries to show that the gym was told about grinding, and on July 14 that Ben was told. For that purpose the members' reports are not offered for their truth, so they are not hearsay (FRE 801(c)). The clerk's entries are statements by Summit Climb's employees about matters within the scope of their job, offered against the gym (FRE 801(d)(2)(D)); they also qualify as business records, because the gym regularly records member reports when received and its custodian laid the foundation (FRE 803(6)).
- *To prove that unit #4 was actually grinding.* The members were not acting under any business duty to Summit Climb, so FRE 803(6) does not cover their statements, and no other exception is shown (nothing establishes that the reports were made while or immediately after the members perceived the noise, as a present sense impression would require). That layer is inadmissible for its truth, including against VertiCo.
- *Limiting instruction.* The court should admit the log against Summit Climb on notice and instruct the jury not to treat it as proof that the unit was malfunctioning or as evidence against VertiCo (FRE 105).

**Admissible for notice against Summit Climb, with a limiting instruction.**

**(d) Ben's testimony.** A lay witness may give an opinion that is rationally based on his perception, helpful, and not based on scientific, technical or other specialized knowledge (FRE 701).
- *"It sounded normal and lowered smoothly."* Ben rode the unit twice that morning and is describing what he heard and felt. That is a permissible lay opinion, and its weight is for the jury.
- *"The cable must have jumped the spool because her harness loop was twisted, which strips the brake."* This explains the internal mechanics of a brake failure Ben did not observe. It rests on technical knowledge, so FRE 701(c) bars it as lay opinion. Summit Climb cannot recast him as an expert: climbing experience does not qualify him on magnetic-brake engineering (FRE 702), and the video, which shows a correctly clipped harness, leaves no factual basis for the opinion.

**The first statement is admissible; the mechanism opinion is excluded.**

**(e) Dr. Ortiz.** Under FRE 702, as amended in 2023, the proponent must show the court that it is more likely than not that the expert's specialized knowledge will help the jury, the testimony rests on sufficient facts or data, it is the product of reliable principles and methods, and the opinion reflects a reliable application of those methods to the facts. The judge acts as gatekeeper (*Daubert*), and after 2023 the sufficiency of the basis and the reliability of the application are admissibility questions, not just matters of weight.
- *Qualification and helpfulness.* A mechanical engineer specializing in fall-protection equipment is qualified, and brake mechanics are beyond lay knowledge.
- *Sufficient facts or data.* She tested three units of the same model and year, at the temperature recorded in the gym's thermostat log, under a load matching Pia's weight, and she considered the video. VertiCo will argue that she never tested unit #4. But VertiCo took that unit apart in its own laboratory, and testing same-model exemplars under recreated conditions is an accepted substitute. Experts may rely on the thermostat log and video, which are themselves admissible (FRE 703).
- *Reliable method.* Standard torque testing in a temperature chamber is testable, follows established procedures and is generally accepted (*Daubert* factors).
- *Reliable application.* She matched the conditions of the accident and addressed the main alternative cause (a harness problem) by relying on the video showing a correct clip. VertiCo will argue that the grinding reports suggest brake wear, an alternative cause she did not address. That is a fair point: her opinion that heat fade alone "caused the fall" should stay within what her testing shows. The court may admit her opinion that heat fade was sufficient to cause, and did cause, the free fall, and leave the wear question to cross-examination, or require her to account for wear before stating sole causation.

**Admissible**, with the court ensuring the causation opinion does not go beyond what her method supports.

### Summary

VertiCo is likely strictly liable on design and warning theories, with no effective defense against Pia. Summit Climb was negligent; its release bars ordinary negligence but likely not gross negligence, which is for the jury. Items (a), (c) (for notice), the first part of (d), and (e) are admissible; item (b) is excluded unless VertiCo opens the door; Ben's mechanism opinion is excluded.

**Common mistakes**
1. Excluding pre-accident design changes under FRE 407. The rule covers only measures taken after the injury.
2. Forgetting that FRE 407 applies to product-defect and failure-to-warn claims, unlike California's § 1151 in strict liability.
3. Treating a business record as covering statements of outsiders who were under no business duty.
4. Letting a lay witness explain technical failure mechanisms. That opinion requires FRE 702 qualification.
5. Assuming a release bars gross negligence or protects a non-party manufacturer.

**Accuracy notes.** Tort rules follow R-TRT-018, -038, -041, -050, -071, -072, -081, -083, -084 and -087; evidence rules follow R-EVD-002, -003, -007, -013, -048, -054 to -056, -059, -074, -084, -090, -092 and -099. The point that the release does not reach gross negligence follows R-TRT-071 (California's *City of Santa Barbara* rule); a minority of States enforce releases more broadly.
