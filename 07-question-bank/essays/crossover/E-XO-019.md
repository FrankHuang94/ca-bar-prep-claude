## Facts

Monterey Hospitality Group, Inc. ("MHG") owns six hotels. In January 2025, its outside lawyer, Joan Whitfield, a partner at Whitfield & Cho LLP, negotiated a three-year, $2.4 million agreement under which Pacific Tile Distributors, Inc. ("Pacific") would supply tile for MHG's renovations. During a break in the final negotiating session on January 22, only Joan and Pacific's sales vice president, Leo Marsh, who led Pacific's negotiating team, were in the room. Joan says Leo told her, "Don't worry — you can cancel any purchase order within 30 days." The written contract, signed January 24, says nothing about cancellation.

On January 25, Joan emailed MHG's CEO, Rita Okafor, with the subject line "Privileged & Confidential": "Before you sign purchase orders, note that Leo agreed orally that we can cancel any order within 30 days. I will try to get this in writing." She never did.

In August 2025, MHG cancelled a $600,000 order 25 days after placing it. Pacific sued MHG for breach of contract in Monterey County Superior Court. Joan and her partner Ken Cho appeared for MHG; Joan planned to try the case and to testify about Leo's statement. Leo denies making it. Assume the court has ruled that evidence of an oral cancellation agreement is not barred by the written contract.

At her deposition in December 2025, Rita testified, without objection from Joan: "I signed orders freely because Joan told me in an email that Leo had agreed we could cancel within 30 days."

In March 2026, Joan's paralegal, assembling a document production, mistakenly attached to an email to Pacific's lawyer, Raj Patel, a memo from Joan to Rita titled "PRIVILEGED — ATTORNEY WORK PRODUCT — Trial Risk Assessment." It said: "Candidly, Leo's words were less definite than my January email suggested — something like 'we can work with you on cancellations within 30 days.' A jury could find there was no firm agreement, and MHG may then ask why I did not put a cancellation clause in the contract." Raj read the entire memo, forwarded it to Pacific's CEO, and listed it as a trial exhibit. He did not tell Joan. Whitfield & Cho has no procedure for checking attachments before documents go to opposing counsel. Joan learned of the error when she saw Pacific's exhibit list in May.

Pacific moved to disqualify Joan as trial counsel. At the hearing, Joan told the judge: "Leo clearly and unequivocally agreed to a 30-day cancellation right. I have never had any doubt about it." She filed a form signed by Rita stating, "MHG consents to Joan Whitfield acting as trial counsel although she will testify." Joan had not explained the form's significance to Rita. Ken Cho, who has tried many commercial cases, is available to try this one.

At trial:
(a) MHG offers Joan's testimony that Leo told her MHG could cancel any order within 30 days.
(b) On cross-examination, Pacific suggests that Joan invented the cancellation term after the dispute arose, to protect herself from a malpractice claim. MHG then offers Joan's January 25 email.
(c) Pacific offers the Trial Risk Assessment memo.

## Call of the Question

1. Under the California Evidence Code, how should the court rule on the admissibility of (a) Joan's testimony about Leo's statement, (b) the January 25 email, and (c) the Trial Risk Assessment memo? Discuss.
2. What ethical violations, if any, have Joan and Raj committed? Answer according to California and ABA authorities.

## Model Answer

### 1. Evidence (California Evidence Code)

**(a) Joan's testimony about Leo's statement**

- **Relevance.** Evidence is relevant if it has any tendency in reason to prove or disprove a disputed fact of consequence (§ 210). Whether Pacific agreed to a 30-day cancellation right is the central disputed fact. Relevant.
- **Personal knowledge and competence.** A witness may testify only about matters within her personal knowledge (§ 702). Joan heard Leo's words herself. Her role as MHG's lawyer does not make her incompetent as a witness; whether she may also act as advocate is an ethics question (Part 2).
- **Hearsay?** Hearsay is an out-of-court statement offered to prove the truth of the matter stated (§ 1200). Leo's words are offered as the **legally operative words** of an agreement, as an offer or assurance that is itself a term of the deal. They matter because they were said, not because they were true when said. They are not hearsay.
- **Alternatively, an authorized admission.** If the statement were treated as offered for its truth, it would still be admissible against Pacific. California has no general rule admitting every employee's statement against the employer, but it admits a statement by a person **authorized by the party to make statements concerning the subject matter** (§ 1222). Leo, a vice president who led Pacific's negotiating team, was authorized to speak for Pacific about the contract's terms.
- **§ 352.** Pacific may argue that a lawyer-witness carries unfair extra credibility with a jury. That goes to weight and can be addressed by cross-examination and instructions; it does not substantially outweigh the testimony's high probative value.

**Admissible.**

**(b) The January 25 email**

- **Hearsay.** Offered to show that Leo agreed to a cancellation right, the email is Joan's out-of-court statement offered for its truth (with Leo's words as an inner layer, which are operative words or an authorized admission as in (a)). An exception is needed for Joan's layer.
- **Prior consistent statement (§§ 791(b), 1236).** A testifying witness's prior consistent statement is admissible, for its truth, to rebut an express or implied charge that her testimony was recently fabricated or influenced by bias or improper motive, if the statement was made **before** the alleged motive arose. Joan testifies and is subject to cross-examination. Pacific expressly charged that she invented the term after the dispute arose to protect herself from a malpractice claim.
  - *MHG:* The dispute arose in August 2025, and any malpractice concern arose only then. The email was written in January 2025, before any motive to fabricate existed.
  - *Pacific:* Joan's motive to protect herself arose on January 24, when she let MHG sign a contract with no cancellation clause. She wrote the email the next day to paper over that omission, so it does not predate the motive.
  - *Resolution:* On January 25 there was no dispute, no cancelled order and no claim. The email reads as routine advice to a client about how to use the contract. A court would likely find that the improper motive Pacific alleges, fear of a malpractice claim, arose with the August dispute. The email is admissible under § 1236 because it complies with § 791(b).
- **Privilege.** The email is a confidential communication from lawyer to client, made to give legal advice, and so within the lawyer-client privilege (§§ 952, 954). The privilege belongs to MHG, the client. A holder waives the privilege when, without coercion, it discloses a significant part of the communication or consents to its disclosure (§ 912(a)).
  - Rita, MHG's CEO, testified at her deposition, without objection, that "Joan told me in an email that Leo had agreed we could cancel within 30 days." That disclosed the substance of the email, and with it a significant part of the communication, so the privilege was waived as to that communication.
  - In any event, MHG, the holder, is now offering the email itself, which is a further waiver. Pacific has no privilege to assert over MHG's communication.
- **Authentication.** Joan can authenticate the email as one she wrote and sent.

**Admissible** as a prior consistent statement; the privilege has been waived.

**(c) The Trial Risk Assessment memo**

- **Relevance; hearsay.** Joan's statement that Leo's words were "less definite" contradicts her trial testimony and her January email. If it were not protected, it would be admissible both to impeach her and, as a prior inconsistent statement of a testifying witness, for its truth (§§ 770, 1235). But protection is the threshold question.
- **Privilege.** The memo is a confidential communication from MHG's lawyer to MHG's CEO, giving legal advice about trial risk. It is privileged, and MHG holds the privilege.
- **Work product.** A writing that reflects an attorney's impressions, conclusions, opinions or legal theories is **not discoverable under any circumstances** (CCP § 2018.030(a)). The memo is Joan's own assessment of the evidence and of MHG's exposure. It is absolute work product, and the protection belongs to the attorney.
- **Inadvertent disclosure is not a waiver.** Waiver requires disclosure by the holder or the holder's consent (§ 912(a)). A paralegal's mistaken attachment is neither. California courts hold that an inadvertent disclosure does not waive the privilege (*State Compensation Insurance Fund v. WPS, Inc.*), and the same reasoning applies to Joan's work product. Under federal law the analysis would turn on reasonable precautions and prompt rectification (FRE 502(b)), but this case is governed by California law.
- **Did the email waiver reach the memo?** Pacific will argue that MHG cannot use Joan's favorable January email while hiding her later doubts. But § 912 speaks of waiver as to the disclosed communication, and the memo is a different communication, written more than a year later, that MHG never chose to disclose. Its separate status as absolute work product reinforces the result.

**Inadmissible.** Pacific remains free to cross-examine Joan about her recollection, but not with the memo.

### 2. Ethical violations

California lawyers are governed by the California Rules of Professional Conduct and the State Bar Act; the ABA Model Rules are persuasive.

**A. Joan: lawyer as witness.**
- *ABA (MR 3.7):* A lawyer may not act as advocate at a trial in which she is likely to be a necessary witness unless her testimony relates to an uncontested issue or the nature and value of legal services, or disqualification would work **substantial hardship** on the client.
- *California (rule 3.7):* Same, except that the third exception is that the lawyer has obtained the client's **informed written consent** (for a lawyer representing the People or a governmental entity, the consent must come from the head of the office or a designee, rule 3.7(a)(3)), and the court keeps discretion to disqualify despite consent (rule 3.7 cmt. [3]).
- *Application:* Joan is the only witness besides Leo to the disputed statement, and Leo denies it. Her testimony is necessary and the issue is hotly contested. Under the ABA rule, there is no substantial hardship: Ken Cho, an experienced trial partner, can try the case, because rule 3.7's bar is not imputed to other lawyers in the firm. Under the California rule, Joan relies on Rita's signed form. But "informed written consent" requires written disclosure of the relevant circumstances and material risks, such as the jury's possible confusion of advocate and witness and the risk that Joan's credibility, not the evidence, becomes the issue. Joan did not explain the form. The consent is not informed. Even with proper consent, a court could disqualify Joan given the centrality of her testimony and her personal stake.

**Conclusion:** Joan may not act as trial counsel under either system on these facts. She may continue pretrial work, and Ken may try the case.

**B. Joan: personal-interest conflict.**
- *Rule (both systems):* A lawyer may not represent a client if there is a significant risk that the representation will be materially limited by the lawyer's own interests, unless she reasonably believes she can represent the client competently and diligently and the client gives informed written consent (rule 1.7(b)) or informed consent confirmed in writing (MR 1.7(a)(2), (b)).
- *Application:* Joan's own memo recognizes that if the jury rejects the oral term, MHG "may then ask why I did not put a cancellation clause in the contract." She has a personal stake in winning on the oral-agreement theory, which may skew her advice about settlement and her own testimony. That is a significant risk of material limitation. There is no record that she disclosed it or obtained MHG's consent, and Rita's signed form addresses only the advocate-witness question.

**Conclusion:** Joan violated rule 1.7(b) and MR 1.7. She should disclose her exposure, advise MHG that it may wish to consult independent counsel, and obtain proper consent or withdraw.

**C. Joan: candor to the tribunal.**
- *Rule (both systems):* A lawyer may not knowingly make a false statement of fact or law to a tribunal or fail to correct a false statement of material fact previously made by the lawyer (rule 3.3(a)(1); MR 3.3(a)(1)). California also requires a lawyer never to seek to mislead a judge by an artifice or false statement of fact or law (B&P § 6068(d)).
- *Application:* At the disqualification hearing, Joan told the judge that Leo "clearly and unequivocally agreed" and that she had "never had any doubt about it." In her Trial Risk Assessment memo, written before the hearing, she had said that Leo's words were "less definite" than her email suggested and that a jury could find no firm agreement. She knew her statement to the court was false at least as to her lack of doubt, and it was material to whether she would be a credible, necessary witness. Joan may argue she was advocating her client's position, but "I have never had any doubt" is a statement of fact about her own state of mind, not argument.
- *Correction:* Both systems require her to correct the statement. She can do so by withdrawing it, without volunteering the memo. If correction required revealing protected information, the ABA rule would require disclosure if necessary (MR 3.3(c)); in California, remedial measures stop short of disclosures barred by § 6068(e).

**Conclusion:** Joan violated rule 3.3(a)(1), MR 3.3(a)(1) and § 6068(d) and must correct her statement.

**D. Joan: confidentiality precautions and supervision of the paralegal.**
- *Rules:* California requires a lawyer to maintain client confidences "at every peril" (B&P § 6068(e)(1); rule 1.6), including by taking reasonable precautions against inadvertent disclosure. MR 1.6(c) expressly requires reasonable efforts to prevent inadvertent or unauthorized disclosure. Lawyers with managerial authority must make reasonable efforts to ensure that nonlawyer assistants' conduct is compatible with the lawyer's obligations (rule 5.3; MR 5.3).
- *Application:* The firm had no procedure for checking attachments before documents went to opposing counsel, and the paralegal's mistake sent the most sensitive document in the case to the adversary. Joan, a partner, is responsible for that gap. A single mistake by staff is not automatically a violation, but the complete absence of safeguards for outgoing productions is a failure to take reasonable precautions. Joan must also promptly inform MHG of the disclosure (rule 1.4; MR 1.4) and seek its return.

**Conclusion:** likely violations of rule 5.3 and the duty to safeguard confidences under both systems.

**E. Raj: the inadvertently sent memo.**
- *California (rule 4.4; *Rico v. Mitsubishi Motors Corp.*):* A lawyer who receives a writing that he knows or reasonably should know was inadvertently sent, and that is privileged or work product, must refrain from examining it more than necessary to determine that it is privileged, and must promptly notify the sender.
- *ABA (MR 4.4(b)):* The lawyer must promptly notify the sender. Whether he must stop reading or return the document is left to other law.
- *Application:* The memo was conspicuously labeled "PRIVILEGED — ATTORNEY WORK PRODUCT" and was a lawyer's candid assessment of her own case, plainly not something MHG meant to produce. Raj read all of it, forwarded it to his client, listed it as an exhibit and never notified Joan.

**Conclusion:** Raj violated California rule 4.4 by reading beyond what was necessary and by not notifying Joan, and he violated MR 4.4(b) by not notifying her. Under *Rico*, a lawyer who exploits such a document may be disqualified, and Pacific's use of it at trial should be barred.

**Common mistakes**
1. Calling Leo's words hearsay without considering that contract terms are legally operative words, or relying on an FRE-style employee-admission rule that California does not have.
2. Admitting the email as a prior consistent statement without asking when the alleged motive to fabricate arose.
3. Treating an inadvertent production as a waiver. In California waiver requires the holder's disclosure or consent.
4. Applying an ABA-style "substantial hardship" exception under the California rule, or treating any signed form as informed written consent.
5. Treating Joan's hearing statement as mere advocacy. A statement about her own lack of doubt is a statement of fact.

**Accuracy notes.** California's limit on remedial disclosure when correction would reveal confidential information follows R-PRF-061 [S-550] [S-1265]; the answer does not depend on it, because Joan can correct her own statement by withdrawing it. The California lawyer-witness rule, including the government-lawyer consent provision and the court's retained discretion, follows R-PRF-069 [S-1253]. The answer does not decide whether California recognizes a broad subject-matter waiver comparable to FRE 502(a); it rests the memo ruling on § 912's focus on the disclosed communication and on absolute work-product protection. Section 1222's "authorized to speak" requirement is stated as in R-EVD-090; the authority of a vice president leading the negotiation is a fact question argued from the facts given.
