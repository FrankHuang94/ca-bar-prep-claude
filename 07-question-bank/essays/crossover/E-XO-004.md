# E-XO-004 — Maya and Raj v. Valley Hops Brewing (Torts + Remedies)

## Facts

Valley Hops Brewing Co. ("Brewco") has operated a small brewery since 2015 in a light-industrial zone at the edge of the town of Elm Grove. In 2022 Maya and Raj paid $900,000 for a house on two acres directly next door, in the adjoining residential zone. They knew the brewery was there; at the time it was quiet and caused them no problems.

In April 2025 Brewco expanded in two ways. First, it opened an outdoor beer garden with amplified live music Thursday through Sunday from 6 p.m. to midnight. Sound measured at Maya and Raj's bedroom windows reaches 70 decibels; Elm Grove's noise ordinance limits noise in residential zones to 55 decibels after 10 p.m. Maya, a nurse who works early shifts, now sleeps poorly four nights a week. Second, Brewco built an outdoor silo for spent grain. On warm days a sour smell drifts onto Maya and Raj's property, and they have stopped using their patio.

Also in April 2025, Brewco's contractor installed a concrete culvert to carry the brewery's wash water to the town storm drain. Because of the contractor's survey error, the last four feet of the culvert lie on Maya and Raj's land, and during Brewco's daily wash cycle water spills from its end into their vegetable garden, which has flooded repeatedly and lost most of its plants.

On May 15, 2025, Maya and Raj wrote to Brewco's general manager, Tom, describing the noise, the smell, the culvert's location and the flooding, and enclosing a surveyor's map. Tom, who sets Brewco's operating policies with its owners, replied: "We were here first. We're not changing anything." Several other residents of the street have also complained to the town about the music.

An appraiser says the house would now sell for about $800,000 instead of $900,000 because of the noise and odor. The strip of land under the culvert is worth about $2,000. Removing the culvert and rerouting the drain would cost Brewco about $120,000 and require a three-week shutdown; adding a short pipe extension to carry the water from the culvert's end directly into the storm drain would cost about $8,000. The beer garden generates about 30% of Brewco's revenue; Brewco employs 45 people, and the town council has praised the beer garden as good for local business. Covering the silo with an odor-control system would cost about $25,000.

Maya and Raj sued Brewco in January 2026.

## Call of the Question

1. What tort claims can Maya and Raj assert against Brewco, and what defenses can Brewco raise? Discuss.
2. What remedies can Maya and Raj obtain? Discuss.

## Model Answer

### Call 1 — Tort claims and defenses

#### A. Private nuisance (noise and odor)

**Rule.** A private nuisance is a nontrespassory invasion of another's interest in the private use and enjoyment of land that is both substantial and unreasonable. Substantial means offensive or annoying to a person of ordinary sensibilities in the community. An intentional invasion is unreasonable if the gravity of the harm outweighs the utility of the defendant's conduct, or if the harm is serious and the defendant could compensate for it and still carry on. (California defines a nuisance as anything injurious to health, offensive to the senses, or an obstruction to the free use of property that interferes with the comfortable enjoyment of life or property, Civ. Code § 3479.)

**Substantial interference.** Amplified music reaching 70 decibels at bedroom windows until midnight, four nights a week, would disturb an ordinary person's sleep, not just a sensitive one; Maya's early shifts make the harm worse but are not the reason it is substantial. The odor has made the patio unusable. Brewco may argue that Maya is unusually sensitive, but the decibel level exceeds the town's own nighttime limit by 15 decibels, which shows the noise is offensive by community standards.

**Intentional.** After the May 15 letter, Brewco knew its music and silo were invading Maya and Raj's enjoyment and continued them, so the invasion is intentional.

**Unreasonable.** On the utility side, the beer garden brings in 30% of revenue, supports 45 jobs and is popular. On the gravity side, the harm is to sleep and health in a residential zone, recurring four nights a week; the noise violates the ordinance; the odor deprives the owners of outdoor use of their home; and the property has lost about $100,000 in value. Brewco could reduce the harm at modest cost (ending music at 10 p.m. or lowering the volume; a $25,000 odor-control cover) and still operate profitably. A serious harm that the defendant can avoid or compensate for while continuing its business is unreasonable.

**Conclusion:** Brewco's music and odor are a private nuisance.

#### B. Public nuisance

**Rule.** A public nuisance is an unreasonable interference with a right common to the general public, such as public peace or comfort; violation of a public ordinance is strong evidence. A private person may sue only if she suffers harm different in kind, not merely in degree, from that suffered by the public.

**Application.** The music violates the noise ordinance and disturbs several residents of the street, so it may be a public nuisance. Maya and Raj's injury is arguably different in kind: as the adjoining owners, they suffer sleep loss in their own home and a $100,000 decline in property value, not just the general annoyance shared by passers-by. Brewco will argue that other neighbors suffer the same kind of noise, just less of it. Because their claim succeeds as private nuisance anyway, public nuisance adds little.

**Conclusion:** Possibly a public nuisance with special injury; the private nuisance claim is stronger.

#### C. Trespass

**Rule.** A defendant is liable for trespass if he intentionally enters land in another's possession, causes a thing to enter it, or fails to remove a thing he is under a duty to remove. The only intent required is the intent to cause the entry; a mistaken belief that one has a right to be there is no defense, and no actual harm is needed.

**The culvert.** Brewco, through its contractor, intentionally placed the culvert where it now sits; the survey error means only that it was mistaken about the boundary, which is no defense. A hirer is generally not liable for an independent contractor's negligence, but Brewco is not being held vicariously liable for careless work: it ordered the very structure that intrudes and, more importantly, after the May 15 letter and survey map, it has kept the culvert on Maya and Raj's land and refused to remove it. That is a continuing trespass by Brewco itself.

**The water.** Each time Brewco runs its wash cycle, it sends water out of the culvert onto the garden. After the May 15 letter, Brewco knew with substantial certainty that its wash water would enter their land, so each discharge is an intentional trespass, and it has caused actual harm (dead plants).

**Noise and odor are not trespass.** Sound waves and smells are intangible; they are analyzed as nuisance, not trespass. In California, intangible intrusions are trespass only if they cause physical damage to the property (*Wilson v. Interlake Steel Co.*), which the music and odor have not.

**Conclusion:** Brewco is liable for trespass for the culvert and the water discharges.

#### D. Brewco's defenses

- **Coming to the nuisance / "We were here first."** Moving next to an existing activity does not bar a nuisance claim; it is only a factor in the reasonableness balance and in fashioning a remedy. Here the argument barely applies: Maya and Raj came to a quiet brewery in 2022; the beer garden and silo that cause the harm began in 2025, three years after they arrived. Brewco came to them, not the reverse.
- **Zoning compliance.** Operating a brewery in a light-industrial zone is lawful, and compliance with zoning is relevant but not conclusive. It does not authorize noise above the residential ordinance limit, and it says nothing about invading a neighbor's land with a culvert.
- **Social utility.** Jobs and the town council's praise weigh in the balance but do not license a substantial, avoidable harm; they matter more to the remedy (below).
- **Consent, laches.** Maya and Raj objected within weeks and sued within a year, so there is no consent or unreasonable delay.

**Conclusion:** No defense defeats liability; utility and zoning will affect the scope of relief.

### Call 2 — Remedies

#### A. Damages

**Nuisance — permanent or continuing?** If a nuisance is permanent (it cannot or will not be abated at reasonable cost), the plaintiff recovers past and future harm in one action, measured by diminution in market value. If it is continuing (abatable), the plaintiff recovers only past damages, including loss of use and discomfort and annoyance, and may sue again or seek an injunction.
- The music and odor can be abated at reasonable cost (earlier closing or lower volume; a $25,000 cover), so the nuisance is continuing. Maya and Raj recover past damages from April 2025: discomfort and annoyance, Maya's lost sleep, and loss of use of the patio.
- If the court refuses an injunction (see C), it should treat the nuisance as permanent and award the $100,000 diminution in value, as in *Boomer v. Atlantic Cement Co.* They cannot recover both the full diminution and an injunction that removes the cause of that diminution.

**Trespass.** Trespass is actionable without harm, so nominal damages are available at minimum. They also recover actual harm: the lost garden plants (measured by the lesser of diminution in value or cost of restoration, though personal-use replanting costs are usually recoverable), and the reasonable rental value of the four-foot strip for the period of occupation. If the culvert is allowed to remain permanently, they recover the value of the land taken (about $2,000) or the resulting diminution of the parcel.

#### B. Injunction against the noise and odor

A permanent injunction requires (1) an inadequate legal remedy, (2) a property right or protectable interest, (3) feasibility of enforcement, (4) a balance of hardships (including the public interest) favoring relief, and (5) no equitable defense.

1. **Inadequate legal remedy.** The noise and odor recur every week. Damages would require repeated lawsuits (multiplicity of suits), and loss of sleep and of the enjoyment of one's home is hard to value. Land is unique. Damages are inadequate.
2. **Protectable interest.** Maya and Raj's interest in the use and enjoyment of their land is a property right.
3. **Feasibility.** An order limiting music to 55 decibels after 10 p.m. (or ending it at 10 p.m.) is prohibitory and easy to enforce by measuring sound. Requiring an odor-control cover is a one-time mandatory act that needs little supervision.
4. **Balancing of hardships.** Brewco will stress that the beer garden produces 30% of its revenue, supports 45 jobs and is popular, so a total shutdown would be a severe hardship and against the public interest. Maya and Raj's hardship is nightly sleep loss in their home, an ordinance violation, and a useless patio. Brewco's refusal after notice ("We're not changing anything") weighs against it. A total ban on the beer garden would likely be disproportionate, but a **tailored** injunction (music ending at 10 p.m., or limited to the ordinance level after 10 p.m., and odor control on the silo) would protect the plaintiffs at small cost to Brewco. Courts often choose such partial injunctions as the middle ground.
5. **Defenses.** No laches (prompt complaint and suit) and no unclean hands. Coming to the nuisance does not apply because the harm began after Maya and Raj arrived; a *Spur*-type compensated injunction (plaintiff pays the defendant's relocation costs) is therefore unwarranted. The *Boomer* alternative of permanent damages instead of an injunction is unattractive where, as here, the harm can be cheaply abated.

**Conclusion:** The court will likely grant a tailored injunction limiting the music's hours and volume and requiring odor control, plus past damages.

#### C. The culvert — removal or equitable easement?

**Rule.** A landowner is ordinarily entitled to a mandatory injunction requiring removal of an encroaching structure. Under the relative-hardship doctrine, a court may instead deny removal and award damages if (1) the encroacher acted innocently, not willfully or negligently, (2) the owner will not suffer irreparable injury from the encroachment, and (3) removal would cause hardship greatly disproportionate to the benefit. California courts may then create an equitable easement in the encroacher's favor on payment of compensation.

**Application.**
- *Innocence.* The culvert's placement resulted from the contractor's survey error, not a deliberate choice, so the initial encroachment was arguably innocent. Maya and Raj will respond that a survey error is negligence, and that after May 15 Brewco knowingly kept the culvert there and refused to act, which looks willful. This factor is contested.
- *Irreparable injury from the structure.* The four-foot strip under the culvert is worth about $2,000; the structure itself causes little harm. The real injury is the water discharge, which can be stopped separately.
- *Disproportionate hardship.* Removal would cost $120,000 and a three-week shutdown, against a $2,000 strip: a hardship roughly 60 times the benefit.
- *Tailoring.* An $8,000 pipe extension would stop all discharge onto the garden.

**Conclusion:** A court will likely (a) enjoin Brewco from discharging water onto Maya and Raj's land, requiring the $8,000 extension, and (b) either order removal of the culvert if it finds Brewco's post-notice conduct willful, or, more likely, deny removal and grant Brewco an equitable easement for the culvert on payment of fair compensation (the strip's value, any diminution, and past trespass damages).

#### D. Punitive damages and election

Punitive damages are available for torts committed with malice, oppression or fraud, proved in California by clear and convincing evidence (Civ. Code § 3294). Brewco's continued trespass and nuisance after notice ("We were here first. We're not changing anything") shows conscious disregard of the plaintiffs' rights, though a business dispute over noise and a mistaken boundary is not obviously "despicable." Because Brewco is an entity, the malice must be that of an officer, director or managing agent; Tom, the general manager who sets operating policy, likely qualifies. Punitive damages are possible but uncertain.

**Best remedies.** A tailored injunction on music and odor; an injunction against the water discharge; past damages for discomfort, lost sleep and the garden; and either removal of the culvert or compensation for an equitable easement. They should not seek the $100,000 permanent-diminution award if they obtain an injunction that abates the nuisance.

**Common mistakes**
1. Treating "we were here first" as a defense. It is a factor, and here the harmful activity began after the plaintiffs moved in.
2. Calling noise and odor a trespass. They are nuisance unless they cause physical damage (*Wilson*).
3. Excusing the culvert because the contractor made a survey error. Mistake about the boundary is no defense to trespass, and Brewco kept it there after notice.
4. Ordering the beer garden shut down without balancing, or refusing all relief because of jobs; tailor the injunction.
5. Ignoring the $8,000 pipe extension, which lets the court stop the harm without the $120,000 removal.

**Accuracy notes.** The answer does not rely on California's right-to-farm or statutory-authorization nuisance provisions (repo item V-609) or on Restatement section numbers (V-600, V-363). The tree-damage multiplier (Civ. Code § 3346) is not raised because the plants are garden crops, not trees or timber.
