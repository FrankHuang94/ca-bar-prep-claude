## Facts

Arturo Reyes and his first wife divorced in State X in 2010. Their only child, Ines, is now an adult. In 2011, in State X, Arturo signed a typed will in the presence of a notary public and one witness. The notary took Arturo's acknowledgment and affixed her seal; the witness watched Arturo sign and signed below his name. The will states: "I leave all of my property, including any home I own at my death, to my daughter, Ines. I have intentionally omitted all other persons from this will." At that time, State X law provided that a will signed by the testator before a notary and one witness is valid.

State X is not a community property state; under its law, property acquired during marriage belongs to the spouse who holds title.

In 2013, Arturo married Wen in State X. Before the wedding they signed a written premarital agreement providing: "In the event of divorce, each party waives any claim to the other party's separate property." From 2013 to 2015, while they lived in State X, Arturo saved part of his engineering salary in a brokerage account in his own name, now worth $400,000. In 2014 he used his salary to buy a cabin in State X, titled in his name alone, now worth $250,000.

In 2015, Arturo and Wen moved to California, where they lived for the rest of Arturo's life. In 2017 they bought a house in Sacramento with savings from their California salaries, taking title as "Arturo Reyes, a married man." They paid off the loan with their salaries. The house is now worth $900,000. Wen never signed anything about the house.

In 2020, Arturo's mother died and left him $200,000, which he deposited in a new savings account in his own name. Nothing else was ever deposited in that account, which now holds $210,000 with interest.

In 2022, Arturo bought a $100,000 life insurance policy on his life, naming Wen as beneficiary and paying the premiums from the couple's joint checking account. He told Wen, "This is so you can pay for my funeral and not have to worry."

Arturo died in 2026, domiciled in California, survived by Wen and Ines. He never made another will.

## Call of the Question

Answer according to California law.

1. Is Arturo's 2011 will valid?
2. What rights do Wen and Ines have in (a) the Sacramento house, (b) the brokerage account, (c) the inheritance account, (d) the State X cabin, and (e) the life insurance proceeds?

## Model Answer

### 1. Validity of the 2011 will

**California formalities.** An attested will must be signed by the testator and by at least two witnesses who, being present at the same time, witnessed the signing or acknowledgment and understood that the instrument was the testator's will (Prob. Code § 6110). Arturo's will had one witness. A notary's acknowledgment is a different act from attestation, so the will does not meet § 6110. It is typed, so it cannot be a holograph (§ 6111).

**Harmless error.** A witnessing defect is excused if clear and convincing evidence shows that, when he signed, the testator intended the document to be his will (§ 6110(c)(2)). The formal setting, a typed will signed before a notary and a witness, supports that intent, so the will might be saved this way.

**Choice of law.** The cleaner route is § 6113: a written will is validly executed if its execution complied with the law, at the time of execution, of the place where it was executed, or of the testator's domicile at execution or death, or with California law. In 2011, State X law validated a will signed before a notary and one witness, and Arturo signed it in State X while domiciled there. **The will is valid under § 6113.**

California has no rule that a later marriage revokes a will. Wen's protection lies in community property law and the omitted-spouse statute, discussed below.

### 2. Rights in Arturo's property

**Framework.** California is a community property state. Property acquired during marriage while domiciled in California is presumed to be community property (CP) (Fam. Code § 760); property acquired before marriage or by gift or inheritance is separate property (SP) (§ 770). At death, one-half of the CP belongs to the surviving spouse (Prob. Code § 100), and one-half of the decedent's quasi-community property (QCP) belongs to the surviving spouse (§ 101). The decedent may dispose by will only of his SP, his half of the CP and his half of his QCP (§ 6101). At death, QCP is property acquired by the decedent while domiciled elsewhere that would have been CP had he been domiciled in California, but it includes only **personal property wherever located and California real property**, not land in another state (§ 66).

#### (a) The Sacramento house

*Character.* The house was bought in 2017, during the marriage, while the couple was domiciled in California, with savings from their salaries, and the loan was paid with salaries. It is presumptively CP. Ines will rely on title: "Arturo Reyes, a married man," and the Evidence Code § 662 presumption that the owner of legal title owns the full beneficial title. But title in one spouse's name does not make CP his SP; between spouses, community property acquired with community funds remains CP absent a valid transmutation (*In re Marriage of Valli*). A transmutation requires a writing with an express declaration by the adversely affected spouse (Fam. Code § 852(a)). Wen signed nothing. **The house is CP.**

*The will's attempt to give the whole home.* Arturo's will leaves Ines "any home I own." But Arturo owned only half; Wen's half is hers at the moment of his death (Prob. Code § 100), and a will can pass only the testator's half of each community asset (§ 6101). Because the will gives Wen nothing, she simply keeps her half; no question of electing between the will and her community rights arises.

*Result before the omitted-spouse claim:* Wen owns $450,000 (her half); Arturo's $450,000 half passes under the will, subject to Wen's omitted-spouse claim.

#### (b) The brokerage account

The account was funded from 2013 to 2015 with Arturo's salary during the marriage, while the couple was domiciled in State X. Under State X's title system it was Arturo's alone, and Ines will argue that its character was fixed under State X law. But had Arturo been domiciled in California, his salary during marriage would have been CP, and a brokerage account is personal property, which is QCP at death wherever it is located (§ 66). **The account is Arturo's QCP.** One-half ($200,000) belongs to Wen under § 101; Arturo's half ($200,000) is subject to his will and to Wen's omitted-spouse claim.

#### (c) The inheritance account

Property acquired by inheritance is SP, as are its rents, issues and profits (Fam. Code § 770). Arturo kept the $200,000 in a separate account with no other deposits, so it is fully traceable. **The $210,000 is Arturo's SP**, all of which his will can dispose of, subject to Wen's omitted-spouse claim.

#### (d) The State X cabin

Arturo bought the cabin with his State X salary during the marriage. At **dissolution**, California treats such property as QCP "wherever situated," but at **death** QCP excludes real property outside California (§ 66). Succession to land is governed by the law of the state where it lies. Ines takes the cabin under the will to the extent State X law allows. State X's title system treats it as Arturo's, and any spousal protection Wen has depends on State X law, which the facts do not give. California's QCP rules and its omitted-spouse statute do not reach the cabin.

#### (e) The life insurance

A beneficiary designation in an insurance policy is a nonprobate transfer that is valid without will formalities and passes under the policy, not the will (§ 5000). **Wen receives the $100,000.** The premiums were paid with community funds, but Wen, the non-insured spouse, is the beneficiary, so no community claim arises against her.

### 3. Wen as an omitted spouse

**Requirements.** A surviving spouse who married the decedent after the execution of all of his testamentary instruments, and who is not provided for in them, receives an omitted spouse's share (§ 21610). Arturo's only will was signed in 2011; he married Wen in 2013 and never made another will. The will does not provide for her.

**Exceptions (§ 21611).**
1. *Intent on the face of the will.* Ines will point to the clause "I have intentionally omitted all other persons." But the failure to provide for the spouse must be intentional and that intention must appear in the instrument. A general disinheritance clause that does not mention a spouse or a future marriage is not enough (*Estate of Shannon*). Arturo wrote the clause two years before he married Wen, and it says nothing about a spouse. **This exception fails.**
2. *Transfer outside the will in lieu of a testamentary provision.* Ines will argue that the $100,000 life insurance policy was Arturo's provision for Wen. But Arturo described its purpose as paying for his funeral, not as a substitute for an inheritance, and $100,000 is modest compared with an estate of more than $1 million. **This exception fails.**
3. *Waiver.* Wen signed a premarital agreement, but it waived claims to separate property only "in the event of divorce." It says nothing about rights at death. A waiver is construed by its terms, and this one does not reach Wen's rights as a surviving spouse. **No valid waiver.**

**Wen is an omitted spouse.**

**The omitted spouse's share.** An omitted spouse receives (1) the decedent's one-half of the CP, (2) the decedent's one-half of the QCP, and (3) a share of the decedent's SP equal to the intestate share, but not more than one-half (§ 21610). A spouse's intestate share of SP is one-half when the decedent leaves one child (§ 6401(c)).

| Asset | Wen's own share | Wen's omitted-spouse share | Ines (under the will) |
|---|---|---|---|
| House ($900,000, CP) | $450,000 | Arturo's half: $450,000 | — |
| Brokerage ($400,000, QCP) | $200,000 (§ 101) | Arturo's half: $200,000 | — |
| Inheritance ($210,000, SP) | — | One-half: $105,000 | $105,000 |
| State X cabin ($250,000) | Under State X law | Not reached by § 21610 | Cabin, per State X law |
| Life insurance ($100,000) | $100,000 (nonprobate) | — | — |

The omitted-spouse share is satisfied first from property not disposed of by the will (none here) and then from the will's beneficiary (§ 21612). Here that means Ines's gift under the will abates to fund it.

**Conclusion.**
- **Wen** takes the whole Sacramento house, the whole brokerage account, half the inheritance account ($105,000) and the $100,000 insurance proceeds.
- **Ines** takes the other half of the inheritance account ($105,000) and the State X cabin, to the extent State X law gives effect to the will.

**Common mistakes**
1. Invalidating the will under § 6110 and stopping there. § 6113 validates a will executed in compliance with the law of the place of execution.
2. Treating title "Arturo Reyes, a married man" as making the house his SP.
3. Calling the State X cabin QCP at death, using the dissolution definition.
4. Treating the general disinheritance clause, or a premarital waiver limited to divorce, as defeating the omitted-spouse claim.
5. Computing Wen's share from the whole estate instead of from Arturo's half of the CP and QCP plus a capped share of his SP, or forgetting that Wen already owns her own halves.
